3.1 Thermal mass Heavy walls that hold the day's heat and give it back hours later 11 min
11 minutes, read aloud

Put your hand flat on a thick stone wall that faces west, early on a summer morning. It's cool, cooler than the air. Come back at four in the afternoon, after hours of sun, and the outside face is hot to the touch. But go inside, to the room behind that same wall, and the inside face is only now beginning to warm. It will be at its warmest late in the evening, long after the air outside has started to cool.

That delay is the subject of this lesson. Heavy materials, stone, brick, concrete and earth, take up heat slowly, hold a great deal of it, and give it back slowly. Used well, they carry the heat of the afternoon into the night, when you can wash it away, and they hold a room near the day's average temperature instead of its extremes. Used badly, they keep a room hot all night. You'll learn how the delay works, where heavy construction helps and where it hurts, and how to run a heavy building through a hot day.

How it works

Start with storage. Heavy materials hold a lot of heat for each degree they warm. A cubic metre of concrete holds as much heat, degree for degree, as about seventeen hundred cubic metres of air, the air in several whole houses. Rammed earth and brick are not far behind, and water holds twice as much again. So a room with heavy floors and walls can take in a great deal of heat, from the sun, from people, from warm air, and warm up only a little. You met this in the lesson on winter sun, where a heavy floor stored the sun's heat for the evening.

Now the delay. Heat takes hours to cross a thick, heavy wall, because every layer has to warm up before it passes heat on to the next. So the day's warmth reaches the inside of a thick wall late, and weakened. Here's what that looks like for a day that swings between sixteen and thirty-six degrees, computed for this lesson with typical values. Through two hundred millimetres of rammed earth, the peak arrives inside six to eight hours late, depending on the earth, and the inside face swings only about two degrees either way, while the air outside swings ten. Through three hundred millimetres of concrete, the peak arrives eight hours late or more, and the inside face swings about one degree. Through four hundred millimetres of earth, it's about twelve hours late, and the inside swings less than half a degree. A thin sheet, like a fibre-cement roof, passes the whole swing straight through, with no delay at all: its inside face swings seven degrees either way.

Insulation does a different job. A well-insulated timber wall lets very little heat through, but with little delay. Insulation resists heat; mass stores it and delays it. In most climates good buildings use both, with the insulation outside and the mass inside, where the room can use it. And it's the surface facing the room that does most of the daily work. The daily wave of heat only reaches about the first ten centimetres of a concrete slab in any strength, so a bare, exposed surface matters more than extra thickness.

Put storage and delay together and a heavy building behaves like a flywheel. Its temperature inside settles near the average of the day, with the peaks shaved off. That's why mass is most useful where the days are hot and the nights are cool: the average can be comfortable even when the afternoon isn't. Australia's government guidance puts it in numbers. Where the day and the night differ by about six degrees or less, build light. Between six and ten, use a moderate amount of mass. Above ten, heavy construction pays.

And if the day's average itself is too hot? Then mass alone can't help, and you have to lower the average, by night flushing. Open the building at night, when the air outside is cooler than the mass, and let it wash over the heavy surfaces and carry the stored heat away. In the morning, close up and shade the windows, so the cooled mass soaks up the heat of the day. In a full-scale experiment in France, night ventilation lowered the temperatures inside during the day by about one and a half to two degrees. The gains flattened out at about eight to ten air changes an hour.

The best examples

The most striking measurements come from Burkina Faso, in West Africa, where researchers from the University of Seville logged four kinds of house, a week at a time, through 2021 and 2022. On days above thirty-five degrees, a round house of the Mossi people, with rammed-earth walls about twenty centimetres thick under a thatched roof, stayed one or two degrees below the outdoor peak, about an hour late. The best of the four was a compact house of concrete block, sharing its walls with its neighbours around a courtyard, under a roof of thin fibre-cement sheet, with a ceiling hung below it and an air gap between the two. It stayed more than five degrees below the outdoor peak, and its own peak came more than five hours late. The worst was a house with rammed-earth walls under the same kind of fibre-cement sheet, just six millimetres thick, with no ceiling beneath it. It ran one to three degrees hotter than the air outside, with almost no delay, and the researchers blamed the roof. Each house was measured in a different week, some in different seasons, so the comparison is rough.

That's the lesson in one study. Earth walls couldn't save a house with a bare, thin roof, while a compact house of concrete block did well under the same kind of roof, because a ventilated ceiling kept the roof's heat away from the room. The roof, as you learned in the lesson on too much sun, is the surface that faces the sky.

The clearest test of night flushing was run by Baruch Givoni in the summer of 1993, at Pala in southern California. He compared light and heavy test buildings, closed and ventilated at night. Night ventilation did very little for the light building, but it was very effective for the heavy one, and kept it far below the outdoor peaks, especially in heat waves. On an extremely hot day, with thirty-eight degrees outside, the heavy building peaked at only twenty-four and a half inside.

Rules of thumb

One. Match the mass to the day–night swing: light construction where it's about six degrees or less, a moderate amount of mass between six and ten, and heavy construction above ten.

Two. Put the mass inside the insulation, and leave it bare. Tile, stone, concrete, or plaster laid straight onto masonry, not carpet, cork, timber linings or suspended ceilings, which insulate the mass from the room.

Three. Shade it by day and flush it by night. Keep the summer sun off it, open up when the air outside is cooler than the mass, at up to about ten air changes an hour, and close up and shade in the morning.

Four. Use the delay. A heavy wall facing the afternoon sun can carry that heat into the night: eight hours or more through three hundred millimetres of concrete, and about twelve through four hundred of earth.

Five. Fix the roof first. Heavy walls can't make up for a thin roof that faces the sky; insulate it, or hang a ventilated ceiling beneath it.

Where it fails

Mass works against you where the nights stay warm. In the humid tropics, where the day and night differ by only a few degrees, heavy walls store the day's heat and give it back through the night, just when you want to cool down, which is why the Australian guidance advises against high mass there. Mass is also a poor fit for rooms heated or cooled only now and then, because it slows their response. Night flushing fails in runs of warm nights, which a study of climate data from across Europe found can happen even where its potential is high. And night flushing needs openings you can leave open safely: in England's rules for new homes, bedroom windows on the ground floor, or easy to reach, only count as open at night if they have fixed or lockable shutters, grilles or railings, and in noisy places windows are treated as shut.

Be wary of legends, too. The cliff dwellings of Mesa Verde are often described as passive solar buildings that catch the winter sun. As far as the research edition could find, no one has measured them. The National Park Service ties the sites mainly to water: Balcony House faces north-east and is fairly chilly in winter, but it sits beside two springs.

Recap

To sum up. Heavy materials store a great deal of heat and pass it through slowly, so a thick wall delivers the afternoon's heat hours later and much weakened. A heavy building settles near the day's average, which is comfortable where days are hot and nights are cool, and night flushing lowers that average further. Put the mass inside the insulation, leave it bare, shade it by day, flush it by night, and fix the roof first. Avoid heavy construction where nights stay warm, and in rooms used only now and then.

Check yourself

First question. Why is the inside of a thick west wall at its warmest late in the evening rather than in the afternoon?

Because heat takes hours to cross a thick, heavy wall. Through three hundred millimetres of concrete, the afternoon's peak arrives eight hours or more later.

Second question. Days reach thirty-six degrees and nights fall to eighteen. Is heavy construction a good idea, and what else does it need?

Yes. The swing is large, so mass will hold the rooms near the day's average. It needs shade by day, and ventilation at night to carry the stored heat away.

Third question. Why did a house with earth walls in Burkina Faso run hotter than the air outside?

Because its roof was a bare, thin fibre-cement sheet, which passed the sun's heat straight in with no delay. The best house had the same kind of roof, with a ventilated ceiling under it.

3.2 Into the ground A few metres down, the earth holds close to the year's average 9 min
9 minutes, read aloud

It's January in Guojiawan, a village in the hills of northern Shaanxi, on China's Loess Plateau. Through the whole month the air outside averages nearly thirteen degrees below zero. Some families here live in caves cut into the yellow loess of the hillside, with a brick front wall and more than two metres of earth over the vaulted ceiling. Others live in brick houses built on the level ground.

In January 2023, researchers logged the bedrooms of three homes in this village through the whole month, with no heating in them. In the cave, the bedroom averaged about two degrees below freezing. That's cold, but it's nearly eleven degrees warmer than the air outside, with no fire at all. In a free-standing house of heavy stone vaults, it averaged a little over three below. In the modern brick house, it averaged more than nine below. This lesson is about why the earth and heavy walls did that, how deep you need to go, and what building into the ground costs you.

How it works

The ground is an enormous, slow store of heat. Through the year the sun and the air warm and cool its surface, and that yearly wave of warmth sinks into the ground very slowly, getting smaller and later as it goes. Here's how it goes in ordinary soil, computed for this lesson from the standard model. A metre down, about two-thirds of the surface's yearly swing is left, and the warmest day comes about three weeks after it does at the surface. Two metres down, less than half is left, about six and a half weeks late. At three metres, less than a third, about ten weeks late. Below about six metres, less than a tenth of the swing remains.

And what's left is centred on one number: the average air temperature of the whole year. Deep ground, deep groundwater and the year's average air temperature all tend strongly toward the same value. So a room surrounded by earth sits against walls that stay close to the year's average: much warmer than the air in winter, and much cooler in summer.

Notice the delay, too. Three metres down, the ground is warmest about ten weeks after the surface is, in the autumn, and coldest in the spring. The deeper you go, the later it runs: about a season behind at four metres.

That tells you what the earth can and can't do. It can't make a cold place warm. Where the year's average is eight or nine degrees, the ground a few metres down sits at eight or nine, and a room against it still needs heating in winter. But it gives you a head start of many degrees in winter and a store of coolness in summer, with no machinery at all. Where the year's average is mild, the earth can hold a room close to comfortable almost all year.

Building into the ground has three costs. The first is weight. Earth is heavy, so roofs that carry it need vaults, arches or heavy structure. The second is water. The ground is damp, and rain drains toward anything dug into it, so a buried room needs drainage and a skin that keeps water out. The third is air and light. A room surrounded by earth needs at least one face open to the sky, for daylight and for fresh air, and in summer its cool walls can gather condensation from warm, humid air.

The best examples

The cave houses of the Loess Plateau, called yaodong, come in three kinds. The most common are cut straight into a cliff or hillside. Others are dug into the four sides of a rectangular pit, six or seven metres deep, which becomes a sunken courtyard. In one survey of eleven such houses in Henan, one courtyard measured about eighteen by twelve metres, and the living caves ran five to twelve metres back into the earth. The third kind is a free-standing vault of stone or brick. A 1981 assessment counted about forty million people living in yaodong. The measurements from Guojiawan show what the earth gave them: in the depth of winter, the unheated cave was about eleven degrees warmer than the air outside, three times the gain of the brick house in the same village. The free-standing stone vault, with walls eighty centimetres thick, did almost as well, so thick, heavy walls did much of the work.

At Matmata, in southern Tunisia, Amazigh, or Berber, communities built the same way in a hot climate. A pit dug straight down into the ground forms the courtyard, and rooms for sleeping, cooking, storage and animals are cut into the rock around it. The courtyard gives every room its daylight and air, and the ground around them keeps them cool through the heat of the day.

The pithouse from the last chapter, at Mesa Verde, was sunk more than a metre into the ground. And a modern building was measured with the same care as Guojiawan's caves. At Oak Ridge, in Tennessee, government researchers monitored an earth-sheltered office and dormitory of three hundred and seventy-two square metres, hour by hour, through two summers. Through the cooling season it used about thirty per cent less energy than well-built buildings above ground, and they thought fifty per cent was possible. Its peak cooling load was half, and at least nine in ten of the people using it were comfortable all of the time.

In Iceland, where timber was scarce, farmhouses were built for centuries with thick walls and roofs of turf on stone footings, over a timber frame. The thick turf gave the walls stability and kept the heat in. The farm at Glaumbær, in the north, whose buildings date from the middle of the eighteenth century to about 1880, is one of the turf farms on Iceland's tentative list for World Heritage.

Rules of thumb

One. Go down two or three metres to cut the yearly swing to a half or a third. Below about six metres, the ground holds nearly steady at the year's average.

Two. Know your climate's yearly average. Where it's cold, the earth gives you a head start, not comfort. Where it's mild, it can do most of the work.

Three. Remember that the ground runs late, by about three weeks for every metre of depth. It's warmest in autumn and coldest in spring.

Four. Give every buried room a face to the sky: a sunken courtyard, a cliff face or an open front, for daylight and fresh air.

Five. Keep the water out. Drain the ground around the building, give it a waterproof skin, and ventilate it, so cool walls don't gather condensation in summer.

Where it fails

The earth can only bring you to the year's average, and in a cold climate that isn't warm. The unheated cave at Guojiawan still averaged below freezing in January. Buried rooms can be dark, stuffy and damp if they aren't given a courtyard or an open face, and water is a constant threat to anything dug into the ground. Earth over a roof is heavy, so structure and excavation cost more. And in humid summers, the cool walls of an underground room can run with condensation unless it's ventilated and dried.

Recap

To sum up. The yearly swing of temperature sinks into the ground slowly, shrinking and running late: less than half of it is left at two metres, and less than a tenth below about six. Deep down, the ground sits close to the year's average air temperature, so rooms surrounded by earth are warmer than the air in winter and cooler in summer. The earth gives a head start, not comfort, in cold climates. Buried rooms need a face to the sky, drainage, a waterproof skin and ventilation.

Check yourself

First question. As you go deeper into the ground, what temperature does it settle toward?

Toward the average air temperature of the whole year. Below about six metres, it holds nearly steady there.

Second question. Three metres down, when in the year is the ground warmest?

About ten weeks after the surface is warmest, in the autumn rather than at midsummer.

Third question. Why were the unheated cave bedrooms at Guojiawan so much warmer in January than the brick house?

Because thick, heavy earth wrapped them, holding them near the year's average and slowing the escape of heat, while the thin walls of the brick house followed the outdoor air. The thick stone vault did almost as well, so heavy walls did much of the work.

3.3 Warm surfaces Heat the mass, not the air, and you feel warm at a lower air temperature 9 min
9 minutes, read aloud

Take off your shoes and step into a room in a traditional Korean house on a winter night. The floor is warm under your feet, warmest at the end nearest the kitchen fire. The family sits on it, eats on it, and later sleeps on it. The air in the room is cool, cooler than you'd set a thermostat, and yet nobody is cold.

Now picture the opposite: a modern room on a winter evening, the thermostat reading twenty-one degrees, and one wall of tall, cold glass. You sit near it and feel a chill on that side of your body, and you turn the heating up. Both rooms are telling you the same thing. Warmth isn't only a matter of the air. It's also a matter of the surfaces around you. This lesson is about how that works, how builders from Korea to Rome to Russia heated surfaces instead of air, and what a warm surface can and can't do.

How it works

Your body loses heat in several ways: to the air around you, by evaporation from your skin and breath, by touch, and by radiation. Every surface you can see is trading radiant heat with you. A warm surface sends more to you than you send to it; a cold one takes more from you than it gives. So what matters is not just the temperature of the air but the temperature of the surfaces, averaged over how much of your view each one fills. Engineers call that the mean radiant temperature.

In the still air of an ordinary room, you trade about as much heat with the surfaces as with the air. So the temperature you actually feel, which engineers call the operative temperature, is roughly the average of the two. That simple average is the whole lesson. If the walls and floor around you average twenty-four degrees, air at eighteen feels like twenty-one. If they average seventeen, because of cold glass or cold, uninsulated walls, the air has to be at twenty-five to feel the same.

That's why heating the surfaces works. Warm the floor, the walls or a heavy stove, and people are comfortable with cooler air. A study of floor heating found it reached the same felt temperature with air a degree to a degree and a half cooler than other systems. The same author adds that in well-insulated houses the difference in heat lost is small, because the surfaces there are already close to the air's temperature.

There are limits. A floor that's too warm is uncomfortable to stand on. The American standard for thermal comfort keeps floors between nineteen and twenty-nine degrees for people in ordinary indoor shoes. And a cold surface on one side of you can make you uncomfortable even when the room's average is fine. The same standard limits how much colder a wall or window may feel to one side of you than the other, to about ten degrees. Tall, cold glass also sends a sheet of chilled air sliding down to the floor, a draught you feel at your ankles.

The last idea is storage, which you met in the first lesson of this chapter. A heavy stove or a heavy floor can be heated hard for a short time, and then give its heat back gently for many hours, at a surface temperature you can touch.

The best examples

The Korean ondol heats a room from below. A fire mouth outside the room, in small houses the kitchen stove, sends its smoke and hot gases through flues under the floor, beneath stone slabs bedded in clay, to a chimney on the far side. The slabs store the heat and give it off to the room above. Korea's heritage agency dates the earliest remains to the last centuries before the common era, and puts the practice at more than two thousand years old. The earliest known versions heated only a strip of floor along one wall; the whole heated floor came later. In 2018 ondol culture was named one of Korea's national intangible heritages. And it never went away: from the 1960s it was adapted to hot-water pipes, and Korean homes, high-rise apartments included, are still commonly heated through the floor today.

The Romans heated their baths the same way, with the hypocaust. Vitruvius describes it: a floor raised on piers of small bricks, about two feet high, carrying large tiles, over a paving that slopes gently toward the furnace, so that, as he puts it, a ball thrown in would roll back out. Hot gases from the furnace ran under the floor and up through hollow box tiles in the walls, so the walls were warm too. You can still see it at the Stabian Baths in Pompeii, built from the second century before the common era.

And across the cold north, people built the heat into a single piece of heavy masonry. The Russian stove, the pech, can weigh around two tonnes, often with a sleeping berth on top. The heaviest kind of German and Austrian tile stove, the Grundofen, is refuelled only every eight, twelve or twenty-four hours. A masonry heater today burns a hot, fast fire, for two hours or less, once or twice a day. Its mass, at least eight hundred kilograms of it, then gives off radiant warmth for many hours, with the surface typically between about forty and fifty-five degrees.

Rules of thumb

One. Warm the surfaces, not just the air. In still air you feel roughly the average of the air temperature and the surface temperature around you.

Two. Keep heated floors between nineteen and twenty-nine degrees, and don't cover them with thick rugs or carpet.

Three. Tame cold glass. Use better glazing, keep people out of the chill beside big windows, or put heat at the window to stop the cold draught falling to the floor.

Four. Store the heat near people. A heavy stove, fired briefly once or twice a day, can warm a room for many hours.

Five. Insulate first. In a well-insulated building the walls stay warm by themselves, and every heating system works better.

Where it fails

Heavy, warm surfaces are slow. A heated floor takes hours to warm up, and it can't be turned down quickly when the sun comes out, so a room can overheat on a sunny winter afternoon. A floor can't comfortably run much warmer than about twenty-nine degrees under people's feet, so in a poorly insulated building it may not be able to put out enough heat on the coldest days. In well-insulated houses, radiant heating saves little energy, because the surfaces are warm anyway. And fire under the floor has its own danger. When coal briquettes replaced firewood in Korea from the 1960s, the fumes from briquettes burning under the floor poisoned many people with carbon monoxide, one reason the country moved to hot water.

Recap

To sum up. You trade heat with the surfaces around you as much as with the air, so in still air you feel roughly the average of the two. Warm surfaces let people be comfortable in cooler air, and cold ones, like big cold windows, make a warm room feel chilly. The ondol, the hypocaust and the masonry stove all heat heavy surfaces and let them give the heat back slowly. Keep floors between nineteen and twenty-nine degrees, tame cold glass, store heat near people, and insulate first.

Check yourself

First question. In the still air of a room, which two temperatures does comfort mostly depend on?

The air temperature and the mean radiant temperature of the surfaces around you. You feel roughly their average.

Second question. The walls and floor of a room average twenty-four degrees. About what air temperature will feel like twenty-one?

About eighteen degrees, because eighteen and twenty-four average twenty-one.

Third question. How can one short fire a day keep a room warm with a masonry heater?

The fire heats a heavy mass quickly, and the mass gives the heat back slowly, at a gentle surface temperature, for many hours.

3.4 Holding heat in the cold Compact form, thick insulation, a raised sleeping platform and a low door 10 min
10 minutes, read aloud

It's February 1993 at Resolute, in the Canadian High Arctic, and the air outside is between twenty-eight and forty-one degrees below zero. On the snow stand two domes of cut snow blocks, built by researchers with advice from four Inuit advisers on their design and furnishings, and wired with thermometers inside and out. The larger is about four metres across and just over two metres high, made of seventy-two blocks, with a sleeping platform half a metre above the floor. The smaller one is hung inside with caribou skins.

With three to five people inside and one or two seal-oil lamps burning, the air in the snow houses rose as much as forty-five degrees above the air outside, most of all in the small, skin-lined one, and whenever they were heated they stayed at least twenty degrees warmer. The larger one, standing empty, was only about five degrees warmer, and the researchers put most of that down to heat from the ground. Hold on to that difference. The snow did its part, but the warmth came from the lamps and the people, and the house's job was to keep it. This lesson is about how buildings in cold places keep their heat: by their shape, by insulation, by keeping the air in, and by where people sit and sleep.

How it works

Heat leaves a building in two ways: through its surfaces, and with the air that leaks or is let out. Through the surfaces, the loss grows with three things multiplied together: how much surface there is, how easily heat passes through each square metre, and how much warmer it is inside than out. So there are four things you can do. Have less surface. Make each square metre resist heat. Keep the warm air in, and bring fresh air in on purpose. And make the most of the heat you get for free, from people, lamps, cooking and the sun.

Start with shape. For the same volume, some shapes have much less surface than others. Take a thousand cubic metres, the size of a large house. As a hemisphere, like a snow house, it has a surface of about five hundred and seventy-five square metres, floor included. As a cube, six hundred. Stretched into a bar forty metres long and five wide and high, eight hundred and fifty: more than forty per cent more surface to lose heat through, for the same room inside. Size matters too. Halve every dimension of a building and its surface for every cubic metre inside doubles, which is why small buildings are always harder to keep warm, whatever their shape. Buildings that share walls, in terraces and blocks of flats, have less of their surface exposed to the cold.

Next, insulation. The walls of the first Passive House, finished in Darmstadt, Germany, in 1991, let through about a seventh of a watt per square metre for each degree of difference. Old stone walls measured in Scotland let through six to eleven times as much.

Then the air. Every gap lets warm air out and cold air in, so cold-climate buildings are sealed carefully and then ventilated on purpose, through a heat exchanger that uses the outgoing stale air to warm the incoming fresh air. Builders test this by pumping air in or out until the pressure inside differs from outside by fifty pascals, and measuring how fast the air flows. The Passive House standard allows no more than six-tenths of the building's air to be replaced each hour at that pressure.

Last, warm air rises, so where you put people matters. The snow house's sleeping platform lifts the sleepers into the warmest air, and, as you heard in the lesson on keeping the wind out, its entrance tunnel is sometimes dug below the floor, so the coldest air collects there instead of in the room.

The best examples

The snow house is the purest example: a dome, the shape with the least surface above the ground for the room inside; walls of snow, which hold a great deal of still air; a skin lining, which cut the heat lost through the walls; a door on the side away from the wind; and lamps and people inside. The researchers named their four Inuit advisers in the paper: Nangat Idlout, Herodier Kalluk, Tony Manik and Aleesuk Eckalook.

The Sámi, in the far north of Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia, built for the cold too. Their turf hut, the goahti, is built around a central hearth under a smoke hole, with a roof of birch bark, turf and moss, and a floor of birch twigs, covered with reindeer hides, to keep the damp away.

The modern story begins in 1977 at Regina, on the Canadian prairie, with the Saskatchewan Conservation House, built by the Saskatchewan Research Council with the National Research Council of Canada and the province's universities. Its walls were insulated to about R-40 in North American units, much like the Passive House walls you'll hear about next, its roof to about R-60, and it was sealed to about eight-tenths of an air change an hour at the test pressure, against three to five for ordinary houses of the day, with a heat exchanger to bring in fresh air. Its designed heat loss was about a third of an average Regina house's: with twenty degrees below zero outside and twenty above inside, about three kilowatts instead of ten.

Fourteen years later, the first Passive House, a row of four homes at Darmstadt-Kranichstein designed by the architects Bott, Ridder and Westermeyer, turned that idea into a standard. Its measured heating in the first year was about twenty kilowatt-hours per square metre, about twelve in the second, and under ten after that, and over twenty-five years it averaged about eight. When two of the houses were tested again in 2016, both were tighter than the standard demands.

Rules of thumb

One. Keep it compact and simple. Fewer wings and corners, and shared walls where you can, mean less surface losing heat.

Two. Insulate thickly and without gaps. In a cold climate, walls of about a seventh of a watt per square metre per degree, like Kranichstein's, are a good target.

Three. Seal it, then ventilate it on purpose, through a heat exchanger. The Passive House limit is six-tenths of an air change an hour at fifty pascals.

Four. Sleep high, enter low, and close the door. Put sleeping and sitting places in the warm upper air, and let entrances trap the cold below or in a lobby.

Five. Count the free heat. In a tight, well-insulated building, people, cooking, lamps and the sun supply much of the heating, and the Passive House standard asks for no more than fifteen kilowatt-hours of heating per square metre a year.

Where it fails

A building that holds heat in winter can hold too much in summer. In the north of England, a certified Passive House bungalow for two elderly residents was measured for almost two years. In 2014 its lounge was above twenty-five degrees for about half of all the hours in the year, against a design prediction of five per cent. The researchers blamed heat from appliances, how the windows were used, too little mass, the summer settings of the ventilation, and the sun on the south side. Sealing a building without ventilating it on purpose leaves the air stale and damp. And remember the empty snow house, only five degrees warmer than outside. A building's shell holds heat; it doesn't make it. How warm it is depends on the people, the lamps, the stove and the closed door.

Recap

To sum up. Heat escapes through a building's surfaces and with its air. Keep the form compact and share walls, since less surface loses less heat, and small buildings lose the most for their size. Insulate thickly, seal the building and ventilate it on purpose through a heat exchanger, put people high and entrances low, and count the free heat of people, lamps and sun. The same buildings that hold heat in winter can overheat in summer, so plan for both.

Check yourself

First question. Why is a compact building easier to keep warm?

Because it has less surface for the same volume, and heat escapes through the surface.

Second question. How much warmer than outside were the snow houses with people and lamps inside, and how much was the larger one when it stood empty?

Up to forty-five degrees warmer with people and lamps, and only about five degrees when empty. The shell keeps heat; the people and lamps make it.

Third question. What are the Passive House limits for heating and airtightness?

At most fifteen kilowatt-hours of heating per square metre a year, and at most six-tenths of an air change an hour at fifty pascals.

3.5 Earth as a material Rammed earth, adobe and cob need a good hat and boots, and care 9 min
9 minutes, read aloud

It's spring in Djenné, in Mali, and the whole town has turned out for the Great Mosque. Sticks of palm wood stand out of its walls in rows, like the pins of a pincushion. They're scaffolding, built into the walls for good. People climb them and spread fresh mud plaster over the building by hand, until the whole mosque has a new skin. They do it every year, at a festival, and without it the walls would slowly melt in the rains.

That festival is the best introduction to building with earth. Earth is cheap, local, heavy and beautiful, and it can last for centuries. But it has one great enemy, which is water, and it lasts only as long as someone keeps it dry and keeps it mended. This lesson covers the three main ways of building with earth, the rules for keeping it dry, the codes that say how thick and strong it must be, and what happens when those rules are broken.

How it works

Building earth is soil with enough clay in it to bind the sand and gravel together. It's strong when pressed, weak when pulled, and much weaker when wet.

There are three main ways of building with it. Rammed earth is damp soil packed hard, layer by layer, inside formwork, which is then taken away. Adobe is mud brick, shaped in moulds and dried in the sun, then laid with mud mortar. Cob is wet earth mixed with straw and built up by hand, in lifts, with no formwork at all.

All three need what the cob builders of Devon, in England, call a hat and a good pair of boots. The hat is the roof, with eaves wide enough to throw the rain clear of the walls. The boots are a base of stone, fired brick or concrete that lifts the earth clear of splashing rain, and, with a damp-proof course in it, keeps the ground's damp out. The Devon Earth Building Association puts the top of that base three hundred to four hundred and fifty millimetres above the ground. New Mexico's code for earth buildings asks for at least six inches, about fifteen centimetres.

The coat matters as much as the hat and boots. An earth wall has to be able to dry out, so its plaster must let moisture pass and be softer than the wall itself. That means lime or earth plasters. Hard cement renders and impervious paints trap moisture behind them, and the wall behind a cement render can decay unseen.

Codes set how thick and strong the walls must be. New Mexico asks for adobe bricks with a strength of about two megapascals, walls fourteen inches thick on the ground floor of a two-storey adobe house and ten above, and exterior walls of rammed earth at least eighteen inches, about forty-five centimetres, thick. New Zealand, which has frequent earthquakes, asks for walls at least two hundred and fifty millimetres thick, and in its standard for houses built without an engineer's design, reinforcement running both along and up the wall.

Earth is heavy, so it's good thermal mass, as you learned in the first lesson of this chapter: Australia's guidance says rammed earth can delay the day's heat by as much as ten to twelve hours. But it's no substitute for insulation. In Scotland, old earth walls about six hundred millimetres thick, lined with timber, were measured losing heat at around half the rate of old stone walls, but still three to six times the rate of a well-insulated modern wall like Kranichstein's.

The best examples

The Great Mosque of Djenné, rebuilt in 1906 and 1907, is perhaps the most famous earth building in the world, and the town around it is a World Heritage site. Its toron, the palm-wood scaffolding in its walls, makes the yearly replastering part of the building itself. The mosque shows that with earth, maintenance is not a failure of the design. It is the design.

At Tiébélé, in southern Burkina Faso, the royal court of the Kassena people was inscribed as a World Heritage site in 2024. The men build the houses, of earth, and the women, and only the women, decorate the walls. Many West African builders mix cattle dung into their earth plasters, and the laboratory agrees with them. In tests on a clay from Burkina Faso, a little dung, up to six per cent, made the plaster harder-wearing and more resistant to water. In another study, on earths from Brazil and Portugal, a mortar with dung survived half an hour under water, while the plain earth mortar fell apart in two to five minutes.

In Egypt, between 1946 and 1952, the architect Hassan Fathy built the village of New Gourna, near Luxor, of mud brick, reviving the Nubian way of raising vaults and domes without any formwork, brick by leaning brick. Fathy's book about it made it famous, and it is also one of architecture's most instructive failures. The villagers it was built for refused to move there, and the village stood largely empty for years. Its worst enemy has been groundwater rising into the walls, and deep drains, about six metres down, were later dug to intercept it. A campaign by UNESCO and Egypt's government has since restored its key buildings, finishing its first phase in 2021.

Rules of thumb

One. Give it a hat: a roof with eaves wide enough to keep rain off the walls.

Two. Give it boots: a base of stone, fired brick or concrete, about three hundred to four hundred and fifty millimetres high, with a damp-proof course, to keep splashing rain and rising damp away from the earth.

Three. Let it breathe. Plaster earth with lime or earth, never cement or impervious paint.

Four. Build it thick enough, and in earthquake country, reinforce it. Follow the local code: New Mexico's eighteen inches of rammed earth, for example, or New Zealand's two hundred and fifty millimetres, reinforced.

Five. Plan the upkeep. Decide who will inspect and repair the walls, and how often, and keep the knowledge of how to do it alive.

Where it fails

Earth fails through water and through shaking. Rain, splash, flooding and rising groundwater all soften it, as New Gourna shows. At Timbuktu, a conservation survey of the great earth mosque, the Djingareyber, traced its decay to water getting in through badly sloped roofs, and to the thinning knowledge of how to plaster. And heavy, unreinforced earth is deadly in earthquakes. At Bam, in Iran, in 2003, adobe walls fell outward and dropped their heavy vaults onto the rooms below. About four-fifths of the buildings in the city's ancient citadel collapsed, and tens of thousands of people in the city died. Cement repairs to old earth walls cause harm of their own, by trapping moisture inside. And earth alone isn't enough to keep a house warm in a cold climate; it needs insulation too.

Recap

To sum up. Rammed earth, adobe and cob are all soil bound by clay: strong when pressed, weak when pulled, and weaker when wet. Give earth walls a hat of wide eaves, boots of stone or brick, and a coat of lime or earth plaster that lets them dry. Build them as thick as the codes ask, and reinforce them in earthquake country. Earth stores heat well but insulates poorly. And plan the maintenance from the start, because with earth, care is part of the design.

Check yourself

First question. What are the hat and boots of an earth wall?

The hat is a roof with eaves wide enough to throw the rain clear. The boots are a raised base of stone, brick or concrete that keeps splash and rising damp away from the earth.

Second question. Why should an earth wall be plastered with lime rather than cement?

Because cement is harder and lets less moisture through, so it traps water in the wall, which weakens the earth. Lime lets the wall dry out.

Third question. What did the earthquake at Bam show about earth buildings?

That heavy, unreinforced earth walls fall outward and drop their roofs and vaults. In earthquake country, earth needs reinforcement and lighter roofs.
