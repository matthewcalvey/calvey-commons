Nature's forms

Letting gravity find the form, growth and pattern, copying nature's principles, materials that grow, and the building as landscape.

Near Barcelona, around 1900, Antoni Gaudí designed a church upside down. From the ceiling of a workshop he hung a web of cords, and on the cords he hung small sacks weighted with lead, each in proportion to the part of the building it stood for. Gravity pulled every cord into the shape that carries its load in pure tension. A mirror on the floor showed him the web the right way up. He photographed the model, turned the photographs over, and drew the church on top of them.

The church, for the workers' colony of the textile maker Eusebi Güell, was never finished. Only its crypt was built, between 1908 and about 1915, with columns that lean as the hanging model said they should. But the method is one of the most powerful ideas in structure, and it's the subject of this lesson: let gravity find the form.

How it works

A hanging chain can only carry tension. It can't bend or push, so under its own weight it settles into the one shape in which every link simply pulls on the next. For a chain of even weight, that curve has a name, the catenary. Now freeze the chain and turn it upside down. Every pull becomes a push, and you have an arch in which every part is in pure compression.

That matters because stone, brick, earth and unreinforced concrete are strong when squeezed and weak when pulled. An arch made of them stands as long as the line along which its weight travels, called the line of thrust, stays inside the material. The shape of the arch decides where that line goes.

In 1675, the English scientist Robert Hooke published the idea hidden in a Latin anagram, solved in print only after his death. In plain English: as a flexible line hangs, so the rigid arch will stand, upside down. Working with Christopher Wren on St Paul's Cathedral, he reasoned that the ideal dome was a ring of such inverted chains.

The numbers show why it matters. Take the Roman semicircular arch. Work out its line of thrust under its own weight, as engineers have for more than a century, and you find that a semicircle must be at least just over a tenth of its radius thick, or the line escapes the stone and the arch folds up on hinges: at the crown, at the springings, and at the haunches, about fifty-four degrees down from the top. An arch shaped like the inverted chain has its line of thrust running along its middle, so it can be far thinner.

The shape depends on the loads. A chain with weights hung from it takes a different shape from a bare chain, and that's why Gaudí weighted his cords: to find the forms that suit a real building's loads, towers and roofs and all. And the idea extends from lines to surfaces. Hang a net or a cloth, let it set, turn it over, and you have a shell that works in compression. Stretch a soap film on a wire frame and it pulls itself into the smallest surface that fits, evenly stressed everywhere: the natural shape for a tent or a cable net, which stays in tension and doesn't need turning over.

The best examples

The Pantheon in Rome, probably dedicated about AD 126, is still the largest dome of unreinforced concrete in the world, 43.3 metres across. You met its oculus in the chapter on light. A hemisphere isn't the shape a hanging chain would choose, so the Roman builders worked on its weight instead. The shell thins from 6.4 metres at the base to 1.2 metres at the oculus. The stone mixed into the concrete gets lighter as the dome rises: heavy travertine low down, then broken tile, then light volcanic tufa and pumice near the top. Five rings of twenty-eight coffers take out more weight. In 1986, Robert Mark and Paul Hutchinson estimated that ordinary heavy concrete all the way up would have raised the stresses by about eighty per cent.

In the twentieth century, a line of engineers and architects built thin shells and nets that get their strength from their shape. In Mexico, Félix Candela built concrete shells shaped like saddles, a shape made entirely of straight lines, so it could be cast on formwork of straight boards; the roof of his Cosmic Ray Pavilion, of about 1951, is between 1.6 and 5 centimetres thick. In Switzerland, Heinz Isler found his shells by hanging cloth and turning the shape over; at a motorway service station he built in the late 1960s, the shells are about thirty metres across, each standing on three points. And in Stuttgart, Frei Otto, who led the university's Institute for Lightweight Structures from 1964, found forms with soap films, nets and hanging models. With the architects Behnisch and Partner he roofed the stadium, the arena and the pool of the Munich Olympic Park, finished in 1972, with a steel cable net carrying acrylic glass. At Mannheim, in the mid-1970s, a flat grid of timber laths was pushed up from the ground into a curved timber gridshell.

Stuttgart has kept the tradition going. Since 2010, the university's research institutes have built an experimental pavilion almost every year, with forms worked out by computer from biological structures and made by robots. The 2011 pavilion copied the plate skeleton of a sand dollar in plywood 6.5 millimetres thick, with more than eight hundred and fifty different parts. The 2013 to 2014 pavilion, based on the wing cases of beetles, was wound from fibre by robots in thirty-six elements that together weighed 593 kilograms.

Rules of thumb

One. For masonry, earth and unreinforced concrete, shape the structure so the line of thrust stays well inside the material. The shape does the work.

Two. To find the shape, hang it: a chain for an arch, a loaded net for a vault, a cloth for a shell. A computer model can do the same.

Three. Load the model the way the building will be loaded. Weights change the shape.

Four. Where the shape is fixed, work on the weight: thick and heavy low down, thin and light higher up, as in the Pantheon.

Five. For tents and nets, find the form with a soap film or stretched fabric, so the surface is evenly stressed.

Where it fails

A form found by hanging is perfect for one set of loads. Wind, drifting snow on one side, an earthquake or a moving load push the line of thrust away from the middle, so real arches and shells need some extra depth or stiffness. Thin shells need curved formwork and a great deal of skilled labour, and a shell a few centimetres thick does little to keep heat in or out. Gaudí's church was never finished, and his model is lost; it survives only in photographs. And the Stuttgart pavilions are temporary experiments: as far as the research edition could find, they were built to test design and fabrication, and no one has measured how buildings like them would perform to live in.

Recap

To sum up. A hanging chain takes the shape of pure tension; turned over, it becomes an arch in pure compression, which is what stone, brick and concrete want. A semicircular arch has to be thick, just over a tenth of its radius, while an inverted chain can be thin. Load the model as the building will be loaded, as Gaudí did, and extend it to shells with cloth and to tents with soap film. Where a shape is fixed, as in the Pantheon, lighten it as it rises.

7.28 min Growth and pattern Ornament from plants, and structure that grows like them Leaves of SouthwellHôtel Tassel, BrusselsA column that grows
8 minutes, read aloud

Walk into the chapter house of Southwell Minster, in Nottinghamshire, and look at the stone around the seats. The capitals and canopies are carved with leaves so true to life that botanists have named the plants: ivy, maple, oak, hop and hawthorn among them. The room was begun in 1288, and it has another surprise: its stone vault spans the whole room with no central column to hold it up. In 1945 the historian Nikolaus Pevsner wrote a short book about the carving alone, The Leaves of Southwell.

Those medieval carvers copied what they saw in the hedgerows. Later designers tried two other things: to draw out the order behind a plant, its symmetry and repetition, and turn it into pattern; and to make structure the way living things make theirs, with material placed where the forces are. This lesson follows all three: copying nature, abstracting it, and growing like it.

How it works

Copying is the simplest. Carve the leaf as it is. At its best, as at Southwell, the result is astonishing, and it teaches the eye to look. But a copied leaf is only a picture of a leaf.

Abstracting came next. In 1856, the architect Owen Jones published The Grammar of Ornament, a huge survey of decoration from around the world, with a hundred colour plates and thirty-seven principles. Its last chapter shows real leaves and flowers drawn at rising levels of abstraction, from the specimen to the pattern. His argument was that designers shouldn't copy flowers and leaves directly, but draw them conventionally, from the principles behind their forms. He said his book was not a pattern book. It was copied slavishly anyway.

Growing like nature is the deepest idea of the three. In 1917 the biologist D'Arcy Thompson published On Growth and Form. He argued that living things take their shapes from physics and mechanics as much as from heredity, and he called the form of an object a diagram of forces. A bone, a shell or a tree is shaped by what it carries. His best-known examples come from engineering. He described how the spongy bone inside the top of the human thigh bone had been found to follow the same lines as the stresses an engineer had drawn for a crane. And he compared the skeleton of a four-legged animal to a cantilever bridge, like the Forth Bridge in Scotland, with the backbone carrying the load out to the legs.

That opens a different way to borrow from nature: not its look, but its logic, putting material where the loads are.

Art Nouveau, around 1890 to 1910, tried to join the look and the logic. Its designers took the curving growth of plants as their line, and ran it through the structure and the ornament alike.

The best examples

The Hôtel Tassel in Brussels, built by Victor Horta in 1892 and 1893, is among the first Art Nouveau buildings. Its rooms open around a stair lit from above through a glass roof. The iron columns are exposed, and the same plant-like line runs from the ironwork into the stair rail, the floor tiles, the glass doors and the painted walls, so the decoration and the structure read as one growth. When UNESCO listed Horta's town houses in 2000, it named the open plan, the way the light is spread through the house, and the curving decoration joined to the structure.

For structure that grows, the best example is Frank Lloyd Wright's great workroom at the Johnson Wax headquarters in Racine, Wisconsin, built between 1936 and 1939. Its roof stands on a forest of slender concrete columns, each a hollow shell only two or three inches thick. Each is nine inches across at the foot and widens to a round, flat head eighteen and a half feet across, like a lily pad on a stalk, about twenty-one or twenty-two feet up. The columns are the opposite of a classical column, thin at the bottom and wide at the top, and the building inspectors doubted they would carry the twelve tons required. So in June 1937, a test column was built and loaded. It carried sixty tons, five times the requirement, before it was pulled down, and the design went ahead.

Between the heads of the columns, Wright let in the daylight. The structure was proved; the light was another matter, as you'll see below.

Rules of thumb

One. Abstract; don't copy. Learn a plant's order, its symmetry, its repetition and its growth, and design from that.

Two. Where you can, let one line do several jobs, as Horta did: the column, the rail and the ornament as one continuous growth.

Three. Put material where the forces are, as living things do: thick where the load gathers, thin everywhere else.

Four. Test a new structural idea at full size. The Johnson Wax column was accepted because it was loaded until the doubters were satisfied.

Five. Be wary of the look without the logic. A column shaped like a tree only works like one if the forces run where its branches go.

Where it fails

Copied ornament turns into wallpaper: the Grammar of Ornament became a pattern book, against its author's wishes. The style itself lasted barely two decades, and Horta's great Maison du Peuple, in Brussels, was demolished in 1965, despite a protest by more than seven hundred architects. And a natural image doesn't guarantee a good environment. At Johnson Wax, the roof lights between the columns were filled with glass tubes, which leaked for years; parts were later replaced with plastic. The Hôtel Tassel is celebrated for its light, but as far as the research edition could find, no one has ever measured it.

Recap

To sum up. There are three ways to borrow from growing things. Copy them, as the carvers of Southwell did. Abstract them, as Owen Jones taught, turning the order of a plant into pattern. Or grow like them, putting material where the forces are, as D'Arcy Thompson described and as Wright's columns at Johnson Wax did: nine inches at the foot, eighteen and a half feet across at the head, and proved to carry five times their required load.

7.39 min Biomimicry Copy the principle, not the shape Water on a lotus leafWhy water rolls off a lotus leafWater Cube, BeijingA mound that breathes
9 minutes, read aloud

Pour water on a lotus leaf and it won't spread. It gathers into beads that roll off at the slightest tilt, picking up the dust as they go, and the leaf is left clean. From 1971, the German botanist Wilhelm Barthlott surveyed the surfaces of plants with the electron microscope, and he found out why. The lotus leaf is covered in tiny bumps, and the bumps are covered in even tinier crystals of wax. A drop sits on the tips, touching almost nothing, and it rolls.

In 1997 he and Christoph Neinhuis published the finding, and it launched a field. By 2001, a façade paint built on the same principle was on the market, so that rain could clean a wall. The paint doesn't look anything like a leaf. What it copies is the principle: a surface rough at two scales, and water-repellent. That's the lesson of biomimicry in one example, and this lesson is about how to do it well, and how it goes wrong.

How it works

Biomimicry means learning from how living things solve problems. The word was made popular by the American writer Janine Benyus, in her book of 1997, Biomimicry, and she co-founded the Biomimicry Institute in 2006. The idea is old: people have always borrowed from nature. What's new is the insistence on the mechanism.

The rule is to copy the principle, not the shape. Living things get their shapes from their own materials, their own sizes and their own purposes, and those are rarely ours. A building shaped like a leaf is still a building. But a building surface that works the way a leaf surface works can do what the leaf does.

It helps to work in three steps. First, understand the biology, from measurements, not from a story. Second, state the principle in physical terms: what force, what flow, what geometry does the work? Third, build the thing and measure whether it does the job. Most failures skip the first step or the third.

For the lotus, the principle can be measured. On most surfaces, a drop of water spreads a little. The angle between the drop and the surface, called the contact angle, is under ninety degrees on a surface that wets, and over ninety on one that repels water. On a lotus leaf it's more than 165 degrees. A drop of the same size at 165 degrees touches less than a twentieth of the area it would touch at ninety, so it barely holds on, and rolls away with the dirt.

The best examples

In 2011, a team of German engineers and biologists, from Stuttgart and Freiburg, made a shading fin for façades with no hinges. They took the idea from the bird-of-paradise flower. When a bird lands on the flower's perch, the perch bends under its weight, and the petals fold aside to show the pollen. The Flectofin copies the principle: a thin fin of glass-fibre-reinforced plastic that bends, and in bending swings a flap across the light. Hinges and bearings are the parts of a moving façade that wear and jam, and this one has none.

The Water Cube, the swimming centre built for the Beijing Olympics of 2008, takes its structure from a foam. In 1993, the physicists Denis Weaire and Robert Phelan found, by computer simulation, a way of dividing space into cells of equal volume with 0.3 per cent less surface than the best arrangement known before, Kelvin's of 1887. The engineers at Arup turned that geometry into a steel frame, clad in about four thousand cushions of plastic film up to nine metres across. It's a good example of borrowing a principle, but notice where it came from: not from biology, but from mathematics and the physics of bubbles.

And then the most famous example, and the most cautionary. The Eastgate Centre, an office and shopping building in Harare, Zimbabwe, finished in 1996 by the architect Mick Pearce with the engineers Arup, is said to have been modelled on termite mounds, which were thought to keep a steady temperature through a system of chimneys. It's widely reported to use a tenth of the energy of a conventional building, and to have saved three and a half million dollars on air conditioning. There are two problems. The research edition found no published measurement behind those figures. And the biology has moved on. When researchers measured mounds of the termite Macrotermes michaelseni in Namibia, publishing in 2017, they found the mound works more like a lung than an air conditioner. The sun heats its sides by day, and the air in most of the outer channels rises; at night it cools, and the flow reverses. The wind has little to do with it. The mound's job is to exchange the colony's air.

The building may work perfectly well, by ordinary stack ventilation, as in chapter two. But the story it's told with isn't the evidence.

Rules of thumb

One. Copy the principle, not the shape. Write down what the organism does, in physical terms, before you design anything.

Two. Check that the biology is current. The termite story changed; stories do.

Three. For moving parts, consider bending instead of hinges, as the Flectofin does.

Four. Measure the result. A claim like a tenth of the energy needs a meter, not a metaphor.

Five. Credit mathematics and physics too. Not every good form comes from biology, and the principle matters more than the source.

Where it fails

The commonest failure is copying the shape and calling it biomimicry. The next is copying a principle that doesn't survive the change of scale or material. The lotus surface is a good principle, but a leaf regrows its wax and a coating doesn't: the paint's water-repellent surface wears away, soaps and oils defeat it, and in the discoverer's own look back at the field, nearly thirty years on, the research edition found no figures for how long the products last. Then there's the misread biology, as with the termites. And there are the unmeasured claims: Eastgate's energy figure, and the Water Cube's reported thirty per cent saving on energy costs, trace to no measurement the research edition could find.

Recap

To sum up. Biomimicry means learning how living things solve problems, and the rule is to copy the principle, not the shape. Understand the biology from measurements, state the principle in physical terms, build it and measure it. The lotus gave façades a self-cleaning surface; the bird-of-paradise flower gave a hingeless shade. The Water Cube borrowed from the mathematics of foam. And the Eastgate Centre, the most famous example, rests on a termite story the biology no longer supports and on energy figures nobody has published.

7.410 min Materials that grow Timber, bamboo, straw, hemp and fungus, and a bridge that grows Ise, the next siteEvery grown material has a clockMjøstårnet, NorwayRoot bridge, Meghalaya
10 minutes, read aloud

Every twenty years, at Ise, in Japan, the main buildings of the country's most sacred Shinto shrine are built again from new timber on the empty site beside them, and the old ones are taken down. The sixty-second rebuilding was in 2013, and the next is due in 2033. The custom goes back to the late seventh century, though the records show a gap of more than a hundred years in the middle ages. The timber is hinoki, Japanese cypress, seasoned in ponds. And under a plan drawn up in 1923, the shrine has been growing its own hinoki forest for future rebuildings, on a timescale of about two hundred years.

A building rebuilt from its own forest is an extreme case, but it makes the point of this lesson. Materials that grow come with a clock: the years it takes the plant to grow, and the years the material lasts once it's cut. This lesson looks at timber, bamboo, straw, hemp and fungus, and at a bridge in India that's still growing, and at what each is good for, and where each goes wrong.

How it works

Plants build themselves out of carbon taken from the air, so a building made of them holds that carbon for as long as it stands. That's the main reason for their revival. But the carbon only counts if the forest or the field grows back: one international standard for these calculations credits the stored carbon only when the wood comes from sustainably managed forest.

Timber is the oldest and most versatile. Its modern form is mass timber. Cross-laminated timber, or CLT, is made of boards glued in layers, each layer at right angles to the one below, to make big, stiff panels for walls and floors. It was developed in Austria and Germany in the 1990s, and first approved, in Austria, in 1998. Glued-laminated timber, or glulam, makes beams and columns the same way, with the layers running together. Large timber members burn slowly: the outside chars, and the char protects the wood inside.

Bamboo is a grass, and a fast one: its stems are mature in three to eight years. It's very strong along its fibres. Engineered bamboo, strips glued into boards and beams, is promising but variable: a review of more than ninety studies found compressive strengths from 7 to 82.5 megapascals, a spread of more than ten times, and, in bamboo heat-treated with oil, glue lines up to forty-seven per cent weaker after a hot soak. There are test standards, but not yet full design rules.

Straw is what's left of a grain crop. Baled and stacked like big bricks, then plastered inside and out, it makes thick, well-insulated walls. Bale building began on the plains of Nebraska, where the first documented example, a schoolhouse of 1896 or 1897, was built of hay bales; and the International Residential Code in the United States added an appendix for straw-bale building in 2015.

Hemp-lime, or hempcrete, is the chopped woody core of the hemp plant mixed with a lime binder. It's light and insulating, and it buffers moisture, but it's too weak to carry a building, so it goes around a frame. And it takes a long time to dry and harden. Near Turin, researchers monitored an experimental hemp-lime arch, which carries its own weight the way the arches of lesson one do, from December 2023 to July 2024. The moisture inside kept rising, by about ten points, as the lime slowly set; the material took one to four days to give up its heat, and its thickest parts, sixty centimetres, followed only the seasons.

Fungus is the newest. Mycelium, the fine threads that fungi grow through the ground, can bind farm waste into light blocks. In the summer of 2014, a tower about twelve metres high, called Hy-Fi, stood in the courtyard of MoMA PS1 in New York, built by the architects The Living with Ecovative and Arup from ten thousand bricks grown from farm waste and fungus. When it came down in September, the plan was to compost the bricks.

The best examples

Timber is now reaching real heights. Brock Commons Tallwood House, a student residence at the University of British Columbia in Vancouver, finished in 2017, has eighteen storeys: seventeen of timber, on a concrete podium, with CLT floors, glulam columns and concrete cores for stairs and lifts. It's about fifty-three metres tall, and the timber structure went up in two months or so. Mjøstårnet, in Norway, finished in 2019, is 85.4 metres tall, with a glulam frame and concrete floors in its top seven storeys. In 2022, a tower in Milwaukee, Ascent, overtook it, at twenty-five storeys.

The most remarkable structure that grows, though, isn't a building. In the hills of Meghalaya, in north-east India, the Khasi and Jaintia peoples grow bridges. They guide the aerial roots of the rubber fig across a stream, on a frame of bamboo, until the roots take hold on the far bank. Over the years the roots thicken and fuse, and the bridge grows stronger as it ages. A survey published in 2019 recorded seventy-six bridges, with spans from two metres to 52.7, most under twenty. Only fifteen could be dated with confidence, and one bridge held roots of about seven, eighteen and sixty-six years at once: it's always partly new. Since 2022, the living root bridges have been on UNESCO's tentative list for World Heritage.

Rules of thumb

One. Use each material for what it's good at: timber and bamboo to carry loads, straw and hemp to insulate and to buffer moisture, fungus for light and temporary work.

Two. Keep them dry. Plant materials rot if they stay wet, so detail the roof, the base of the wall and the plaster to shed water and let the wall dry out.

Three. Treat exposed timber as a fire decision. How much of a room's timber is left bare changes how a fire in it behaves.

Four. Count the stored carbon only if the forest or field grows back.

Five. Plan for the long clock. Ise plans its forest two hundred years ahead, and a root bridge needs tending for as long as it's used.

Where it fails

Moisture is the first enemy of every material in this lesson, and some, like hemp-lime, take months to dry. Fire is the second. In a study published in 2017, researchers from Edinburgh reported five fire tests in a small room built of CLT, with some of its surfaces left bare. Only one fire went out by itself, where the charred layer stayed attached. In the others, protective boards or charred layers came away and exposed fresh wood, or, with three surfaces bare, the burning walls kept each other alight, and the fires had to be put out by hand. Engineered bamboo still varies too much for full design rules. The carbon numbers are often calculator estimates, not measurements: the savings claimed for Brock Commons come from a carbon calculator. The Hy-Fi bricks lasted a summer. And even Ise's story of unbroken renewal has its gap: more than a century, in the middle ages, when the rebuilding stopped.

Recap

To sum up. Materials that grow store the carbon they took from the air, as long as the forest or field grows back. Timber is the most versatile, and mass timber towers now reach twenty-five storeys; bamboo is fast and strong but variable; straw and hemp insulate and buffer moisture, but don't carry loads; mycelium is new and short-lived. Keep them all dry, treat exposed timber as a fire decision, and plan for the clock: at Ise, a forest grown two hundred years ahead; in Meghalaya, a bridge that's never finished growing.

Check yourself

First question. Why does the carbon stored in timber only count if the forest grows back?

Because the carbon came from the air as the tree grew. If the forest isn't replanted and managed, the carbon isn't taken back up, so one international standard only credits wood from sustainably managed forest.

Second question. What happened in the Edinburgh fire tests on rooms lined with CLT?

Only one fire in five went out by itself, where the char stayed attached. In the others, protective boards or charred layers came away and exposed fresh wood, or three bare surfaces kept each other burning, and the fires had to be put out by hand.

Third question. Why does a living root bridge grow stronger with age?

Because it's alive: the rubber fig's roots keep thickening and fusing, and new roots are added, so one bridge can hold roots decades apart in age.

In 1935, Frank Lloyd Wright drew a weekend house for Edgar and Liliane Kaufmann on Bear Run, a stream in the woods of western Pennsylvania. The Kaufmanns loved the waterfall there. Wright didn't put the house where they could look at it. He put the house on top of it: concrete terraces reaching out over the stream from the rocks, and walls of sandstone quarried on the site, laid in thin courses like the rock ledges beneath. From inside you hear the falls more than you see them. The house, Fallingwater, was finished in 1937.

Wright called his approach organic architecture. It's the idea that a building should grow out of its place: from its ground, its materials, its climate and its views, so that it belongs there and nowhere else. This lesson is about that tradition, what it teaches about siting and material, and where its most famous buildings struggled.

How it works

Wright took the phrase form follows function from his teacher, Louis Sullivan, and turned it into form and function are one. In practice, the organic tradition comes down to four moves.

First, read the site before drawing anything: where the sun and the wind come from, where the water runs, what grows, what the ground is made of, and where the views are.

Second, take the materials from the place, where you can, and use them as the place shows them: stone laid the way its beds lie in the ground, timber as trees.

Third, fit the building to the land's own shapes. Terraces follow ledges, roofs follow slopes, and the building steps with the ground instead of flattening it.

Fourth, blur the line between inside and outside, with terraces, porches, walls that slide away, and views framed from where people sit.

None of these moves is new. They're what vernacular builders did everywhere, as the earlier chapters showed. What the organic architects added was ambition: to make each building a single composition with its landscape.

The best examples

Fallingwater is one of the most famous houses of the twentieth century, and one of the most thoroughly measured, but only for its structure. Its terraces began to sag as soon as they were built: when the formwork came off, the main terrace dropped by more than an inch. By the mid-1990s, its corners had sunk by seven inches and five and a half inches over a cantilever of fifteen feet. The engineer Robert Silman found its main beams working at about ninety-five per cent of their capacity. In 2001 and 2002, steel cables were threaded through the structure and tensioned, lifting the terrace back by about half an inch to three quarters of an inch, at a cost of about four million dollars.

Taliesin West, Wright's winter home and school near Scottsdale, Arizona, begun in 1937, is built of what he called desert masonry: rocks gathered nearby, set flat face out in wooden forms, with concrete poured in behind them. The walls lean at fifteen degrees, and the roofs were originally canvas. It's a building made from its desert. But it was also a winter camp: Wright and his apprentices spent seven months of each year in Wisconsin, and left Arizona before the summer. Moving with the seasons is an old and honest climate strategy; it's just worth saying out loud.

In Finland, Alvar Aalto's Villa Mairea, built in 1938 and 1939 for Harry and Maire Gullichsen in a pine forest, brings the forest inside. Unstripped young tree trunks hold up the entrance canopy, columns are bound in rattan, and a sliding wall opens the house to the garden. It makes no claims about energy or climate. It set out to bring the forest in as feeling and material, and it does.

On the northern California coast, the landscape architect Lawrence Halprin planned The Sea Ranch in 1962, on a former sheep ranch: 2,310 house sites on 3,500 acres, with half the land kept in common. Instead of scattering houses across the open meadows, he grouped them between the lines of trees, the hedgerows, that already crossed the land, where they're sheltered from the strong coastal wind and leave the meadows and their views open. Sheep still graze there, to keep the grass short against fire. One of the first buildings, Condominium One, of 1965, by the architects Moore, Lyndon, Turnbull and Whitaker, is ten homes in redwood under roofs that slope towards the sea.

Rules of thumb

One. Read the site before you draw: sun, wind, water, ground, what grows, and where the views are.

Two. Take the materials from the place where you can, and lay them the way the place shows them.

Three. Shelter in the lee of what's already there: hedgerows, slopes and trees, as at The Sea Ranch and in the windbreak lesson of chapter two.

Four. Open the building to the landscape in the good season, and close it, or leave it, in the hard one.

Five. Engineer the daring parts properly. A cantilever over a waterfall is a structural decision before it's a poetic one.

Where it fails

The organic tradition is admired far more than it's measured. Fallingwater has been measured exhaustively, but only for how fast it was sinking; as far as the research edition could find, no one has published a single measurement of the conditions inside. Taliesin West solved its climate partly by leaving it for the summer. No source the research edition found contains a wind measurement for The Sea Ranch, though its siting is famous for its response to the wind; the hedgerows themselves may have been the instrument that showed where the wind blew. And Villa Mairea is often cited in writing on nature and health as if it made claims it never made. The lesson is to enjoy these buildings for what they are, great compositions with their landscapes, and to be careful about crediting them with performance nobody measured.

Recap

To sum up. Organic architecture means making a building out of its place: read the site, take the materials from it, fit the building to the land, and blur inside and outside. Fallingwater sits over its waterfall and has cost decades of structural repair; Taliesin West is made of its desert and was left each summer; Villa Mairea brings its forest in, and claims nothing more; The Sea Ranch tucked its houses between the hedgerows, out of the wind. They're great lessons in siting and material, and mostly unmeasured for comfort.
