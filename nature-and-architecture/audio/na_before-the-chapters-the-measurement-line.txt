This is the front of a book about nature and architecture, and it begins with a confession about how the book was made, because the way it was made turned out to be its argument.

The book was planned as a history. Eight chapters, from the first decorated caves to the materials now being grown in laboratories, each asking how people brought the living world into the buildings they made, around them, and to the line between inside and out. Three axes were laid down at the start. Time, which gives the chapters their order. Place, meaning the world region and its climate, whether hot and dry, hot and humid, temperate, cold or high. And position, meaning whether the nature in question stands outside the building, inside it, or at its threshold, which this book treats as a real place rather than a line, because porches and courtyards and screens and sunspaces are where most of the interesting things happen. Across all three axes runs a spectrum of seven modes: nature as image, as borrowed form, as analysed model, as living material, as the climate the building answers, as a working system the building belongs to, and as a partner with standing of its own. The spectrum is not a ladder. A building from the present day can sit at image and a building from four thousand years ago can sit at system.

Then the research began, and one instruction changed everything. Every lane was told that for every celebrated building, tradition or device in its territory it must ask a single question: has anyone ever put instruments on this, and what did they find. And where the answer was no, the lane was told to write the absence down as a finding rather than pass over it.

Forty-one lanes did that, across eight chapters, and their answers did not come back as a scatter. They came back in kinds. The same kinds, again and again, in different centuries and on different continents. By the end there were sixteen of them, and together they are the line this book is drawn along.

The first kind is the simplest and it governs the first three chapters. The measurement was never made. The idea is ancient and the number is absent. The courtyard, the wind tower, the heated floor, the tuned cave, the sited house, the glasshouse: all of them were built, celebrated, copied and written about, and where a tradition is most celebrated the instruments are most often missing. When a wind tower or a courtyard was finally measured, in the second chapter, the reading tended to contradict the reputation.

The second kind belongs to the fourth chapter and it is worse than the first. The measurement was commissioned, it came back unfavourable, and the building went up anyway. A sealed double-skin wall was tested at full scale in a laboratory in the early nineteen thirties, the report said it would need far more heat and more than twice the fan energy of ordinary heating, and the building was finished the following year with the double skin deleted and the seal kept. It overheated at once.

The third kind arrives with the fifth chapter, when instruments finally spread through the profession. The measurement was made and it is enclosed. Behind paywalls, inside purchased standards, in trade journals no index reaches. The profession's most candid examination of its own buildings turns out to be its least obtainable.

The fourth kind is the sixth chapter's. The measurement was made, given away freely, and still lost, because a live web page is not a durable record. The one standard that certifies a building only after a year of metered occupation has no retrievable published study of any building it has certified.

The seventh chapter, which is the practice chapter, adds five kinds of its own. The measurement was made, enclosed, and simultaneously made mandatory, so that a legal limit on the shape of a building rests on papers the regulated profession cannot read. The measurement was made, is reachable, and does not support the claim it is cited for, because nobody held the instrument at a standard distance. The measurement was made and openly published and the metric was standardised without it, so that the validation and the reproducibility studies all sit downstream of the rule they contradict. The measurement was made once, and the second measurement was never commissioned, because nobody funds the second visit. And the measurement is made universally at the coarsest possible resolution, one number per building per month at the meter, and almost nowhere at any finer one.

The eighth chapter, looking forward, adds seven more. Measured at the scale of the laboratory coupon and asserted at the scale of the building. Made, but made about a computer model rather than a building. Made, licensed open, and still undelivered. Made, readable, one query away, and simply not quoted by anyone advocating the thing it measures. Measured on the artefact, the nest box or the crossing, and never on the population it was built for. Made only in the future tense, as a preprint or a projection. And made by two methods that disagree by a factor of two or more, with the discipline having standardised on the cheaper one.

Sixteen kinds. The chapters were not written to produce them; they were found in the evidence and then named. Two things cut across all sixteen and are the plainest statements this book can make.

Measurement density collapses at the façade. Lane after lane found the space between buildings instrumented to a fine grain, and materials measured to the micronewton, and organisms measured in their tens of thousands, and then almost nothing about the rooms people actually occupy. One lane's entire body of measured interior evidence for its subject topped out at a study of eighty-nine people, set against eight million people measured outdoors.

And then it collapses again at the person. One lane's largest instrumented human sample in the open air was sixteen skin sensors, set against thousands of trees. The final chapter found the sequence running one step further, from the street to the room to the person to the controller, whose own energy use not a single paper in its field reported, and then a step further still, to the other species a building was designed to shelter, whose outcomes nobody had measured at all.

There is one necessary honesty about that. The interior is measured at scale for one question, thermal comfort, where a public database holds more than a hundred thousand records from twenty-three countries. So the charge is not that the inside of a building cannot be measured. It is that nobody has chosen to measure it for the questions this book is about.

And there is one more pattern, the strangest. The enclosure is selective. One lane asked for two papers from the same journal, the same issue, with overlapping authors, in the same minute. The paper of conclusions opened. The paper of measurements was withheld by its publisher. Whatever the mechanism, across this whole book the thing that gets enclosed is the number.

That is the line. Each chapter that follows will say, near the end of its own narrative, which of the sixteen kinds it sits in. The closing section, after the eighth chapter, will say what an architect who accepts all this might actually do differently on a Monday morning. And in between, the chapters do what the book was commissioned to do, which is tell the history, with every claim graded, every conflict left standing, and every gap named.
