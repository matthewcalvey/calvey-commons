This is the third chapter of a series about how people have brought nature into buildings, and it covers the period from about 1750 to 1914, from the Enlightenment to the eve of the First World War. The series asks one question all the way through: how has nature been brought inside, outside and to the threshold of buildings, and what does that record actually establish for an architect deciding what to do now. The first two chapters found that the ideas were ancient and the measurements were not. This chapter is where the measurement begins. A building is read by thermometer while eighty thousand people stand inside it. A house's glazed loggia is recorded at a temperature on a stated date and its dead plants counted. A hospital ward is specified in cubic feet per patient. A cure is followed to its five-year mortality. And, over and over, the building goes on as if the number had never been taken.

The spectrum the series uses runs from nature as image, through form, model, material, climate and system, to nature as partner, and it is not a ladder. In this period the whole spectrum shares a single roof. In 1851 Gottfried Semper took the origin of the wall from a Caribbean hut on show inside a building that was itself a horticultural detail repeated nearly three hundred thousand times; Owen Jones painted its ironwork red, yellow and blue from the Alhambra; and a stand of elms grew through its transept because the building had been bent to keep them alive. Image, model, material, climate and system, all at once, under one glass. Partner is the mode almost absent as a stated idea. Here nature is dosed, staged, framed, grown under glass or shipped in a box, and where it is a partner it is by accident: the grazing animals that mow the park beyond the sunken wall, the elms, the vetiver screen whose scent is part of its cooling, and the sealed case in which the plant's own breath is its water supply.

Begin with the idea, because the period begins with one. In 1753 a Jesuit priest named Marc-Antoine Laugier published anonymously in Paris an essay on architecture whose first move is to imagine a man in a state of nature. The cave is damp and the forest floor exposed, so he sets four trunks upright, lays beams across them and pitches branches over the top, and that shelter, not the cave and not any custom, is the origin of building. From it Laugier draws a standard: the members the hut needs, column, entablature and pediment, are the legitimate members of architecture, and the arch, the pilaster and the attic are licences or faults. Two years later a second edition carried his name and Charles Eisen's engraving, the most reproduced image of natural architecture in the European tradition, in which Architecture sits among broken classical fragments and points a putto toward four trees whose branches make a roof. Rebecca Williamson has noticed that Eisen drew those trees rooted and growing, which Laugier's text does not say: the engraver put the living plant back into an argument the author had made about dead timber. The hut has no climate, no orientation and no interior. Nature supplies the logic of the frame and nothing else, and nobody has ever measured the hut, because it was never meant to be measurable. The research could not open a paginated copy of either edition, so this chapter carries no chapter references for Laugier and does not guess them.

Now the garden, because in England the argument about nature was made with earth before it was made with stone. The founding word for irregular beauty in the English landscape garden is sharawadgi, which Sir William Temple used in an essay written in 1685 for a beauty achieved without evident order, and which he associated with China. Nobody agrees where the word came from. Y. Z. Chang in 1930 derived it from a Chinese phrase for ungoverned grace; Qian Zhongshu in 1940 from Chinese words for scattered and disorderly; Susi Lang and Nikolaus Pevsner in 1948 concluded it could not be shown to be Chinese at all; E. V. Gatenby and later Ciaran Murray proposed a Japanese word for the not aligned; Wybe Kuitert in 2014 proposed a different Japanese term, from applied art, carried to Europe on Dutch East India Company ships and re-read to fit; Michael Sullivan suggested corrupted Persian; and a paper of 2021 moves the whole question into Temple's own Stoic circle. Five or six positions after nearly a century, no verdict, and nobody has done the one measurable thing, which would be a corpus study of the word across four languages.

The period's cleanest demonstration that a working agricultural system can be made to do aesthetic work is the ha-ha, credited by the National Trust to Charles Bridgeman at Stowe. It is a deep dry ditch with an inner wall built up to the level of the garden, so that from the lawn the mown ground seems to run unbroken into the grazed park, while from the field it is a wall that cattle and deer cannot climb: the optical device and the stock barrier are the same object, and the animals beyond it maintain the picture. Nobody has published its typical depth or studied it as a structure or a wildlife corridor.

The quarrel about how much roughness a landscape may keep was fought in the 1790s, when Uvedale Price added the Picturesque to Burke's sublime and beautiful as a third term, made of roughness, intricacy and the marks of age; Richard Payne Knight located it not in the object but in the trained eye; and Humphry Repton replied that a landscape is not a picture but a place lived in, approached and drained. This chapter carries all of that from the standard account, unverified against the texts, and adds one line from scholarship it could not open: Charles Watkins has argued that Price was a working woodland manager whose doctrine had consequences for how trees were grown and felled, and if that holds, the estate records at Foxley would be the one measured source in the whole aesthetic dispute.

Here is the first honest limit. The English landscape garden is among the most studied designed landscapes in the world, and the study is entirely art-historical. The research found no instrumented study of microclimate, shelter, wind, comfort, sound or daylight at Stowe, Rousham, Stourhead or Painshill, none of the planting belts as shelter, none of any cottage orné, hermitage or root house. The period's serious climate thinking went not into the house but into the small unglamorous buildings around it, and those have not been measured either.

Take the ice house. The Dorset History Centre describes the English type as a brick-lined chamber, domed above, buried under a mound of earth and entered by a low passage with doors, and it names four principles: mass and burial, an entrance to the north, sometimes the shade of trees, and drainage, because standing meltwater eats the ice. A specification survives from the Lulworth estate: walls five feet six inches thick all round a well five feet across, with a brick shell four inches thick where it touches the ice, which is roughly thirteen inches of construction for every inch that meets the cold. Susan Roaf argued in 1991 that twentieth-century warming had carried the British ice house across the threshold of its viability, and the abstract, which is all the research could reach, contains no threshold temperature; that figure stays unfilled. The physics of the type comes from somewhere else entirely. Pochee, Gunstone and Wilton modelled the Iranian ice store at Yazd, where shallow water spread in a shaded pond on winter nights freezes not by contact with cold air but by radiating heat to the clear sky. In the coldest week the modelled losses are sixty watts a square metre to the sky, twenty to evaporation, eight gained back from the ground, and one, a single watt, to convection. Convection, the mechanism a modern reader assumes, is about one and a half per cent of the total. The eighteenth-century English builder, with his north door and his tree shade and his earth mound, could not have known that the dominant mechanism was radiation to the night sky, and got it right anyway.

The grotto is the ice house's thermal relative. At Painshill, openings in the ceiling throw daylight onto water and crystal-encrusted rock, the water moves and can be heard, and the lake outside reflects light up into the chamber. At Stourhead the National Trust records that Henry Hoare noted enjoying his grotto as a cooling retreat in summer, which is the chapter's one recorded thermal experience of a garden building, and one man's remark rather than a design intent.

Now cross the Atlantic to the one place in this period where natural philosophy, instrumental observation and building were carried out by one person on one site. Thomas Jefferson kept a weather diary at Monticello for more than fifty years, from 1776, two readings a day with close to twenty thermometers over a lifetime, and he wired a weathervane on the roof to a compass rose on the ceiling of the north-east portico, so that wind direction could be read from under the roof without stepping out, which makes the threshold itself an instrument. Alison Dolbier of the Jefferson Papers has shown that the ledger of his ice house works as a climate proxy: the ice lasted to mid-October in both 1815 and 1816, the year without a summer. And then the greenhouse. It was not a separate building but the south piazza of the house, a small glazed loggia with five semicircular sashes and five pairs of square ones, complete by about 1809, and unheated. In January 1810 his observations record the greenhouse at twenty-one degrees Fahrenheit while his bedroom stood at thirty-seven. In 1811 he wrote to the nurseryman Bernard McMahon that during his previous winter's absence every plant in it had perished; by about 1816 the room was a family sitting room. That is a fully documented, quantified failure of an attempt to bring living plants into the fabric of a house, made by the best-instrumented amateur scientist in North America, for a stated and measured reason, and honestly abandoned. It is the counter-example to every triumphant account of the glasshouse, and it comes first.

The glasshouse is the chapter's technical centre, because it is the first building type whose whole purpose is to manufacture an interior climate for occupants who are not people. John Claudius Loudon invented a wrought-iron glazing bar in 1816 that could be bent to a curve, published the idea of a folded ridge-and-furrow roof, and, on the evidence the research could reach, never built one. Joseph Paxton built what Loudon published, and built it for seventeen years before 1851. In his lecture to the Society of Arts in November 1850 he gave his own sequence: he began investigating glass structures in 1828, built an experimental pine house on the ridge-and-furrow principle in 1833, and began the Great Conservatory at Chatsworth in 1837, completing it in 1840. His reason for folding the roof was daylight, because low morning and evening sun strikes a flat roof at a grazing angle and is largely reflected, while a folded roof presents a face nearly square to it in the hours a plant house most needs light. That is an annual-daylight argument made in 1833, arrived at empirically, and never measured for illuminance. The conservatory was heated by eight coal boilers in a basement served by an underground tramway, through eleven kilometres of pipe, burning about three hundred and five tonnes of coal a winter, with none of it visible from the garden. No source gives the temperature it maintained, so the coal cannot be turned into a heat-loss rate, and that figure stays unfilled.

Then the lily. In August 1849 Paxton took a small plant of the giant Amazonian waterlily from Kew and set it in a tank at eighty-five degrees Fahrenheit; by the second of November its largest leaf was fourteen feet round, and in late November his daughter Annie stood on one. A Lily House followed in 1850, eighteen metres by fourteen, with a ten-metre tank held between twenty-six and thirty-two degrees Celsius through the Derbyshire winter by hot-water pipes. That maintained band is the chapter's clearest climate datum, an interior set by one organism's physiology. The famous story is that the leaf's underside, with its radiating ribs and cross-girders, was the model for the Crystal Palace, and that Paxton said nature was the engineer. Dan Nielsen documents Paxton analysing the leaf as a structure and places his presentation with a lily leaf on the same day as the presentation of the Crystal Palace drawings, the thirteenth of November 1850. The Illustrated London News report of that lecture, examined twice with targeted searches, mentions the lily only twice and only as horticulture, and its structural explanation of the building is the daylighting argument for the folded roof. The report may be abridged, and both accounts stand. But Paxton's own dated sequence puts the roof, the sash bar and the gutter sixteen years before the plant arrived, and the research found no primary text in which he says nature was the engineer. The phrase is not attributed to him here.

The Crystal Palace itself can be read as a chain from pane to plan. Chance Brothers of Smethwick blew cylinder glass into a pane forty-nine inches by ten, the largest their process could make, at sixty thousand or sixty-three thousand panes a fortnight depending on the source, after the glass excise was repealed in 1845; that pane sets an eight-foot roof unit, three units make the bay, and seventy-seven bays make the recorded length of one thousand eight hundred and forty-eight feet, a chain that is the research lane's inference and is marked as such. Paxton, before construction, said nine hundred thousand square feet; Tom Chance counts two hundred and ninety-three thousand six hundred and fifty-five panes shipped, which at the stated size comes to about nine hundred and ninety-nine thousand square feet. Two internally consistent families, one measuring what was required and one what was shipped, and this chapter keeps both and averages neither.

And now the measurement. From the nineteenth of May to the fourteenth of October 1851 the Royal Sappers and Miners read fourteen thermometers inside the building and three outside every two hours between nine in the morning and six in the evening, and they also worked the ventilators. Henrik Schoenefeldt has reconstructed that record from the Commissioners' report of 1852 and fifty-seven newspaper articles, with outside temperatures from the Horticultural Society's garden at Chiswick. On the twenty-seventh of June the building reached ninety-seven degrees Fahrenheit against eighty-three in the shade outside. Across the season the internal peaks exceeded the external ones by two to fifteen degrees, the internal minima exceeded the external minima by three to twenty, daily swings ran to ten and twenty degrees, and on seventy of the hundred and twenty-six days the mean inside was above the mean outside. The whole roof was covered externally in translucent calico, so that the most famous glass building of the century was experienced under a fabric shade in a diffused even light, and there were three tiers of louvres worked by wheel and cord; but the largest single opening, about forty thousand eight hundred square feet of free area by Schoenefeldt's calculation, was unintended, the gaps between the floorboards over the undercroft. It was not enough. On the second of July the glazing of the east and west walls was taken off; on the seventh, part of the north and south galleries; on the nineteenth, with the minimum down to fifty-nine, it went back. Schoenefeldt concludes that Paxton's intention of a moderate and pleasant interior was not fulfilled. The first building in history to be instrumented and read hourly while occupied was read as a failure. Then it was rebuilt at Sydenham, larger, from 1854, and the canvas shading was abandoned so that direct sun could come in and the glass could sparkle. That second Crystal Palace stood eighty-two years, until it burned in November 1936, and was never monitored: the shading came off for the sake of the image, having been put on for the sake of the interior.

Kew shows what the type became when it kept its plants. Decimus Burton and the ironmaster Richard Turner built the Palm House between 1844 and 1848 as a wrought-iron hull on shipbuilding lines, its heating hidden in a basement next door and its flue gas carried away to a detached bell tower that is a chimney. Today it holds a minimum of eighteen degrees Celsius, opens its vents at twenty-eight, keeps humidity above seventy-five per cent by misting, and is still watered by hand every day. The Temperate House, built between 1862 and 1899, holds everything above ten degrees and opens its roof vents at twelve. Two buildings on one site, in one weather, holding two manufactured climates, and in both the setpoint is written by the plants, which is the definition of the type. It is also where the modern enclosure begins: the engineers of the coming Palm House renovation say they made a full performance benchmark of the building, and their project page discloses no number of any kind.

The glasshouse reduced to its minimum is a box. In 1829 the London doctor Nathaniel Ward sealed a moth cocoon in a bottle with some soil and found a fern and a grass growing inside; the seal held about four years. In July 1833 two glazed cases of British ferns reached Sydney from London in good condition, and the return voyage in 1834 brought Australian plants through a range from minus seven to forty degrees Celsius. Inside the case the plant's own transpiration condenses on the glass and runs back to the soil, so it needs no watering for months. John Livingstone had estimated in 1819 that about one plant in a thousand survived the voyage from China to London; after the case, William Hooker at Kew imported more plants in fifteen years than in the whole preceding century, and Japanese honeysuckle went everywhere, because the same closed climate moved the pests. The climate, not the structure, was the invention. The continental winter gardens are far less measured. The royal greenhouses at Laeken outside Brussels were designed by Alphonse Balat from 1874, and after his death in 1895 by Maquet and Girault. Victor Horta was Balat's apprentice and did not design them, whatever the caption says; the pupillage is the lineage, the glasshouse engineer teaching the Art Nouveau architect. Laeken's Winter Garden dome is fifty-seven metres across, and the complex burned about eight hundred thousand litres of fuel oil a year until 2021, when heat from a waste incinerator was piped in. The 1847 winter garden on the Champs-Élysées could not be verified at all, and a promising paper on it turned out to describe a glass installation for a fashion house in 2022. No interior of any of these has a published temperature.

Now the counted bodies. This is where the argument for air and light stops being made from authority and taste and starts being made from mortality returns, and it is also where the architecture most plainly outlived the evidence. Florence Nightingale's prescription for a hospital ward, as compiled from her writings, is fifteen hundred cubic feet of space per patient, a ward no more than thirty feet wide and no less than sixteen high, two beds to a window, the windows openable and running from a foot or two above the floor to the ceiling in opposing pairs on the two long walls, two open fireplaces, and windows to east and west for the sun. The number fifteen hundred appears in one source as a volume and in another as a rate, fifteen hundred cubic feet an hour for the healthy and up to five thousand for the sick, and the literature conflates them; they are different things. She also treated an air movement of about a foot and a half a second as imperceptible, which is an early comfort velocity. The honest limit is that every one of these figures reached this chapter at second hand, because the Internet Archive was blocked at the session's gateway and neither the 1859 nor the 1863 edition of her Notes on Hospitals could be read, so nothing here can be tied to an edition.

At Scutari the numbers are four and they do not agree. Four thousand and seventy-seven soldiers died in her first winter, about ten times as many from disease as from wounds; the death rate is given as falling from forty-two point seven per cent to two point two after the Sanitary Commission's works, as forty-two in February 1855 with thirty-seven and a half overall against twelve and a half at the front, and as sixty before reform; all four are reported with their scopes. Whether her nursing or the Commission's drains caused the fall is disputed on four sides: the traditional account from Cook's biography of 1914; Hugh Small's revision, that the decisive change came when the Commission flushed the sewers in March 1855; Lynn McDonald's counter, that she suspected the drains early; and Small's own admission that the question stays open against the reduction of trench duty.

A rare primary record lets the prescription be tested against practice: the new pavilion ward of the Presbyterian Hospital of Philadelphia, described by its engineer in 1875, was thirty feet wide, her maximum exactly, fifteen feet clear, a foot under her minimum, and held twenty-eight beds in eighty-eight feet, about fourteen hundred and ten cubic feet a bed, below her fifteen hundred. And here is the strangest fact in the chapter. When germ theory dissolved the miasma that the pavilion was argued from, the ventilation numbers did not fall; they rose. Tredgold in 1836 asked four cubic feet a minute a person; Galton in 1880, fifty; Billings in 1884, sixty; and the heating and ventilating engineers in 1895 a minimum of thirty, written into model legislation by 1914, a rate too high for windows to deliver, so that the air logic of the open ward migrated into ducts while the plan survived on daylight, nursing sightlines and habit. Only one surviving Nightingale ward has been measured with instruments: at St Luke's in Bradford, Gilkeson and colleagues used tracer gas on fifty days in 2010 and found three and a half to six and a half air changes an hour with the windows open, and roughly four times the exposure with them shut, in a ward seven and a quarter metres wide, tighter than her thirty feet. Nobody has measured the daylight in any of them.

The sanatorium is the chapter's best-documented case of an architecture built on a health claim that can be checked, and it has been checked. Hermann Brehmer opened the first at Görbersdorf in Silesia in 1854, at six to nine hundred metres, on a regime of mountain air, food and exercise; his pupil Peter Dettweiler opened Falkenstein in 1876 and replaced exercise with rest. That single medical decision made the architecture, because a regime of walking needs paths, while a regime of lying still in the open air needs a roofed, sheltered, open-sided room facing the sun, deep enough for a body on a reclining chair, and that room is the cure porch. At Saranac Lake in the Adirondacks, where Edward Trudeau began in 1884, it acquired the fullest specification of a threshold anywhere in this series: built out from the second storey, sized to turn a bed, no deeper than one and a half times its width, two sides opening fully, reached through a doorway forty-five to forty-eight inches wide with no sill and the floorboards running through, lined in beadboard because it must be disinfected, its floor sloping away from the house with drain slots in the wainscot, facing south by south-west in winter and north in summer with deciduous shade.

And the outcome. At Dannenfels, the works sanatorium of a chemical company, a cohort of a hundred patients treated for six to twelve months between 1893 and 1898 came out as twenty-one healed enough to work, sixteen much improved, twelve improved, four improved for a while, fifteen absconded, twenty-nine dead and three still inside. The German verdict by 1907 was that three point four per cent of patients were cured and that two thirds of former patients were invalids within five years. In Britain the Medical Research Council's report of 1919 found that patients from the King Edward the Seventh sanatorium at Midhurst were not restored to the health of the general population; the report of 1924 expected lasting arrest only in early cases and made no comparison with any untreated group, which is the decisive flaw, because the sanatorium was never tested against no sanatorium; and the London County Council's data of 1927 showed that of three thousand patients discharged after treatment, two thousand two hundred and eighty were dead by 1932, seventy-six per cent in five years. A later Canadian series found the death rate beginning to fall before effective combined therapy, which its authors read as isolation doing the work; and the medical historian Flurin Condrau adds that the cure figures were inflated by admission policy. Four positions stand together: that it cured, that the statistics were an artefact, that it was never controlled, and that it worked as isolation. The cure porch outlived the cure.

Light fared better than air, in a narrow way. Auguste Rollier opened his heliotherapy clinic at Leysin in 1903 for tuberculosis of the bones and glands, and his protocol is what turns sunlight into architecture: five minutes on the feet on the first day, twenty-five-minute sessions by the fifth, the whole body by about the fifteenth, two to three hours a day in summer and three to four in winter. A building that delivers that dose needs a surface a patient can be wheeled onto by the clock, facing a known way, usable in winter, and stepped in section so that no balcony shades the one below. Rollier's tally after the war was one thousand seven hundred and forty-six of two thousand one hundred and sixty-seven recovered, uncontrolled, and for a different disease from the one the pulmonary sanatoria treated, so the two sets of figures cannot be compared. But in the winter of 1918 to 1919 Kurt Huldschinsky in Berlin used ultraviolet lamps on rachitic children and it worked, and that specific claim became vitamin D. The general sunlight cure for tuberculosis failed; the specific one for rickets was true.

The open-air school is the cheapest experiment in the chapter and the most honest. The first was a forest school at Charlottenburg near Berlin in 1904; the first in England, at Bostall Wood in Plumstead in the summer of 1907, was two large sheds and some benches, about a hundred and eight children chosen for anaemia, weak hearts and tuberculosis risk, five teachers, a day from nine to six with three substantial meals and a long rest, and a kitchen a quarter of a mile away so that the food arrived cold. The children gained on average nearly a stone each over the summer; the same sentence in the source glosses that as three kilograms, and a stone is six and a third, so the source disagrees with itself and both readings are reported. There was no control group, and no way to tell the effect of the air from the effect of the dinners. The school named in the original brief as Vittra turns out to be a present-day Swedish company that runs schools, not a school of 1900.

The law is where the numbers were never checked at all. New York's Tenement House Act of 1867 required a window in every room and gave it no dimension; the act of 1879 produced the dumbbell plan, whose six-foot shaft between neighbours admitted almost no light to the lower floors and was later acknowledged as unsanitary; and the act of 1901 required every room to have a window on a street, a yard at least four feet deep, or a court of at least twenty-five square feet open to the sky. In Berlin the building ordinance set the tenement courtyard at five point three four metres square, precisely, because a horse-drawn fire engine had to be able to turn in it: the most exact number in the chapter's housing law is a vehicle manoeuvre, and its consequence was the five-storey light well. In England the Prescription Act of 1832 gave an owner a right to daylight after twenty years of enjoying it, a duration with no quantity, and in 1904 the House of Lords in Colls against Home and Colonial Stores set the test as sufficient light according to the ordinary notions of mankind, a standard that requires an expert to say what it means. That judgment created professional demand for a number. The daylight factor as an idea is attributed to Alexander Pelham Trotter in 1895, his book of 1911 could not be opened, and the working number, Percy Waldram's sky factor of nought point two per cent, about one foot-candle or ten lux, is a figure of the 1920s applied to a test of 1904. The law specified openings while nobody could say what an opening delivered, and the research found no instrumented daylight measurement of any building of the period, made then or since.

The ornament of the period keeps three things apart that the word organic blurs: pattern copied or conventionalised from nature, form abstracted from the principle of growth, and structure computed from a natural principle. Owen Jones made abstraction teachable in the Grammar of Ornament of 1856, ending it with leaves and flowers shown at several degrees of abstraction; his rule was to idealise, never to copy. Christopher Dresser taught designers to draw the plant as one knows it to be, showing its law of growth, and in Irena Yamboliev's reading he abstracted not the plant but the way perception responds to it; nothing he claimed has ever been tested. John Ruskin's Lamp of Beauty of 1849 is usually read as a command to copy nature, and it is not: it rations imitation by viewing distance, by function and by the dignity of the subject, which is a positional argument, the same ornament being image at one distance and form at another. In the Nature of Gothic he made the workman's freedom the cause of the turn to nature, so that the social claim and the botanical claim cannot be separated, and William Morris inherited both. Viollet-le-Duc read the same Gothic leaf differently, arguing that the carvers studied buds closely but subordinated them to the stone; and it was Viollet-le-Duc, by Horta's own later account, who handed Art Nouveau its method.

Several corrections belong here. Ernst Haeckel's Art Forms in Nature appeared in instalments from 1899 to 1904, drawn by him and lithographed by Adolf Giltsch, so that the regularity architects admired had passed through two hands. The charge that Haeckel idealised his subjects is real and documented, but it attaches to his embryo plates, where three embryos of different species were once printed from one block, and Robert Richards has argued that even there fraud is not proven; on the radiolaria his drawing was meticulous and set the standard, so the claim that the plates architects copied were symmetricised is unsupported by the sources reached. And René Binet's monumental gate for the Paris exhibition of 1900, the cleanest documented case of a scientific plate becoming a building, did not come from those plates: Robert Proctor shows that Binet drew on Haeckel's report on the radiolaria for the Challenger expedition of 1887, and that he wrote to Haeckel in March 1899 saying everything from the composition to the smallest detail was inspired by his studies, while the Art Forms had not appeared when the gate was designed. The usual caption is backwards. Three further corrections reached this chapter with no material behind them and are carried as corrections only: the attribution of the Green Man dates to about 1931 and not 1939; a letter of Raphael's deriving Gothic from bound trees could not be verified; and James Hall's essay deriving the Gothic cathedral from bound willow rods was read on the sixth of April 1797, not 1792. Horta's own Hôtel Tassel of 1893 draws one plant line through the railing, the tile, the glass door, the skylight and the mural, five materials at five distances from the hand, and nobody has measured its light.

Only Antoni Gaudí let nature do the designing rather than the decorating. For the crypt of the Colònia Güell, commissioned in 1898, he hung a net of cords loaded with small sacks at a scale of one to ten from the ceiling of a shed. A chain hanging under load takes a pure tension curve, and inverted it is a pure compression line for the same loads, so the net solved for a masonry form that works in compression only, without calculation; a mirror on the floor showed the hanging form the right way up, and he photographed it, inverted the photograph and drew the building over it. The model does not survive, one photograph of it does, and the ballast is lead in two sources and sand in a third. Inside the Sagrada Família the columns branch like a forest and the vault skylights are hyperboloids, a geometry and a light fitting in one decision, and the most celebrated coloured interior of the period has no published measurement of its light.

The park returns nature to the industrial city on engineering terms. Central Park's competition of 1857 required at least four transverse roads, and Olmsted and Vaux sank them in cuts below the park's surface so that commercial traffic crosses without entering the scenery; but Olmsted's first act as superintendent, in October 1857, before any scenery existed, was a drainage plan. The commissioners' report for 1859 records one season's work: one million one hundred and forty-four thousand eight hundred cubic yards of earth moved, ten and three-quarter miles of drains laid, and an average of three thousand and twenty-seven men a day. The picturesque was held together by infrastructure it was built to hide. Olmsted's health claim of 1865 rested on the long careers of British statesmen and a list of nervous disorders, and was first put to a controlled test only in 1984; the hundred and nineteen years between the claim and the test are part of the story.

The Back Bay Fens in Boston is the chapter's one true system. A tidal basin fouled by the sewage of two streams was, from 1878, made by Olmsted with the city engineer into a water gate, two conduits and a storage basin, and planted to read as a salt marsh. Of the hundred acres bought, only about fifty could be used for recreation, because half the park was water-management volume, and within twenty years the park department dredged over a hundred thousand cubic yards of sludge from it. Then in 1910 the Charles River Dam held the river at a constant fresh-water level and the tide stopped: the salt marsh died, and after about fifteen years of working life the machine was gone and only the picture remained.

Ebenezer Howard's garden city of 1898 is a set of numbers before it is a plan: an estate of six thousand acres, a thousand of them town and five thousand agricultural belt; thirty-two thousand people; six boulevards a hundred and twenty feet wide; and a rule that when the city was full, growth went to a new one beyond the belt. Every one of those figures is arithmetic; not one was measured from anything about health or climate. Welwyn, begun in 1920, had thirteen and a half thousand people by 1938 against a target of thirty-two thousand. What travelled from Howard was the picture of a house in a garden; what stayed behind was the belt, the cap and the common ownership that made it a city. The model villages that preceded it delivered gardens, light and drains and charged for them in obedience, and the mortality improvement the record asserts for them comes with no figure attached.

The last subject is empire, handled critically. The bungalow descends from a Bengali rural house in bamboo and thatch; the word is in East India Company records from 1676. The plan logic is Bengali knowledge, developed by Bengali builders for the delta's heat, wet and rain, and the colonial bungalow is an appropriation of it under European occupancy and servant hierarchy; the standard study by Anthony King could not be opened and no dimension of any colonial bungalow was found. The veranda does four physical things: it shades the wall, it shades the opening so windows can stay open in rain, it buffers the room with a ventilated volume, and it is occupiable in the cool hours. It also draws a line, because the continuous margin round a central hall controls who may stand, who may wait and who may enter, and the same line was drawn again by the punkah rope: the comfortable colonial room was a labour system, a wetted vetiver screen doused by a worker standing outside it, a cloth fan pulled by a servant who did not share the air he moved. Sylvia Houghteling notes that the screen worked through moisture and scent as much as through temperature, and that nobody measured it. The veranda worked, and it was an instrument of separation, and both statements stand together.

The army wrote the rules down. After the rebellion of 1857 a Royal Commission on the sanitary state of the army in India, with Nightingale behind its method, reported in 1863, and the Cantonment Acts followed from 1864. Surgeon-General De Renzy told the Royal Society of Medicine in 1883 that European troops in India had died at sixty-nine per thousand a year between 1800 and 1856 and that Bengal averaged twenty-two and a half between 1862 and 1880; that Indian barracks allowed at least fifteen hundred cubic feet of air per man, where the home standard had been four hundred and fifty in 1845 and six hundred from 1858; and that the share of troops posted to the hills had doubled from about twelve to about twenty-six per cent. Hot-climate rules in the empire were written in cubic feet of air per body. De Renzy also said that what was still killing the troops was the water supply, not the climate, which is a corrective from the period's own leading sanitary reformer to any account that credits verandas and ventilation with the fall. The same devices were restated by settlers and by the enslaved, in the Queenslander on stumps, the Cape house with its stoep, whose one verified account says nothing of the enslaved Southeast Asian and African craftsmen who built it, and the shotgun house of the Gulf South, Haitian and West African knowledge carried by Black builders, whose origin three scholars date three different ways and this chapter resolves none of.

Does the colonial rule work? The modern tests pull two ways. A simulation of seven traditional houses on Hainan found that deepening a veranda from one and a half metres to three improved comfort on the first floor by nine point three per cent, that wrapping four sides rather than one cut the predicted mean vote by a quarter of a point, and that the air was cooler by between a fifth of a degree and most of one. But field measurement in Malaysia found the elevated, lightweight, veranda-and-window Malay house running one to two degrees Celsius warmer inside than outside and beyond the comfort limit forty-seven per cent of the time, while heavy courtyard shophouses ran five degrees below the outdoor peak. And a ten-day campaign in three naturally ventilated Malaysian homes in 2026 found that what pushes such a room out of comfort is low air speed and high radiant temperature, not air temperature. So the colonial repertoire, tall rooms, shaded openings, cross-ventilation and above all the punkah, was aimed at the variable modern measurement says is binding, which is a defensible judgement about the devices and no defence of the system that operated them; and the raised lightweight house is a comfort strategy, not a cooling one.

On the book's measurement line this chapter sits in the first band, the one where the number was never made, but at that band's far edge. Everything before it in the series is ideas without numbers. Here the thermometer, the ledger, the specification, the mortality return and the tracer gas all appear, and each of them records something the building then ignored. The Crystal Palace was read hourly and rebuilt without its shading. Monticello's loggia was read at twenty-one degrees and became a sitting room. The sanatorium's five-year mortality was seventy-six per cent and the porch outlived the cure. The ventilation numbers rose after the theory that justified them collapsed. The housing laws wrote geometry and nobody measured what it delivered. This is the moment measurement begins to be taken and is not yet heeded, and it is also the moment the modern enclosure begins, because the best measurement of a Victorian glasshouse in existence today is a consultant's benchmark no one is allowed to read.

What carries forward is this. The glasshouse is the first manufactured interior climate, and its setpoint is written by a plant: twenty-six to thirty-two degrees for a lily, eighteen to twenty-eight and above seventy-five per cent humidity for the palms, above ten for the temperate house, a closed loop that survived a forty-seven-degree voyage in a box. The threshold acquired a specification, in the cure porch's proportion and its sill-less door, in the veranda's four functions and its line of separation, in the barrack's fifteen hundred cubic feet of air a man. The number in the law was geometric, not photometric, and was never tested, and the first number for daylight came only when a court asked. The park is a machine before it is a picture, and when the machine stops the picture stays. Nature as the source of form sorts into three modes that are available at once, not in sequence, and only once in the period, in a shed at Santa Coloma with a net of cords and a mirror, does nature do the designing rather than the decorating. And where this chapter is thinnest is where it matters most: the reformers' own books, Nightingale's, Laugier's, Trotter's, and a report on a school in a wood, were found and could not be read, so the figures that reached these pages are second-hand, and the first job of the verification pass that follows is to open them.
