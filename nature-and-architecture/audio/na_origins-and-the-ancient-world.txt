This is the first chapter of a series about how people have brought nature into buildings. The series runs from the deep past to the present and then looks ahead, and it asks one question all the way through: how has nature been brought inside, outside and at the threshold of buildings, and what does that record actually establish for an architect deciding what to do now. This chapter covers the longest stretch of time and the thinnest evidence, from the first decorated caves to roughly the year five hundred.

One idea runs through the whole series. There is a spectrum of ways a building can relate to nature. At one end nature is an image: an ornament, a symbol, a painting of a garden on a wall. Then nature is a form, a shape borrowed from a plant or a body. Then nature is a model, a structure or a process imitated. Then nature is material: timber, earth, stone, fibre, bone, living matter. Then nature is climate: sun, wind, water and cold, read and answered by the building. Then nature is a system: water, plants, soil and habitat made into working parts of the building. And at the far end nature is a partner, where the building and the living world give something back to each other. The most important thing to say at the start is that this spectrum is not a ladder of progress. The ancient world does not sit at the image end waiting for later centuries to reach integration. It sits everywhere on the spectrum at once, and in this chapter the integrated end is often the older one.

Here is the honest limit before anything else. For not one dwelling in this whole period does an instrumented thermal measurement exist in anything the research could reach. Nobody has put a sensor in a reconstructed Neolithic house through a winter and published the numbers. So every claim you will hear that a house was warm because it was sunk into the ground, or because its walls were packed in refuse, or because cattle stood at one end, is an inference or a tradition, not a measurement. Where a number does exist, this narrative says who measured it. Where it does not, the narrative says so.

Let us begin inside, in the dark. The oldest surviving interiors that people treated as a field for images of nature are caves. At Chauvet in the south of France, Anita Quiles and her colleagues assembled two hundred and fifty-nine radiocarbon dates and modelled two phases of human presence, one around thirty-seven to thirty-three thousand years ago and one around thirty-one to twenty-eight thousand years ago. What was painted there was animals: lions, horses and rhinoceros among them. The cave has no daylight beyond its mouth, so the first painted room in the record was a lightless one, made visible by fire. The dating is not uncontested. Paul Pettitt and Paul Bahn have argued on stylistic grounds for a later chronology, and both positions stand. At Lascaux the dates have moved with every campaign; the newest programme, reported by the French Ministry of Culture, puts the human presence at about twenty-one and a half thousand years ago, and the figures themselves are dated only by association with the charcoal and bone on the floor. There is a curious measured footnote to these caves. Bruno Fazenda and his colleagues measured the acoustics of five painted caves in northern Spain and found that motifs, especially lines and dots, are statistically more likely where the low frequencies resonate. They themselves call the association weak but tenable. So even at the very beginning, sound and image inside a natural enclosure may be linked, and the people who measured it are the first to say how thin the thread is.

The first built interiors are made of the site itself. On the shore of the Sea of Galilee, at a camp called Ohalo II that is now under water, Dani Nadel and his colleagues excavated hut floors about twenty-three thousand years old. On one floor they found bedding, bunches of grass laid in a repeated pattern around a central hearth and close to the walls, covered with a thin skin of clay. The hut was branches; the bed was grass; the plan was already three zones, a fire in the middle, working spots, and a sleeping band at the edge, which puts the warmest and the most sheltered places together. On the cold steppe of Ukraine, where there were no trees, people built with the largest animal on the landscape. At Mezhyrich and its neighbours, dated by Lyudmila Iakovleva to about fifteen thousand years ago, mammoth skulls, jaws and long bones were arranged into circular walls, and the jaws and long bones were sorted into lines, chevrons and zigzags. Structure and ornament coincide: the wall is patterned from the inside as well as the outside, and whether every ring was roofed is still questioned for the largest example.

Then come the first monumental rooms, and they are ringed by animals. At Göbekli Tepe in south-east Anatolia the enclosures of T-shaped pillars carry the densest programme of carved animals of any early Holocene building, foxes, boars, snakes and birds among them. Klaus Schmidt, who excavated it, read the enclosures as unroofed sanctuaries built by hunter-gatherers before farming. Edward Banning answered that they may have been roofed, symbol-rich houses, that the pillars stand where roof posts would stand, and that separating sacred from ordinary space is a modern assumption. A later study by Laura Dietrich and colleagues counted nearly three thousand four hundred grinding handstones on the site, which puts food processing there at scale. Temple or house, the same stones support both readings, and that is itself the lesson: nature was carved into the first great rooms before the categories we would use for them existed.

Çatalhöyük, on the Konya plain, is the fullest picture of a Neolithic house we have. Ian Hodder's project describes a settlement of mud-brick houses built against one another for mutual support, entered by ladder through a hole in the roof, so that the roofs were the streets. The hearth and oven sat under that hole, against the south wall, so the smoke left the way the people came in. The south end of the room was the dirty end with ashy floors; the north end had raised, whiter, often replastered platforms, with the burials beneath them. In one building the project counted more than four hundred and fifty thin layers of white plaster on the main room's walls. And the images of nature, the bull skulls with their horns, the paintings of vultures and leopards, cluster on the north and east walls, the walls farthest from the fire. The project built an experimental house with local builders in the late nineteen-nineties. They report that by day enough light comes through the ladder hole to make a white-plastered room quite bright. But the excavated houses have thick soot layers, and carbon residues on the ribs of elderly skeletons point to long indoor smoke, especially in winter. That skeletal evidence is the best measured indicator of interior air quality in this whole chapter. One painting from Çatalhöyük has become famous as the world's first map, a town plan under a twin-peaked erupting volcano. James Mellaart, the first excavator, read it that way, though his own first note had called it a leopard skin over a geometric panel. Stephanie Meece argued in two thousand and six that the leopard skin was the better reading and that a Neolithic village had no cartographic conventions to draw a map with. Then Axel Schmitt and colleagues dated a pumice from the nearby volcano Hasan Dağı to about nine thousand years ago, which makes an eruption and the painting compatible in time. Three readings, one wall; the chapter keeps all three and resolves none.

Now the cold, because cold climates are under-documented in this whole subject and this chapter tries to correct that. The common answer to cold, across unconnected cultures, was to go down into the ground. Patricia Gilman compared pit structures across a worldwide ethnographic sample and found that they go with two conditions: cold winters and a settlement pattern that stores food and stays put through the winter. Every ethnographer in her sample gave the same reason, warmth. That is the strongest cross-cultural statement available, and it rests on what people said, not on a thermometer. The cases fit it. At Sannai-Maruyama in northern Japan, in a snow-belt climate, the site's official account counts about seven hundred pit dwellings, and the largest reconstructed building is a pit-house thirty-two metres long and nearly ten wide, a roof set over a hole, made long rather than tall. At Banpo and Jiangzhai in the loess country of the Wei valley the houses were sunk with central hearths; but the claim you will read everywhere, that their doors faced south to the sun, could not be traced to a primary source, and Yun Kuen Lee's analysis of Jiangzhai explains the doors by the central plaza they all face, not by the sun. At Skara Brae in Orkney, the best-preserved Neolithic interior in northern Europe, the stone houses with their stone dressers and box beds are set into great mounds of midden. That the midden insulated them is an inference repeated from Gordon Childe's excavations onward; the Historic Environment Scotland account is silent on the reason, and Sigurd Towrie's account describes the embedding as a cumulative process of rebuilding into accumulated refuse. No roof survives, so the room and its furniture are measured and the envelope's thermal behaviour is not. The brochs of Iron Age Scotland, of which Mousa is the tallest at about thirteen metres by the UNESCO tentative list's account, have walls five metres thick at the base built as two concentric skins with cells and a stair between them; the sources that describe the building do not say what the hollow wall was for, and structural, circulation and insulation explanations are all inferences. In the Arctic before the year five hundred, the Dorset people's excavated interiors are floors: an axial hearth line, a stone sleeping platform, soapstone lamp fragments, and an entrance passage that the excavator of Cape Ray called a perfect cold trap. In Norway, the Iron Age farm at Ullandhaug had timber-framed houses with outer stone walls against rain and wind, probably turf roofs, and one hall with six fireplaces. The belief that the cattle stalled at one end warmed the people at the other is older than any measurement of it, and Haio Zimmermann's study weighed manure, control and protection as the likelier reasons for stalling.

Orientation in the cold has its own dispute. The first farmers of central Europe built long timber houses all pointing roughly the same way, north-west to south-east. Was it the wind, as Marshall proposed? Was it memory, a symbolic pointing back toward the region the settlers had come from, as Richard Bradley argued? Nils Müller-Scheeßel and his colleagues measured three hundred and thirteen houses at one site in Slovakia by magnetometry, with twenty-four of them radiocarbon dated, and found that the orientation rotated steadily counter-clockwise, about five degrees a century. They reject wind, memory and sun, and propose that builders lining up new houses against old ones by eye drifted left because of a known bias in perception. Four explanations, one pattern.

Interior sound is the sense that histories of this subject almost never measure, so it deserves its own place. The Ħal Saflieni Hypogeum in Malta is a burial complex on three levels cut into soft limestone, its ceilings carved to imitate corbelled masonry. Two independent teams have measured its so-called Oracle Room: Paolo Debertolis and colleagues recorded a double resonance near seventy and one hundred and fourteen hertz, and Rupert Till's impulse-response survey found resonant behaviour around one hundred and ten to one hundred and fourteen hertz and near seventy. That much is measured. The claim that these frequencies were chosen to alter the brain rests on a laboratory electroencephalogram study by Ian Cook and colleagues that was not conducted in the Hypogeum or in any ancient building, and Till leaves design intent open. Stonehenge, an unroofed ring, was tested at one-twelfth scale by Trevor Cox, Bruno Fazenda and Susan Greaney: with all the stones in place the reverberation was about two-thirds of a second, speech inside was raised by about four decibels, there were no echoes, and listeners outside got three to seven decibels less than listeners inside. An open ring of stone behaves like a small room for anyone within it and like open ground for anyone outside. And at Chavín de Huántar in the Peruvian highlands, Miriam Kolar's team measured the underground galleries and the three small ducts that connect the chamber of the great carved stone called the Lanzón to the sunken circular plaza outside. The central duct emphasises frequencies around nine hundred hertz, the third harmonic of the conch-shell horns found there in a cache of twenty; low frequencies pass through all the ducts; the higher frequencies that carry the clarity of speech are cut. The horns themselves sound around three hundred hertz and were measured at about ninety-six decibels a metre away. So a windowless stone interior can be tuned, by corridor proportion and by small ducts to a court, so that one instrument's harmonics pass and speech does not. That is measured. The far more famous claim about Chavín, that water rushing through the canals beneath the plazas made the temple roar like pumas, is a hypothesis from nineteen seventy-six with no published acoustic measurement behind it, and the newest excavation reading of the canals is drainage, ritual water and possibly supply. Two senses of the same building, at two different grades of evidence. In India the polished granite caves at Barabar are said in every guidebook to hold a long echo, and no peer-reviewed measurement of it exists at all.

Now to the great river civilisations, where nature enters the building as form, image and system together. At Karnak the Great Hypostyle Hall holds, by the count of the University of Memphis project that documents it, one hundred and thirty-four sandstone columns: twelve open-flower papyrus columns along a raised central axis and one hundred and twenty-two closed-bud columns in the flanks. The columns are plants in stone; that much is directly observable. The taller nave leaves a clerestory of stone grilles above the aisles, and the roof elsewhere had only a few small holes, so the hall was deliberately dim; the project describes the light as dawn-like. The reading that the whole hall is the primeval marsh from which creation rose is a scholarly reconstruction that professional Egyptological outlets state with hedges, and no source reached here treats it as proven. So the plant is present three ways at once, as form, as light and as an image, and the evidence supports the first firmly, the third physically and the second as a careful reading. Inside Egyptian houses the marsh was painted. In the tomb-chapel of Nebamun, now in the British Museum, a garden with a rectangular pool of fish and waterfowl, hedged by date palms and sycomores, is drawn in plan and elevation at once. At Amarna the so-called Green Room of the North Palace carried a continuous painted riverbank of papyrus and lotus full of birds, with niches treated as little ponds and a garden window, and a recent study in the journal Antiquity identified the birds species by species. A model from the tomb of Meketre, in the Metropolitan Museum, shows the threshold in miniature: a columned porch with papyrus and lotus columns opening straight onto a copper-lined pool ringed by fruiting sycomore figs. As for the story that the earliest windcatcher appears on a painting of the house of a man named Neb-Amun, the research could not close that attribution chain: Hassan Fathy makes the claim, he relies on Alexander Badawy's paywalled article, the dynasty and date he gives do not agree with each other, and no source reached names the tomb. Egyptian houses likely had roof air scoops; the famous first picture of one remains unverified.

Mesopotamia gives us the courtyard house and the royal garden. At Ur, Leonard Woolley excavated houses of about four thousand years ago with a central court entered through a lobby, rooms around it, and brick paving laid to fall to a central drain. He inferred an upper storey with a wooden gallery, and he said openly that he did so by analogy with the modern Arab house. Jean-Claude Margueron has since suggested that the central space may sometimes have been covered rather than open, and whether the stairs led to an upper floor or only to a roof terrace remains hard to settle. So the earliest well-documented courtyard house already treated the court as drain, light and air at once, and whether it was open to the sky in every case is still an open question a century after excavation. The Assyrian kings are unusual because they left three matched records. Ashurnasirpal's inscription at Nimrud lists a canal cut through a mountain and orchards planted with more than thirty kinds of tree gathered on campaign. Sennacherib's aqueduct at Jerwan still stands, a limestone canal on corbelled arches, and Frederick Mario Fales and Roswitha Del Fabbro re-collated about two hundred of its inscribed blocks in twenty fourteen; the texts say the water served Nineveh's orchards and gardens. And Ashurbanipal's relief in the British Museum shows a watered garden with an aqueduct, channels, trees and a pavilion. Intention, infrastructure and image can be compared. That brings us to the Hanging Gardens. The classical writers put them at Babylon; Diodorus describes terraces on vaulted galleries with stone beams, reeds in bitumen, brick, lead sheeting and deep earth for trees, and machines raising water from the river. Robert Koldewey's excavators found vaults at Babylon that are now read as storerooms. Stephanie Dalley argues that the wonder was Sennacherib's garden at Nineveh, misattributed because classical writers sometimes called Nineveh Babylon, and that Nebuchadnezzar's abundant building texts never mention a garden. Ariel Bagg's review accepts the silence at Babylon but rejects Dalley's reading of the water-raising screw, points out that every classical author says Babylon, and concludes the problem is unsolved. Four positions coexist and this series adopts none. What is secure is the type the story describes, irrigated planting on a built and waterproofed structure imitating a mountain, and that type is independently attested at Nineveh in Sennacherib's text and his grandson's relief.

Persia gives the paradise. At Pasargadae, David Stronach found stone water channels with basins defining two rectangles in front of the palace, with a throne seat set in the palace's garden portico so that the king looked down an open vista through the planting. Stronach read the plan as four quarters and called it the first chahar bagh, and UNESCO adopted that reading in its listing. The Iranian and French geophysical survey found something larger and stranger: a grid of squares thirty to sixty metres on a side, probably channels lined by rows of trees, a shallow pond about two hundred and fifty metres long, and canals running up to thirty kilometres from the river. Whether the fourfold garden was born here is a reading imposed on two rectangles of channels, and the survey shows a hydraulic landscape that the four-garden diagram does not describe. The word for these walled parks, pairidaeza, passed through Greek as paradeisos and became our paradise; Xenophon has Socrates describe the Persian king's parks and Lysander admiring trees planted in straight rows at Sardis. Beneath the plateau ran the qanat, a gently sloping tunnel from a mother well, ventilated by a line of shafts, delivering groundwater by gravity to an outlet. A twenty twenty-three review in a Nature journal compiles the measured ranges: mother wells ten to two hundred and fifty metres deep, gradients of a few parts per thousand, lengths from a few hundred metres to tens of kilometres. The form is measurable everywhere and the origin is unmeasurable anywhere. The eighth-century Assyrian text long read as the first description of a qanat has been re-read as not describing one; the earliest excavated galleries, at al-Madam in Sharjah, are Arabian and shallow and date to the Iron Age around a thousand years before our era; the deep Iranian form is later; and Rémy Boucharlat's model is polycentric, several inventions in several places rather than one.

The Greeks wrote the first rules, and they wrote them as medicine. The Hippocratic treatise on airs, waters and places tells a physician arriving in a new city to examine its exposure to sun and wind and the quality of its water, because these decide its diseases. It ranks cities facing the rising sun as healthiest and cities facing the sunset as worst, with mists in the morning and scorching in the afternoon. Xenophon has Socrates say that in a house facing south the winter sun enters the porticoes while the high summer sun passes overhead, so the south side should be built taller and the north side lower to keep out the cold winds. None of this was measured. But at Olynthus, a planned city in northern Greece laid out around four hundred and thirty and destroyed in three hundred and forty-eight, the excavated houses have their open court on the south side, a roofed portico called the pastas along the court's north edge, and the main rooms behind it opening south, with a second storey over them. Fitzjohn and Wilson modelled such a house in twenty twenty-five and ran the sun through it. At the winter solstice only south-facing courts received any direct sun at all, and the south-facing single-storey model had about nineteen percent of its court sunlit at midday. At the summer solstice the pastas raised the shaded fraction of the court at one in the afternoon to about sixty percent, but an east-facing court was better shaded at midday than a south-facing one. Their conclusion is that the motive was winter sun, not summer shade, which is the opposite of the way the popular story is usually told. The popular story, from Ken Butti and John Perlin, calls Olynthus the planned solar city where every house had its sun; the excavation-based literature describes a dominant plan with variation, and the same authors' second study found that comfort differed from room to room and house to house within a block. So the plan matches the rule, the simulation confirms the winter half, and the uniformity claim overstates. The healing sanctuaries followed the same logic. Vitruvius states as a rule that temples of the healing gods should be sited in the healthiest places with good springs, because the sick recover faster there; Kos began as a cypress grove with springs, Pergamon's sanctuary sits in a spring-fed basin, and Epidaurus combined sleep, diet, bathing and exercise. That these places were sited in nature is documented; that people were healed by nature is a modern characterisation, and a twenty twenty-four study argues that the role of dream-incubation has been overstated.

Vitruvius gathered all of it into a code around the year twenty before our era: healthy sites, streets angled to break the winds, the origin of building in huts imitating swallows' nests, baths lit from the winter sunset because bathing ran from noon to evening, and a room-by-room orientation table, winter dining rooms to the south-west, bedrooms and libraries east against damp and moths, summer rooms and painters' studios north for steady light, wine cellars north, oil rooms south. It is a guideline of its time, not a measurement, and the fit between his rules and excavated buildings is a separate question. In one case that fit has been tested with physics. James Ring re-examined a room in the Forum Baths at Ostia with a south-facing window of about fifty-two square metres, and ran a winter heat balance. Unglazed, the window lost about ten times what the sun brought in; glazed, the room ran at a small surplus on a sunny winter afternoon, with the thick suspended floor storing the heat overnight. Glazing was indispensable, and cast glass panes are indeed found chiefly in bath buildings at Pompeii and Herculaneum, while ordinary houses used cloth or skins. Vitruvius told the same story as a rule; Ring told it as a heat balance, and he did not cite Vitruvius. Vitruvius also tells the legend of the Corinthian capital, an acanthus growing round a basket on a girl's grave at Corinth, seen by the Athenian craftsman Callimachus. The earliest surviving Corinthian capital is not at Corinth or Athens but at Bassae in the Arcadian mountains, decades earlier, a single interior column lit by a tall opening in the side of the sanctuary. Joseph Rykwert read the legend as a myth about death and vegetative rebirth, and the physical and the narrative evidence do not meet in place or in date.

Roman houses are rain machines. Vitruvius classifies atria by how their roofs handle water: sloping inward to the central opening, or outward. At Herculaneum, Gemma Jansen documented the chain: the roof delivers rain through spouts into the basin, the basin feeds a cistern about three and a half metres deep with waterproofed masonry, a covered well-head keeps the stored water from sun and dirt, and overflow runs to the street. The gardens of Pompeii are the best-measured ancient plantings anywhere, because Wilhelmina Jashemski filled the cavities left by decayed roots in the volcanic fill with plaster and cast them in place. In the large vineyard opposite the amphitheatre her team recorded two thousand and one vine-root cavities planted at almost exactly four Roman feet, the spacing Columella gives for hand cultivation, plus fifty-seven trees. Productive nature stood inside the city block and inside the house walls. Where the garden could not be real it was painted. The garden room of the Villa of Livia at Prima Porta, of about thirty to twenty before our era, was partly underground with a vaulted ceiling, the kind of room used in summer heat, and its walls carry an imaginary garden beyond a low fence where plants of every season fruit and flower at once. The sunken room gets a view it could not have. At the House of the Golden Bracelet in Pompeii the painted garden adjoins a real one with a fountain of twenty-eight jets and vine roots cast along the walls, so image and system share one suite. The Pantheon admits sky, sun and rain through a single opening, about eight metres across by Robert Hannah and Giulio Magli's figure, and disposes of the rain through a slightly convex floor with drains. Hannah and Magli argue that the disc of sunlight was set to strike the cornice at the equinoxes and to fill the doorway around the twenty-first of April, Rome's traditional birthday; their own caveat is that the building is not a precision instrument, and the objection recorded here is that any hemisphere with an oculus at Rome's latitude produces the equinox effect regardless of intent. And Pliny the Younger's letters describing his two villas are the fullest ancient account of rooms placed by the hour of the sun, by the wind they catch or avoid, by the view they frame and the sound they admit: a sun-room, a glazed alcove, a bedroom heated by a hypocaust, another shut against the sea's noise and the daylight, a covered gallery that keeps its own cool in summer, a fountain heard from a bed, and a room roofed entirely by a vine so dense that it is like sitting in a grove and yet dry in rain. It is an owner's account with no surviving plan to test it against.

South Asia begins with water as the plan of the city. At Mohenjo-daro the Great Bath is, by Jonathan Mark Kenoyer's account, a sunken brick tank about twelve metres by seven and about two and a half deep, waterproofed with bitumen, with an outlet into a corbelled drain. Michael Jansen estimated more than seven hundred wells across the city by projecting the density of the excavated areas, and that figure is an estimate, not a census. Wells, bathing floors and drains were distributed as densely as houses. Three centuries before our era the emperor Ashoka had it carved on rock and pillar that banyan trees had been planted along the roads for the shade of cattle and people, that mango groves had been planted, that wells had been dug every eight kos with steps, and that rest houses and drinking places had been made for animals and people alike. Vegetation here is public equipment with a service interval, and the king adds that these are small things beside righteousness. In the same century the Barabar caves were cut into granite and polished to a mirror finish, and the front of the Lomas Rishi cave carves a timber building into the rock, a curved architrave and lattice in stone. At Karla, two or three centuries later, the relationship inverts: real teak ribs survive in the stone vault of a hall that no longer needs them. Stone made to look like timber, and timber kept inside stone; the imitation of nature here is an imitation of construction. Ajanta's chaitya halls take their light through one great horseshoe window over the door, and no illuminance study of any of these interiors was found. The most radical Indian building type of the period has a living organism at its centre. Ananda Coomaraswamy showed that the Buddhist bodhi-ghara, a railed and pillared pavilion built around a sacred tree with the crown rising free above the roof, took over an older cult of tree and altar, and the reliefs at Bharhut and Sanchi show the type. The same logic at landscape scale keeps the sacred groves of the Western Ghats intact, relic forest preserved by village communities on religious grounds, documented by Madhav Gadgil and V. D. Vartak, of undated but ancient origin and still in use. And at the very end of the chapter's window, Sigiriya in Sri Lanka, built between four seventy-seven and four ninety-five, lays out water gardens on a surveyed axis, a fourfold garden with an island, a fountain garden fed through underground terracotta pipes and rock-cut drains from a reservoir, and the Central Cultural Fund confirms that the fountains still run in the rainy season, fifteen centuries on. Symmetry organises the water gardens; asymmetry follows the boulders; the same buried conduits join them.

China's earliest siting text is a poem. In the ode of Duke Liu in the Book of Songs, the leader climbs a ridge, looks over the plain, reads the sunlit and the shaded sides of the hills and where the streams flow, takes bearings by the sun's shadow, and only then settles his people. The Kaogongji later makes it a rule: level the ground with water, set a gnomon, mark the sunrise and sunset shadows, check by the noon shadow and the pole star, and lay out the capital as a nine-li square with the temple to the left and the altar of soil to the right. Orientation is derived from sky and water before any wall is drawn. At Fengchu, excavated in nineteen seventy-six, the earliest complete courtyard compound in China stands on an axis about ten degrees west of north; Yang Hongxun's account records that the ground was higher to the north, and the builders raised the south end so the platform stands about a metre above ground there and only twenty centimetres at the north. That is the one excavated fact with a climate in it, the front court on the sunny raised side; the screen wall, the two courts, the side rooms and the drains that every drawing shows are largely reconstruction. The Han emperors built nature as an image at reservoir scale. In one hundred and four before our era Emperor Wu built the Jianzhang Palace with the Taiye pool, holding three islands named for the immortal isles of the eastern sea. A twenty twenty-two study by Wang and Hein reports the surveyed pool at about four hundred and fifty by five hundred and ten metres, and shows that the great Kunming pool nearby, nominally for naval training, was the master reservoir regulating the capital's water. Representation and hydraulic system coincide in one body of water, and the pool-and-three-isles model lasted more than fifteen hundred years. The wind-and-water tradition has its founding text in the Book of Burial attributed to Guo Pu: qi scatters when it rides the wind and stops when it meets water, so the best site acquires water and the next shelters from wind, with hills behind. Whether the received text is fourth-century or a Song compilation is disputed. Whatever its date, the configuration it prefers, sheltered ground facing water with hills behind, is the one that shelters from cold wind and catches low sun in a temperate continental climate, and it states that as cosmology, not climate. This chapter records the coincidence without converting one into the other. In the far north-east, the heated floor begins. Song Ki-ho compiled ninety-two dwelling sites with five hundred and twenty-seven remains of partial flues, a line or two of heated channel along one wall, and placed their origin in the Iron Age of eastern Manchuria and the Russian Primorye around the second or first century before our era, with the kingdom of Goguryeo adopting them only in the late fourth century. Popular sources push the origin back thousands of years without published evidence. The earliest heated rooms had a thermal gradient, not a uniform floor. And at Toro in Japan, a first-century village lifted its granaries onto posts above the wet ground and sank its houses into it: opposite sections for grain and for people in the same humid climate.

The Americas close the chapter, with some of its best measurements. At Caral on the Peruvian coast, five thousand years old, Ruth Shady's excavators found seven fire-altar rooms, each a small circular chamber inside a square enclosure with a hearth and a buried duct running out through the pyramid, so that outdoor air reached an indoor fire in a nearly closed stone room. The bags of fibre filled with stones that make up the platform fill have been tested as seismic isolators in modern laboratories; whether the builders intended that is an inference on top of those tests. At Poverty Point in Louisiana, Anthony Ortmann and Tristram Kidder showed by micro-stratigraphy that the great Mound A went up in one short campaign with no pauses, and Kidder's new survey showed the earthworks far less symmetrical than the old maps, which removes the regular plan that the solstice-alignment claims relied on. At the Newark Octagon in Ohio, Ray Hively and Robert Horn's survey found the walls marking the extremes of moonrise and moonset over the eighteen-and-a-half-year lunar cycle, and their simulation of billions of random octagons put the chance of that at somewhere between one in a thousand and one in forty million depending on assumptions; Christopher Turner's critique found three of the seven proposed sightlines more than half a degree off and a far weaker combined probability. UNESCO adopted the lunar reading in twenty twenty-three; the dispute is live. At Teotihuacan, Ivan Šprajc measured the axis of the Pyramid of the Sun at fifteen degrees twenty-eight minutes east of north and read the east-west line as recording sunrises and sunsets on dates two hundred and sixty days apart, the length of the ritual calendar, with the mountain Cerro Gordo anchoring the north. That is the city's logic above. Below, more than two thousand walled apartment compounds, by Michael Smith and colleagues' count, housed perhaps a hundred thousand people in windowless rooms lit, aired and rained on through sunken patios drained by pipes beneath the floor. The two scales use different natural logics, calendar above and rain below. And in the Tepantitla compound a mural paints a mountain over a sea of shells and starfish, with streams and swimmers, the working hydrology of the region painted on the wall of a court that itself collects rain. Tikal in Guatemala had no river. Its plazas were plastered catchments and its reservoirs were tanks. Kenneth Tankersley and colleagues found in the Corriental reservoir a filter of zeolite and coarse quartz sand, identified by X-ray diffraction, matching a volcanic outcrop about thirty kilometres away, and six radiocarbon dates put its operation between about two hundred and thirty-five before our era and nine hundred and eighty-five of our era. David Lentz and colleagues found the central Palace and Temple reservoirs contaminated in the late period with mercury and toxic algal blooms, while Corriental stayed clean. A city can make drinking water a designed product, and the record can tell the filtered tank from the poisoned ones. In the Amazon, the dark earths called terra preta are measured facts, soils enriched in carbon, phosphorus and calcium, rich in pottery, on bluffs above the flood. Michael Heckenberger's work in the Upper Xingu was co-authored with Kuikuro community members, and Morgan Schmidt's twenty twenty-three study, also with Kuikuro collaborators, showed present-day villages making the same soils by the same means. Lucas Silva's team proposed in twenty twenty-one that the key ingredients arrived by river before farming; Umberto Lombardo's team answered in twenty twenty-two that the soils sit where rivers cannot reach and match ash and bone, not silt. That dispute stands. Fertile ground can be a made thing that outlives its makers by two thousand years, and it is the chapter's clearest case of nature as partner. In the Four Corners, the shallow Basketmaker houses before the year five hundred had hearths and storage pits but no ventilators; the fully developed earth-sheltered room, a duct in, a deflector slab, a hearth, a smoke hole out, dated by tree rings to the six hundred and seventies at Mesa Verde, comes just after this chapter ends. And on the north coast of Peru the Moche raised the Huaca del Sol from more than one hundred and forty-three million sun-dried bricks, by Hastings and Michael Moseley's count, made by communities who stamped them with their marks, and painted their plazas with deities, felines, fish and spiders.

So what does the record establish, and what does it not? It establishes that from the beginning nature came inside as image, in caves, on plaster, on the walls of summer dining rooms and of an apartment court. It establishes that builders read sun, wind and water with precision: the Greek physicians and Vitruvius as rules, the Chinese odes and the Kaogongji as procedure, Olynthus as a plan that a modern simulation confirms for winter, Teotihuacan and Newark as measured alignments still argued over. It establishes water as a working system at every scale, the Herculaneum cistern, the Indus wells, the Assyrian canal, the Persian qanat, the Sigiriya conduits, the Tikal filter. It establishes the threshold, the court open to the sky, at Ur, at Fengchu, at Olynthus, in the Roman atrium and the Teotihuacan patio, as the oldest and most widespread instrument for bringing light and air into rooms. And it establishes a few living partnerships, a building built around a tree, a grove kept by a village, a soil made by a people.

What it does not establish is performance. No ancient interior in this chapter has a measured temperature. The insulation of the midden, the warmth of the pit-house, the cooling of the courtyard, the heat of the cattle, are inferences and traditions, some of them very likely true, none of them measured for the period. The famous origin stories are, almost every one, disputed: where the Hanging Gardens were, whether the Pasargadae garden was fourfold, where the qanat was invented, whether Olynthus was a solar city, whether the Pantheon was a calendar, whether the Çatalhöyük painting is a map, whether the Newark walls mark the moon, whether the terra preta was made or deposited, whether the Nazca puquios are ancient or colonial. And one practical limit runs beneath this whole chapter: every photograph proposed for it comes from an open collection, but the licences could not be read from the collection pages during this research, so each one is marked to be confirmed before it is used.

The lesson for now is simple and a little uncomfortable. The integrated end of the spectrum is ancient. Courtyards, sunken patios, ducted hearths, filtered reservoirs, heated floors, planted mounds, and rooms placed by the hour of the sun were all in use before the year five hundred. What the ancient world lacked was not the ideas but the measurement, and that is exactly what later chapters will have to supply.
