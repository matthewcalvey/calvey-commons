This is the sixth chapter of a series about how people have brought nature into buildings. The series runs from the deep past to the present and then looks ahead, and it asks one question all the way through: how has nature been brought inside, outside and at the threshold of buildings, and what does that record actually establish for an architect deciding what to do now. This chapter covers the shortest stretch of time and the thickest evidence, from the year two thousand to today, and it is the first chapter in the series whose subject is a field that has audited itself.

One idea runs through the whole series, and it is worth restating briefly. There is a spectrum of ways a building can relate to nature. At one end nature is an image, a picture of a leaf, a framed view. Then nature is a form, a shape borrowed. Then a model, a structure or process imitated. Then a material, living or once-living matter as the substance of the building. Then a climate, the building shaped by sun, wind and water. Then a system, the building as a working part of an ecological or hydrological cycle. And at the far end nature is a partner, the living world as a collaborator with its own agency. The spectrum is not a ladder of progress, and in this chapter it does something the earlier chapters did not: it splits. The evidence for nature in architecture gathers at one end and the claims gather at the other, and the two curves cross, as we shall see, somewhere near the window.

Here is the honest limit before anything else, and in this chapter it is a different limit from the one the earlier chapters declared. It is not that nobody measured. The period measured more than any before it. The limit is that the measurements were very often taken on the wrong object, at the wrong scale, or in a form that cannot be read back. This research ran without web search, so what it could reach it reached through bibliographic indexes and open repositories, and where it could not open a source it says so and does not fill the gap from memory. Every figure in this chapter is a drawing to be made rather than a photograph, because the image collections were out of reach and because, from here onward, a drawing that puts two datasets side by side is worth more than a picture of a façade.

Let us begin with the framework a practising architect is most likely to have actually read. In twenty fourteen a New York consultancy called Terrapin Bright Green published a report by William Browning, Catherine Ryan and Joseph Clancy that reduced Stephen Kellert's seventy-odd biophilic mechanisms to fourteen patterns in three groups, and did something unusually honest with them. It graded each pattern from zero to three asterisks for the quality of the peer-reviewed evidence behind it, and it defined the scale. Three asterisks means robust evidence with great potential for impact. No asterisk means minimal research and only anecdote adequate for hypothesising an effect. Three patterns received three asterisks: a visual connection with nature, prospect, and refuge. Now read those carefully. Prospect is an unimpeded view over a distance. Refuge is a protected place to withdraw to. Both were argued into architecture from landscape aesthetics, and both can be satisfied in a windowless room with a long internal sightline and an alcove. So exactly one of the fourteen patterns carries the framework's own top grade while actually requiring nature to be present, and that one is the visual connection, which rests on Roger Ulrich's nineteen eighty-four hospital study of twenty-three matched pairs of patients that the previous chapter weighed. At the other end of the scale three patterns received no asterisks at all: a material connection with nature, a connection with natural systems, and risk. In the language of this series those are the material and the system modes. The framework's own authors, having mined more than five hundred publications, could not find robust support for the proposition that building out of natural material, or connecting a building to a working natural system, does anyone measurable good. There is no pattern at all for partnership. The previous chapter inferred all this from Ulrich alone. Terrapin, working forward from five hundred publications, arrived at the same distribution and printed it in a table, and the profession has adopted the report's summary claim and ignored its grading. One more thing about that document, because it matters in practice. It contains no fractal dimensions, no view depths, no sightline distances and no spatial ratios. If you have been told that the fourteen patterns specify any such number, the attribution is wrong.

Now the strongest evidence in the whole biophilic field, which is not about buildings. In twenty nineteen Rojas-Rueda and colleagues pooled nine long-term cohorts covering eight million, three hundred and twenty-four thousand, six hundred and fifty-two people in seven countries and found that for every tenth of a point increase in a satellite greenness index within five hundred metres of home, the risk of dying fell by about four per cent. It is prospective, enormous, checked for publication bias, and it is evidence about the vegetation within half a kilometre of where you live, measured from orbit. Its heterogeneity is ninety-five per cent, which means the nine cohorts were not measuring one common effect, and greener neighbourhoods are in most of those countries wealthier and quieter ones. It cannot license a living wall or a lobby planter or a timber soffit. It is an argument about where to put housing and how much vegetation to put around it, and it is used constantly to sell interiors. In the same year Mathew White and colleagues at Exeter analysed nineteen thousand, eight hundred and six respondents to an English survey and found that people who spent at least a hundred and twenty minutes a week in nature had fifty-nine per cent higher odds of reporting good health, with the benefit peaking at three to five hours and flat beyond. The threshold held for men and women, old and young, urban and rural, deprived and comfortable, and, importantly, for people with a long-term illness. It did not hold among respondents who were not white British. And its definition of nature excluded time spent in private gardens. If a garden does not count, an atrium does not count either, and the two-hours figure has nonetheless migrated into presentations as a specification for interiors.

The plants themselves are the field's most vulnerable claim, and the arithmetic is now published. The story that potted plants clean indoor air descends from chamber experiments at a space agency's Stennis Center in nineteen eighty-nine. In twenty nineteen Bryan Cummings and Michael Waring took not that one study but twelve chamber studies and a hundred and ninety-six results and converted them all into a single metric, the clean air delivery rate, so that a plant could be set beside a window, a fan and a filter on one axis. The median plant delivers twenty-three thousandths of a cubic metre of clean air an hour. To match the ordinary air exchange of a room, about one change an hour, you would need between ten and a thousand plants for every square metre of floor. The claim is not marginally wrong; it is three orders of magnitude wrong. An engineered biofilter that forces air through a root zone is a different device and was not refuted. A ficus in a lobby, cited for air quality, was. The psychological claim for plants is a separate and much smaller literature. A twenty twenty-two meta-analysis found only two or three studies per outcome, a small fall in diastolic blood pressure, a moderate effect on academic achievement in students, and nothing significant on attention or reaction time, which are the outcomes a designer wants. The air-quality literature is old, large and refuted; the psychological one is small, recent and mostly null on cognition; and the marketing claim takes the size of the first and the partial survival of the second and presents their union as their intersection.

The window is the one interior move the evidence supports without heavy qualification, and even there the evidence has a shape you should know. The most quoted recent study of daylight and views put thirty office workers in Durham, North Carolina, for a week each in two rooms, one glazed with electrochromic glass and one with blinds at one and a half per cent transmittance pulled three-quarters down, and measured their sleep with wrist sensors and their cognition with an eighty-three-minute simulation. They slept thirty-seven minutes longer in the glass room and scored forty-two per cent higher. The study was funded by the company that makes the glass, four of its seven authors work for that company, the control room was close to blacked out, the cognitive gain rose from about seven per cent on the Monday to about seventy-nine on the Friday in a way that practice on the test would also produce, and the authors themselves state that they cannot tell whether the benefit came from the daylight or from the view, because the two are usually perfectly correlated. That last admission is not a flaw of one study. It is a property of windows, and it is exactly the distinction an architect needs, because one half is solved by a rooflight and the other half is not.

What about representations, which is what most biophilic interventions are? In twenty twenty Matthew Browning and colleagues ran the cleanest experiment in the field. Eighty-nine undergraduates sat for six minutes either in a real forest, or in a headset showing a three-hundred-and-sixty-degree video of the same forest, or facing a blank white wall. Positive mood rose in the forest, held level in the headset, and fell sharply at the wall. Negative mood fell everywhere. Representation, on this evidence, is a preservative and not a restorative. It stops the decline that a blank enclosure produces; it does not produce the gain that being outside produces. That gives nature imagery a genuine job in a windowless corridor or a basement or a night-shift station, and no job at all as a substitute for a window that could have been provided. The study had no video-without-nature control, so novelty and nature are still entangled, and sixty per cent of those students had never worn a headset before. Forest bathing, the Japanese practice promoted by the Forestry Agency since nineteen eighty-two and converted into measured physiology by Yoshifumi Miyazaki, Qing Li, Bum-Jin Park and their colleagues, supplies many of the numbers biophilic design repeats indoors. A meta-analysis of twenty trials and two thousand, two hundred and fifty-seven participants found ninety per cent of them from Asia and none from Africa, Oceania or the Americas, pooled exposures from fifteen minutes to nine days, and its pooled effect sizes could not be read by this research and are not supplied. Whether the forest does the work, or the walk, or the company, or the belief, is not established. And underneath all of it sit two theories, attention restoration from Rachel and Stephen Kaplan and stress reduction from Ulrich, which make dissociable predictions that a simple experiment could separate, and Berto's review finds no study that has run it. The complaint is internal: Terry Hartig, one of the field's founders, wrote to contest how its meta-analysis was done.

So take an inventory of what has actually been measured inside a building in this whole literature. Plants against volatile organics in sealed chambers, refuted. Plants against human function, two or three studies per outcome. Glass against blinds, thirty people, manufacturer-funded. A headset against a wall, eighty-nine students for six minutes. Wood against concrete, twenty people. Fractal preference on screens, no architectural surface ever tested. The largest sample is eighty-nine. The longest controlled exposure is a week. The most robust finding is a negative one. Against that, eight million people outdoors. The ratio is about a hundred thousand to one, and the profession's design attention runs the other way. An adequate interior study would have to separate daylight from view, include a non-nature control matched for novelty, run long enough for practice effects to settle, sample beyond undergraduates, measure both stress physiology and attention in the same people so the two theories could be told apart, and describe its nature precisely enough for another building to reproduce it. No study reached does more than two of those six things.

Biomimicry, the second field of the period, splits along a different line, the line between the laboratory and the building. At the scale of materials and organisms the measurement is often superb and often locked away. At the scale of buildings it does not exist. Two independent searches of the bibliographic record, forty records, found no instrumented performance study of any completed biomimetic building anywhere, and the literature on termite-inspired buildings is simulation without exception. The building at the head of that literature is the Eastgate Centre in Harare, always shown with a percentage of energy saved attached, and no published measurement of it could be reached. Its completion date, conventionally nineteen ninety-six, its architect and its engineer were not verified from any source, and if the date holds then the building was finished before Benyus named the field in nineteen ninety-seven, which would make it not the founding example but the founding retrospective attribution.

The mound it was modelled on has meanwhile been measured, and the measurement destroys the analogy three times over. The story every lecture tells is that the mound is a chimney driven by the colony's metabolic heat, holding the nest at a constant temperature. J. Scott Turner reframed it in two thousand and one as an organ of respiratory gas exchange, and in twenty seventeen Samuel Ocko, Hunter King and colleagues, with Turner and Rupert Soar as co-authors, instrumented about thirty mounds in Namibia with hot-bead anemometers in the conduits, temperature buttons on the flanks, carbon dioxide sampling every fifteen minutes and a wind vane three metres away. The flow in the conduits reverses twice a day, downward at night and upward by day. It is driven by the sun warming different faces of the mound at different hours, not by the insects' heat; wind matters only above about three metres a second. And the carbon dioxide inside is held at about five per cent, all day, which is two orders of magnitude above anything a person could breathe. The mound is not a ventilated space in any sense a building code means. It is a chamber whose structure keeps a lethal concentration stable. There are three positions in the record on what drives it, the metabolic chimney, transient wind, and diurnal radiant heating, and the second and third are both Turner's; the biology revised itself twice while architecture went on citing the account all three had rejected. The other founding stories fare no better. The engineering literature on the bullet train's tunnel boom contains no kingfisher; it contains acoustic prediction, optimisation of the nose's area distribution, genetic-algorithm searches checked on moving-model rigs, and remedies, tunnel hoods and resonators and active control, that have nothing to do with the train's nose at all. The boxfish that inspired a concept car turns out, in twenty twenty, to be inherently unstable in the water and to be stabilised by its fins; the transferable mechanism was a control system, and copying the hull while discarding the controller keeps the liability and throws away the asset.

Where transfer works, what transferred was never a shape. Wilhelm Barthlott and Christoph Neinhuis measured the lotus leaf in the nineteen nineties, found water contact angles above a hundred and sixty-five degrees, and established that self-cleaning requires a two-tier hierarchy, micrometre papillae carrying nanometre wax tubules, and that single-scale roughness gives repellency without cleaning. Hans Ensikat and colleagues measured harder in twenty eleven and found something that inverts the design instinct: an engineered array of identical micro-pillars, which is what a fabricator would make from a diagram of a lotus, adheres more strongly to a droplet than the leaf does, because all its contact points let go at once while papillae of varying height release in sequence. The property to copy is the variance in height, which a specifier would strike out as a defect. Products followed from two thousand and one, a façade paint among them, and Barthlott's own retrospective thirty years on carries no durability, erosion or comparative performance figure for any of them. The wax on a leaf regenerates in minutes to days; a coating has no metabolism. Surfactants disable the surface, which is worth knowing before a façade is detergent-cleaned. The Water Cube in Beijing is a real derivation and it is not biomimicry: Weaire and Phelan's foam geometry of nineteen ninety-four was found with a surface-energy minimiser in software, it is a counterexample to Kelvin's conjecture and not a proven optimum, a second counterexample has since been found, and real foam was not shown to adopt the structure until four years after the building opened. The compliant façade fin from Stuttgart and Freiburg, a hingeless shading element derived from a plant movement, is the strongest candidate in the field for a principle carried all the way to a building component, and its paper is closed with no open copy, so its test results could not be read and are not paraphrased. The Stuttgart research pavilions, a decade of structures grown from sea urchin plates and beetle wings and spider webs, are the largest body of built biomimetic work in existence, they are temporary and unoccupied, their fabrication research is published and their structural research is in venues that could not be reached, and they are biologically informed geometry in petrochemical and mineral material, which puts them at model and not at material. The one case that reaches partnership is bacterial concrete: a dormant organism in the matrix, woken by water through a crack, laying down mineral and closing it. It has three products and field applications in canals and a parking deck, healed crack widths of about half a millimetre, and its founding quantification is behind a paywall, so the increment over the healing plain concrete already does on its own, and over cheap crystalline admixtures tested on the same instruments, is the number that matters and the number nobody could obtain.

Julian Vincent, Olga Bogatyreva, Nikolaj Bogatyrev, Adrian Bowyer and Anja-Karina Pahl gave the mechanism behind all of this in two thousand and six. Classifying the principles by which biology and technology solve problems, they found only about twelve per cent similarity: technology solves by spending energy, biology by arranging structure and deploying information. If that holds, an organism's form is the answer to a question a building does not have, and copying the form imports the wrong answer, while a principle, stated as a relation, can cross. The method behind the twelve per cent could not be read, so it stands here as a strong hypothesis. And the mound did in fact produce a measured, commercialised transfer into building, which the field does not cite. Builders across west and central Africa use termite mound earth for blocks and adobe and mortar, because the insects have already selected and stabilised the clay with their own secretions; engineers in four countries have published its suitability and strength. The field looked at the mound and saw a diagram of airflow. The masons saw stabilised clay, and they were right about what could be taken.

The third field is the measured city, and here the instruments are excellent and they are all outdoors. Ziter and colleagues rode instrumented bicycles across Madison, Wisconsin, through a summer and found that tree canopy cools the daytime air, that the benefit is non-linear and grows steeply once canopy passes about forty per cent, that the effect works at the scale of a block, sixty to ninety metres, and that at night canopy does almost nothing while impervious surface warms the air in a straight line. Iungman and colleagues modelled ninety-three European cities for the summer of twenty fifteen and found the night-time heat island at one point nine degrees against zero point six by day, and stated plainly that trees address the daytime component while canyon geometry and building density drive the night. People die of heat at night, in bedrooms, when the body cannot recover. So the field's flagship remedy is aimed at the smaller half of its flagship problem, and the levers for the larger half, de-paving, reducing exposed thermal mass, opening the sky, are almost absent from the slogans. The same model produced the most quoted number in urban greening, two thousand, six hundred and forty-four deaths preventable by raising every city's canopy to thirty per cent. Read the same paper further and the mean cooling that achieves it is four-tenths of a degree, the canopy increase required is nearly eighteen percentage points, which for most cities is a doubling or tripling, the year is one summer, and two cities, Göteborg and Stockholm, show zero. Cecil Konijnendijk's rule of three visible trees, thirty per cent canopy and three hundred metres to green space is the standard cities now adopt, and its author is honest about it in the paper that proposes it: the three has no scientific support and was chosen so the rule would stick; the thirty is a deliberate compromise below the forty that Ziter measured; the three hundred is inherited from the World Health Organization. The one component with no evidence is the one about seeing nature from a window, which is the aesthetic one. The misquotation is entirely downstream of the paper. And in Portland, Oregon, a study that repeated Madison's method found the canopy response linear rather than a threshold, while replicating the block scale and the day-and-night division, so the threshold that policy rests on is not replicated in its closest repeat.

Barcelona's superblocks are the world's most cited street experiment, and their evidence is a model. Mueller and colleagues estimated six hundred and sixty-seven premature deaths a year preventable by five hundred and three projected superblocks; the largest component, from air pollution, has a prediction interval running from zero to eight hundred and thirty-eight. The only instrumented, site-specific measurement of a superblock outcome this research could find is fifteen people with a smartphone app recording noise at the edge of Poblenou against its interior, and an evaluation from the programme's own literature reports that the early pilots showed limited effectiveness. Meanwhile in London, Goodman and colleagues evaluated a low-traffic neighbourhood in Waltham Forest with police data from twenty twelve to twenty nineteen and adjacent streets as controls, and found street crime down ten per cent rising to eighteen after three years, bicycle theft up, and no displacement. The British schemes have the weaker reputation and the stronger methods.

The glazed plane is the harm architecture causes most directly and counts least. Loss and colleagues synthesised twenty-three carcass surveys in twenty fourteen and estimated between three hundred and sixty-five million and nine hundred and eighty-eight million birds killed by buildings in the United States every year, median five hundred and ninety-nine million, fifty-six per cent of them at low-rise buildings and forty-four at houses, under one per cent at towers. The advocacy figure of more than a billion is the rounded top of that range, presented without the median, the range or a source; a rehabilitation study that found sixty per cent of birds brought in alive still died argues the true number is higher, and that is a separate claim. At one exhibition hall in Chicago, two decades of daily carcass collection set against weather radar showed that halving the lighted window area would cut mortality by about sixty per cent, a projection from measured relationships rather than an observed outcome. About fifteen per cent of migration nights carry half the season's deaths and those nights are predictable from the weather, so a lights-out protocol triggered on forecast captures most of the benefit for nothing. Across twenty-two buildings on a Virginia campus, glass area was the primary predictor of collisions, open lawn lowered the risk and ornamental trees beside the glass raised it, a finding replicated in Argentina with reflection named as the mechanism; the conventional good move of planting up against a glazed elevation is, for birds, the bad move. The pattern spacing that would turn this into a glazing rule could not be verified, no ordinance text was opened, and the one confirmed legal requirement is a bird-safe ordinance passed in Jersey City in twenty twenty-six. The rules that give the finding legal force are less reachable than the ecology that justifies them.

The rest of the measured city is worth a sentence each. Green roofs are measured well for water and not at all for the room beneath: a Toronto roof passed four times as much rain in winter as in summer, a Norwegian roof with fifteen years of records shows that only about seven in ten extreme rainfalls produce extreme runoff and which ones depends on how wet the substrate already was, and the honest retention figure across the literature is anywhere from four to ninety-eight per cent. Cool paint on a metal roof in Indonesia cut the surface temperature by six point six degrees and the room's air at head height by zero point eight, an eightfold attenuation from skin to occupant in the best case, and the only clean interior number in the whole urban lane. Cities keep mostly native species by composition and hold them at eight per cent of natural density for birds and a quarter for plants, and what predicts the difference is land-use history, so retaining existing vegetation beats planting new. Warm-coloured street lighting at spill levels still draws moths in forest edges, light and noise together harm insects more than either alone, and a coastal city lights the sea to a hundred metres down. The critique of pricing ecosystem services and its founder's rebuttal are both behind paywalls. And urban ecology's literature contains essentially no Indigenous or community land knowledge; the disciplines do not read each other.

That knowledge is the fourth field of this chapter, and it is treated here as living practice, in the present tense, cited to the people whose knowledge it is and to those who documented it, with nations named and never merged. Two adopted design frameworks exist in the English-speaking planning world, and both are nearly invisible to scholarly search. Connecting with Country, published by the Government Architect of New South Wales in twenty twenty-three and tested on the Countries of the Sydney Basin, is guidance and not a requirement, and it says so: it introduces no new planning obligations, it applies in principle to every project and scales engagement by significance with no capital threshold, it defines Country as a network of relationships among all living and non-living things, it states that government does not speak for Country, it distinguishes an environmentally sensitive approach from a Country-centred one from a full designing-with-Country process, it makes access to Country a design obligation, it requires cultural intellectual property protocols and free and informed prior consent, and where it is assessed at all it assesses process and not the building. Nobody is grading the building, and no evaluation of the framework exists. Te Aranga, the Māori design principles in Auckland's guidance, could not be reached at all: the design manual refused the request, the only account found is a Turkish municipal journal article that names seven principles and lists none, its date is given as two thousand and six in one place and two thousand and eight in others, its status as mandatory or advisory is unstated, and the names it gives for the principles' authors were judged almost certainly corrupted and are not printed here, because under this series' rules a wrong Indigenous author name is a failure of the attribution itself.

The fire evidence, by contrast, is among the best-instrumented material in the book. Tony Marks-Block, Frank Lake, Rebecca Bliege Bird and Lisa Curran monitored forty-eight plots across twenty-one burn sites in Karuk and Yurok ancestral territory in north-west California from twenty fifteen to twenty nineteen, with the approval of both Tribal councils obtained before the university's own. Hazelnut shrubs produced thirteen times as many usable basketry stems one growing season after a cultural burn as shrubs three or more seasons unburned; plots burned three or more times carried nearly twice the shrub density; and weavers gathered four point nine stems a minute at culturally burned sites against one point six at wildfire sites and half a stem a minute where fire had been excluded, which is a factor of ten in a weaver's time. Three-quarters of the gatherers observed were women and most groups were mentors with students; that is what the burning is for, and the ecology is reported because the measurement is excellent, not because a practice with its own significance needs a plot study to justify it. All the frequently burned plots lay on the Yurok Reservation, so burn frequency and land tenure cannot be separated, and the authors treat that as the finding: where tenure is stronger, fire is more frequent. The Karuk Tribe holds under four square kilometres in federal trust; nearly half the Yurok Reservation is private timber land; fire was excluded from nineteen hundred and five, and the counter-programme that began in twenty fourteen was designed to burn a hundred and seventy-six acres. On Banbai Country in New South Wales, Michelle McKemey, John Hunter, Nick Reid and two Banbai rangers, Maureen Lesley Patterson and Ian Simpson, who are authors of the paper, compared a four-hectare mosaic cultural burn on the Wattleridge Indigenous Protected Area with a six-hundred-and-eighty-five-hectare agency hazard-reduction burn at Warra National Park, using a before-and-after design with unburnt controls. Neither fire changed the overall plant composition, both reduced fuel, and the agency burn significantly reduced shrub cover while the cultural burn did not; the agency burn also overshot its planned area by three hundred and thirty-four hectares. In December twenty nineteen the Crown Mountain wildfire burnt every plot. The long-term evidence for cultural burning is thin partly because the landscapes being studied are burning down faster than the studies can mature. In Arnhem Land the Traditional Owners' fire management has been written into a national carbon-accounting method, the only Indigenous land practice in this chapter with that legal grade; twelve years of it moved fire from the late dry season to the early, with the total area burned unchanged and every ecological threshold improved except the keeping of long-unburnt habitat, and the Traditional Owners' own plans rank carbon abatement sixth of their six goals. British Columbia confines Indigenous fire stewardship to zero point four per cent of the province; agencies move toward learning after a fire season and then regress. In Sápmi, Alessia Uboni, Birgitta Åhman and Jon Moen found about seventy per cent of the most productive lichen winter pasture in northern Sweden lost to forestry, hydropower, mining and roads since the nineteen fifties, while reindeer numbers over the same seventy years show no trend at all, because the herders closed the gap with bought feed, a herd restructured toward females and machines. A stable output concealed a collapsed input, and the cost was borne by the people doing the adapting, which is the snow house of the first chapter again: the performance belongs to the inhabited system, not the shell. The celebrated finding that Indigenous-managed lands hold as much vertebrate diversity as protected areas is, read at source, an overlay of range maps on tenure polygons whose authors decline to claim any mechanism, and the one study that walked transects, in Arunachal Pradesh, found protected areas richer. And two instruments bind a book like this one: the CARE principles for Indigenous data governance, drafted in Gaborone in twenty eighteen, and OCAP, which is specifically a First Nations framework in Canada, a registered trademark, and not a synonym for Indigenous data practice. Where knowledge is restricted under community law its absence from this chapter is the system working. One more thing about the word partner. In this field it has two referents, Country as an entity with standing, of which the Whanganui River's legal personhood is the clearest case, and a community as a party with jurisdiction. The second is not a way of bringing nature into architecture; it is a description of who holds authority, and to tag it as an aesthetic mode would be an extraction, so this chapter tags only the first.

The fifth field is the living building, and it names the chapter's place on the series' measurement line. The Living Building Challenge is unusual among certifications because, at full Living Certification, it certifies only after twelve months of metered occupation: on-site renewables covering a hundred and five per cent of annual energy, on-site water for twelve months at monthly metering, thresholds a meter can fail. That deserves credit without qualification, and so does the honest reading of the rest of the certificate, which makes cultural celebration and beauty equally mandatory and cannot meter either; it is two standards printed on one page. Here is what this research found when it went after the numbers. Three bibliographic searches returned no peer-reviewed measured-performance study of any certified project. The Bullitt Center in Seattle, opened in twenty thirteen, six storeys, the most photographed environmental building of its decade, publishes its performance continuously as a live dashboard, and its own page carries no figure; nothing from that dashboard could be retrieved, and a dashboard cannot be cited, audited retrospectively or preserved. The Kendeda Building's performance page at Georgia Tech returns an error. Nothing at all came back for the Bertschi School's science wing. The best-measured buildings of the period are among the worst-documented. Meanwhile Passivhaus, a German technical standard with no marketing to speak of, turns out to be the most thoroughly instrumented low-energy standard in the world.

What the Passivhaus instruments found is the cleanest natural experiment in this chapter, because the standard verifies its fabric by instrument and its comfort by calculation. Johnston and colleagues coheated seven certified dwellings in the winter of twenty eleven, holding the empty houses at twenty-five degrees and deriving their heat loss from the energy balance, and found six of them losing between six and twenty-one per cent more heat than predicted and one fifteen per cent less; a few watts per kelvin, in a field where conventional houses miss by a hundred per cent. Gupta and Kotopouleas then analysed a hundred and eighty-eight dwellings from the United Kingdom's national performance evaluation programme, fifty of them Passivhaus, and found the mechanism: in the Passivhaus dwellings the measured airtightness predicted the measured heating energy, and in the others it did not. A standard whose regulated quantity still drives the outcome is doing work; one whose regulated quantity has come loose is performing a ritual. Then the same group monitored a certified sixty-six-square-metre bungalow in the north of England, built for elderly occupants and lived in around the clock by two retired people over sixty-five, for twenty-one months. The lounge was above twenty-five degrees for fifty-one per cent of the hours of twenty fourteen against a predicted five per cent, and the bedroom for twenty-one per cent; it overheated in winter too; the causes were appliance heat the calculation had not allowed for, low thermal mass, a ventilation bypass set by season rather than by temperature, a south-facing lounge, and windows that were not opened. That house is very probably the same house that measured forty-six point seven watts per kelvin in the coheating study, which would make one address the evidence that Passivhaus fabric works and the evidence that a Passivhaus can overheat for half the year. Across a hundred and ninety-five monitored dwellings, Passivhaus bedrooms ran one point six degrees hotter at night than ordinary ones, sixty-five per cent of them failed the sleeping-hours criterion against forty-three per cent of ordinary bedrooms, and eighty-three per cent of the same houses passed the standard's whole-dwelling criterion. In an Irish house, averaging the temperatures across rooms cancelled more than half the bedroom overheating. A standard that passes at building level and fails at room level is measuring the wrong object. In Cesena a simplified model of the kind used for certification under-predicted overheating by a third or more in a warm climate and hardly at all in a cold one. And in Bilbao, across a three-hundred-and-sixty-one-apartment development monitored for four years, the semi-exterior laundry spaces sat five degrees above the outside air day and night and touched fifty degrees, a threshold that only works one way, the inverse of the windcatcher in Yazd that was cooler by day and warmer by night.

The interior air evidence follows the same pattern of an influential number and a robust one. In twenty sixteen Allen and colleagues put twenty-four professionals in a laboratory office for six days, blinded to the conditions, and found their scores on a management simulation sixty-one per cent higher on a day with low volatile organics at the same ventilation, a hundred and one per cent higher with the ventilation doubled, and twenty-one per cent lower for every four hundred parts per million of carbon dioxide. Then thirty-six submariners tested on the same instrument at six hundred, two and a half thousand and fifteen thousand parts per million showed no difference on any measure; sixty-nine students in a pure carbon dioxide crossover at up to twenty-one hundred showed no loss of accuracy; and the original group's own field study of three hundred and two office workers in forty-two buildings across six countries found effects in the same direction at roughly a fifteenth to a twentieth of the chamber's size per unit of gas, with particulates significant above twelve micrograms per cubic metre. Carbon dioxide is a good tracer and a poor agent. The chamber's largest single step came from cutting volatile organics tenfold at constant ventilation, which is a finding about what a room is made of, and the one point at which the living building's materials list touches a measured literature. Nowhere reached is there a study of ecological regeneration on any building site with a measured baseline before construction, a control site and years of monitoring afterward; the regenerative literature is overwhelmingly normative and its one measured record concerns carbon in timber. The apparatus the United Kingdom built after the nineteen nineties to learn from measurement, a framework for aftercare, a platform for comparing prediction with meters, and a national monitoring programme of several hundred buildings, is in its own published form unreachable: the framework's effect equivocal on review, the platform's dataset never analysed, the programme's synthesis a paywalled chapter. And the dataset that founded Passivhaus itself, the European monitoring of the early two thousands, is closed twenty years on.

This series keeps a line that runs through all eight chapters, the line of what was measured and who can read it. The first four chapters found the measurement never made. The fifth found it made and enclosed, behind paywalls and purchased standards. This chapter falls in the next band, and names it: made, given away freely, and still lost. The best-measured buildings of the period publish their numbers live and keep none; a framework in force in a major city's planning guidance cannot be read by any scholarly route; a national programme's findings survive only as fragments in other people's papers. And the collapse the whole series has been tracing is sharpest here. Measurement is dense between buildings, on surfaces, on organisms and on landscapes, and it thins to almost nothing at the façade, and again at the person: two sensors in a bungalow with two occupants; skin conductance on sixty-five students for six minutes; between-person variation larger than the effect in the largest field study. The interior is measured at scale elsewhere in this book for one question, thermal comfort, so the charge is not that it cannot be done. It is that nobody has chosen to do it for the questions this chapter is about.

What carries forward is uncomfortable and specific. The evidence for nature in architecture strengthens as you move outward from the building and weakens as you move in, while the claims run the other way, and the two meet at the window, where a real view of real vegetation in real daylight is the one interior move both curves support and where no study can yet tell you whether the light or the view is doing the work. When a transfer from biology works, what transferred was a principle and never a shape. Day and night are two heat problems with two levers, and the night is the larger. The glazed plane kills at the scale of hundreds of millions of birds a year and the cheapest remedy is a switch on fifteen nights. The fire knowledge of named nations is measured better than most buildings and is confined, by tenure, to slivers of the land it belongs to. A standard is only as measured as its weakest verification act. And a number published live and archived nowhere is, for the purposes of learning, a number that was never taken. The next chapter turns from the field to practice, outside, at the threshold and inside, and inherits all of it.
