This is the eighth and last chapter of a series about how people have brought nature into buildings. The series runs from the deep past to the present and then looks ahead, and it asks one question all the way through: how has nature been brought inside, outside and at the threshold of buildings, and what does that record actually establish for an architect deciding what to do now. This chapter covers the shortest stretch of time of all, because its period has not happened yet. Its evidence is the present, and it is the chapter in which a book like this usually abandons its standards. It does not abandon them here, for two reasons. A projection is not a measurement. Coupon-scale data is not building-scale data.

One idea runs through the whole series. There is a spectrum of ways a building can relate to nature. At one end nature is an image, a picture of a leaf, a framed view. Then nature is a form, a shape borrowed. Then a model, a structure or a process imitated. Then a material, living or once-living matter as the substance of the building. Then a climate, the building shaped by sun, wind and water. Then a system, the building as a working part of an ecological cycle. And at the far end nature is a partner, the living world as a collaborator with something of its own at stake. The spectrum is not a ladder of progress. In this chapter it does something none of the earlier chapters showed so plainly: almost every celebrated case is claimed at one point on the spectrum and evidenced at another. That gap is the characteristic finding of the chapter.

Two limits first. This research ran without web search, so every source was reached by direct bibliographic retrieval or not at all, and where a source could not be opened the chapter says so and does not fill the gap from memory. And image collections were out of reach, so every figure is a drawing to be made.

Begin with the materials that are grown rather than quarried. The expected criticism turns out to be wrong. It is often said that these materials are claimed in advance of any measurement being possible. They are not. Every material here is measurable now and most have been measured well. The problem is scale. Of fourteen substantive sources read, two report a measurement above the scale of a specimen, and not one reports a measurement in an occupied building. The longest continuous monitoring anywhere in the material evidence is six months.

Take mycelium composite, the fungal material grown on agricultural waste. Ghazvinian and Gürsoy at Penn State grew oyster mushroom mycelium on five substrate mixtures across thirty-five treatments at five, six and seven weeks, then dried and compressed the specimens. Compressive strength ran from 33 to 491 kilopascals: a fifteen-fold spread, from two controllable variables, in one species, in one laboratory. Any single published figure for the strength of mycelium composite is therefore a point in a design space and not a property. Cultivation time correlated inversely with strength across every mixture, so the longer you grow it the weaker it gets, because the fungus consumes the substrate it binds.

The fire numbers for the same material are good and are frequently over-read. A team led by Jones at RMIT found pure mycelium peaking at 67 watts per gram of heat release against 446 for acrylic and 375 for polylactic acid, with a char yield of about 23 per cent where the two polymers left nothing. That is a large advantage, and it is an advantage of a substance. It does not establish a fire resistance rating, a reaction-to-fire class or any behaviour in an assembly with a lining, a cavity and a fixing. A building code does not ask a question about a substance. It asks a question about a wall.

Now the correction that kills the number everybody quotes. The property that makes something an insulation is its thermal conductivity. Four primary or review sources on mycelium were read in full and not one measured it. One states in its own text that it adopted a value from a survey of other people's research; another built a whole comparative assessment on an amount of material per unit of thermal resistance, so it must have adopted a figure too; the likeliest primary source could not be reached. The mycelium thermal conductivity in general circulation is therefore not a measurement in anything this book read, and it is not printed here as one. The conductivity is higher than most conventional insulation, so the material needs greater thickness and greater mass for the same resistance. And the moisture dependence of that conductivity is entirely unmeasured for a material that loses two-thirds of its mass in drying.

Hemp-lime gives the chapter its one revealing full-scale campaign, and it contradicts the material's own sales story. Bohn and Bocco at the Politecnico di Torino built a pointed hemp-lime arch of nine cubic metres, thirty centimetres thick at one pier and sixty at the vault, logged every ten minutes for six months. Internal moisture rose about ten percentage points across the whole period and never reached equilibrium. Absorption was fast and drying slow, more than five days to shed seven points after one day of falling outdoor humidity, and a slight steady gain continued despite pronounced outdoor swings, which the authors attribute to lime carbonation still releasing water. Hemp-lime is sold on breathability, and the coupon-scale buffer value supports that over a daily cycle. What six months at full scale measured was a slow one-way accumulation with the daily buffering riding on top, invisible, the authors say, to a twenty-eight-day protocol. The short-cycle result circulates; the long-cycle one decides.

Mass timber has the best full-scale fire evidence in the chapter and a received story that needs correcting. At Edinburgh, Hadden and colleagues burned five compartments lined with five-layer cross-laminated spruce, exposing two surfaces in two of them, a back wall and ceiling in two more, and three surfaces in the fifth. Flashover came between four and nine minutes, peak temperatures ran from 1,114 to 1,236 degrees Celsius and char depths from 10 to 26 millimetres. Auto-extinction, the property on which the safety case for exposed timber rests, was observed in exactly one experiment of five, and only where the char layer stayed attached. Every other test burned on and needed manual suppression after more than an hour, and a third exposed surface removed auto-extinction entirely through mutual radiation. Now the correction. The fire problem of cross-laminated timber is usually named as delamination. Here the largest measured fall-off masses were the plasterboard encapsulation, about ninety-nine kilograms in one test and a hundred and five in another, where another test shed none at all, so encapsulation performance and adhesive performance are two separate specifications and the tests indict both. In 2014 Buchanan, Östman and Frangi listed ten prioritised research needs for tall timber, among them the conditions for self-extinguishment of charred wood and the fall-off time of protective boards; three years later the Edinburgh tests answered both, negatively. And no full-scale fire test of a mass timber building at the heights now routinely proposed was located at all.

The engineered living materials, with bacteria alive inside the matrix, are furthest from a building and most confidently described. The best review reached reports microbial survivability of 9 to 14 per cent at thirty days and no field or full-scale application of any living building material at all. A material whose organisms are between eighty-six and ninety-one per cent dead after a month has a measured living lifetime of weeks. The flagship paper of the field, the demonstration that such a material can be regenerated repeatedly from a parent sample, could not be opened by any route, so the field's single most important claim is unfilled here and uncited rather than repeated.

Two built demonstrations carry almost the whole popular case for grown material, and neither carries a measurement. A tower of grown fungal brick stood in New York for one summer in 2014, and no instrumented study, monitoring report or structural test came back on any formulation; its designer, height, brick count and duration were deliberately not supplied from memory. What it evidences is that it stood for a season in one temperate city and did not fall down. The Hamburg algae façade of 2013 is starker. Two independent bibliographic formulations returned no biomass yield, no thermal contribution, no delivered energy balance, no maintenance record and no statement of current operational status; the only contemporaneous indexed item is a trade-magazine note from 2013 saying the building was going to test an algae-filled façade. The model has been reviewed more often than the building has been measured. That is claimed at the level of a working system and evidenced at the level of a material, and it is the starkest such gap in the book, because it is a gap between a claim and no measurement at all.

So is biological origin better? One careful answer, and it is mostly no. Alaux and colleagues at Graz assessed mycelium insulation across eleven impact categories on a projected 2035 energy background. On climate change it emitted roughly half the greenhouse gases of polyurethane and of extruded polystyrene, and only slightly less than rock wool. On every other indicator assessed it was worse than the comparators: acidification above stone wool, eutrophication higher through the rapeseed oil coating and the rye grain, terrestrial ecotoxicity up a hundred and twenty per cent on industrial scale-up. At a fifteen-year service life rather than thirty it had the highest impacts of the set on most indicators except climate. The penalties come from the agricultural inputs and rise as the process industrialises, so biological origin is the mechanism of the disadvantage as well as the advantage. Then the sharpest generalisation in the materials evidence. Manufacturing holds ninety-five per cent of the non-biogenic emissions, and electricity rises from thirty-nine per cent of those at laboratory scale to sixty-four at industrial scale. A grown material is an electricity product. Its performance is a function of the grid it is grown on, and the advantage modelled is partly the advantage of a decarbonised grid that does not exist yet. Two footnotes travel with those numbers: the cradle-to-gate figure of about nine hundred kilograms of carbon dioxide equivalent per cubic metre disagrees threefold with published values of two hundred and fifty to three hundred and seventy; and a correction was published in May 2025 whose content could not be established.

The carbon accounting for wood is worse: five positions with numbers and no winner. A meta-analysis of substitution gives displacement factors from minus 2.3 to 15 tonnes of carbon avoided per tonne in product, with an unweighted mean of about 2.1 for construction. A second synthesis is reported at second hand at about 1.2. A third holds the benefits overestimated by between two and a hundred per cent. A fourth, argued by Leturcq, holds the displacement factor structurally invalid and time-dependent, and that once harvest and manufacturing residues are counted it should fall by two to four units, which against a mean of 2.1 is an erasure or a sign change. A fifth, a dynamic assessment, finds the biogenic warming potential falling with product lifetime and behaving non-monotonically in rotation length. One thing is agreed by all five. The climate benefit of a wood building is a function of how long the building lasts. That is the one variable the architect controls, and no assessment measures it.

Three absences close this section. Not one of fourteen sources reports an acoustic measurement, for materials whose principal proposed use is partition and insulation where acoustic separation is a code requirement. Not one study sits in a hot-humid climate, where moisture management is hardest and biodegradation sharpest. And where these materials meet weather the evidence stops or turns negative. This family of materials is not under-evidenced. It is evidenced at the wrong grain. The substance is measured, the wall is not, and the room certainly is not.

One celebrated building is regularly listed with the grown materials and does not belong there. The Stuttgart fibre pavilions are woven by robot into forms derived from beetle wing cases, from carbon and glass fibre in a polymer matrix: biologically informed in geometry and built from petrochemical and mineral material, so they belong with the imitated model and not with the grown substance, and they are not grouped in this chapter with mycelium, hemp or timber. Mindermann and colleagues followed the Heilbronn pavilion for a hundred and thirty days and found a difference of 33 kelvin between the carbon and the glass inside a single component, invisible in any drawing and discovered by putting an instrument on the building. Then a sentence to read twice: the authors judged structural monitoring unnecessary, did not carry it out, and confirmed that the structural design held up on the basis of visual inspection. That is a claim at the level of a model supported at the level of an image, in a careful open paper.

Now the climate the building has to answer, beginning with a method problem almost nobody in practice has absorbed. A simulation reads eight thousand seven hundred and sixty hourly rows of weather, and no climate model produces them. Morphing takes an observed year and shifts and stretches it by a climate model's monthly mean changes; dynamical downscaling runs a regional model at high resolution and assembles a genuinely new sequence, at far greater cost, for far fewer places. Morphing has a structural limit: a morphed file inherits the sequence of its parent year, so a four-day heatwave becomes a warmer four-day heatwave and cannot become a nine-day one. Tootkaboni and colleagues ran the comparison on two mid-century Roman residential archetypes under one high-emissions scenario for the 2050s. The house gained about 170 per cent in cooling demand by morphing and about 70 per cent by downscaling; the apartment block about 100 against about 40. That is a factor of about two and a half from the choice of method alone, on the same building, in the same paper. Nothing is hidden, and the profession has standardised on the cheaper method, which yields a number that looks precise, is reproducible, and is wrong by an unknown amount in a knowable direction. The honest form of a claim about 2050 is directional and bounded, never a point estimate.

The stock we already have does not need a future file to fail. Lomas's review of English national monitoring reports that in the summer of 2010 about two per cent of living rooms overheated against the national criterion, roughly six hundred thousand dwellings, and in 2018, England's hottest summer on record, fifteen per cent did, about three point six million. Flats overheated at thirty per cent against twelve for other dwelling types, which is a finding about building type rather than about insulation. Then a detail that ought to change a workflow. Finegan monitored a nearly-zero-energy dwelling in Cork across six habitable spaces for a year and found that averaging the measured temperatures across the dwelling, to match the single average the design tool simulates, cancelled fifty-six per cent of the overheating actually present in the bedrooms. The criterion names a bedroom, the measurement finds the bedroom, and the tool's units hide it. Against which sits one measured result pointing the other way: fifty-nine English adults monitored over seven nights in the summer of 2024, across bedroom temperatures from 17.9 to 30 degrees Celsius, slept with slightly better efficiency and less waking at the warmer end. The twenty-six-degree criterion and that result are not compatible as stated.

The moving façade is the clearest case of a device with no operational record, and the received story needs correcting in both directions. Cocho-Bermejo's comparative review of adaptive façades from 1985 to 2024 states that many of the photographic diaphragms on the Paris institute malfunctioned over time, rendering the façade effectively static, attributes that to maintenance difficulty, and records that broken elements persisted through several refurbishments. That is one sourced failure with photographic evidence. For the Barcelona building and the Abu Dhabi towers the same review documents no failure and no operation: no monitored data, no maintenance record, no performance validation over time. So the widely repeated story that several celebrated kinetic façades have been disabled is, on the reachable record, one sourced case and a set of rumours, and the specific claim about the Abu Dhabi shading could not be sourced and is not stated in this chapter. There is no published post-occupancy evaluation of any kinetic façade's operational reliability anywhere reachable. This is not enclosure. Four decades of the profession's most photographed climate devices have produced nothing to enclose.

Two shorter climate findings, each with a refusal in it. On phase-change materials a structured search returned sixteen records and not one monitored occupied dwelling, so no discrepancy figure between simulated and measured is printed, because none was found; and on radiative sky cooling the founding paper is closed with no open location anywhere, so none of its numbers appear. Passive survivability, which is the right question, is asked entirely in the future tense: of nine items, five are preprints, three are simulations, the one field-measured study is paywalled, and none is a monitored occupied building. Two searches for a standard, code or rating requiring any duration of habitability without power returned only comfort standards. The seventy-two-hour target everybody quotes is the emergency-management planning horizon, imported: the duration a building is asked to hold is borrowed and not derived.

Which brings the chapter to its sharpest unresolved conflict, the one that sets the fire scientists against the rest of this book. Syphard, Brennan and Keeley mapped a thousand destroyed and a thousand surviving structures across every fire in which homes burned in San Diego County over a decade. Structures survived more where defensible space existed immediately adjacent. The most effective treatment distance was five to twenty metres. Distances beyond thirty metres gave no additional protection, even on steep slopes. The effective actions were reducing woody cover immediately adjacent and preventing overhang or contact. The mechanism was then measured properly, with public money, in a wind tunnel. Manzello, Suzuki and Hayashi fired firebrands at full-scale assemblies in Japan and published free and machine-readable results. A pine-needle mulch bed beside a wall ignited by smouldering, transitioned to flaming, melted vinyl siding and established self-sustaining smouldering in the sheathing behind, including in sheathing not pre-dried. With needles accumulated under roof tiles there was ignition under every condition tested, and firebrands were not quenched by vent mesh but burned until small enough to pass through it.

But fire science is not univocal, and the disagreement has to stand. Gibbons and colleagues analysed four hundred and ninety-nine houses within the perimeters of the Victorian fires of February 2009. Reducing remnant native vegetation cover within forty metres from ninety per cent to five per cent reduced the likelihood of house loss by forty-three per cent, and houses two kilometres from public forest suffered twenty-six per cent fewer losses than those at ten metres. And the result that matters most for this book: where vegetation within forty metres was predominantly planted rather than remnant native, thirty-eight per cent fewer houses were destroyed. A later Californian study found that in the North Interior region the relationship ran inverse, with higher greenness at the structures that survived. The two large observational studies agree about the first few metres and disagree about everything past thirty, and this chapter records that and does not average it. What the evidence supports, narrowly, is that the prohibition is on fine, dead, cured fuel in contact with the building: mulch against walls, litter in gutters, needles under tiles, debris behind vents, timber fencing run to the wall. It is not a prohibition on plants: living, irrigated, maintained planting is a different fuel, and the building side can be hardened. The first few metres are a different design problem from the rest of the site. The single experiment that would settle this, living irrigated planting immediately adjacent to a hardened envelope under a firebrand shower, has not been run, and it is feasible with apparatus that already exists.

And the landscape inside which that argument is fought is itself a historical artefact. Mariani and colleagues, working with local Indigenous communities, reconstructed pre-colonial land cover and fire across multiple south-eastern Australian sites from pollen and charcoal. Before 1788 those landscapes were predominantly grassy, with tree cover averaging about fourteen per cent, savanna-like. After British settlement shrub cover increased, the understorey thickened, and the biomass burned rose above levels seen across the whole preceding Holocene, a change the authors attribute to management rather than to regional climate. The high fuel loads that make near-building planting dangerous are, on this evidence, a consequence of removing a management regime that had held that landscape open for millennia. This knowledge is cited twice, as the house rules require: to the Aboriginal peoples of south-eastern Australia, whose fire management created and maintains the landscape the proxies record and who are a living presence and not a precedent, and to the research team who documented it. The article names no specific nations and this chapter supplies none; that absence may in part be an absence in the record and in part be protocol properly governing what is published, so it is named and not guessed at.

The non-human client is the third strand, and every headline number in it came in below its advocacy. Start with the reversal. Farming ceased at Knepp in West Sussex in 2001 and free-ranging herbivores were introduced. Pates and colleagues surveyed vascular plants in square-metre quadrats at Knepp and at Boothby in Lincolnshire, a site converting from arable in 2022, across three summers. On within-plot diversity Knepp is emphatic: a median of nine species per square metre against one, and phylogenetic diversity of nine hundred and eight million years against three hundred and ninety-one. But Boothby's total richness rose from seventy-eight to a hundred and eighty-six across three years, and by 2024 the two-year-old ex-arable site had overtaken the twenty-three-year-old flagship on total species richness, a hundred and eighty-two against a hundred and sixty-two. Total richness is the metric rewilding advocacy quotes most. The within-plot result is the one that survives scrutiny, because a field two years out of arable accumulates ruderals fast. The choice of metric decides whether rewilding works, and the comparison has a virtue rare in this literature, which is that it had a baseline.

Now the provision for named species, which is where architecture actually touches this. Schaub, Meffert and Kerth censused four hundred and seventy-seven compensatory nest boxes on twenty-three buildings in Greifswald, installed as the ecological compensation that permitted renovation. A hundred and sixteen were occupied, twenty-four point three per cent, so three-quarters of the compensation was empty. And the strongest single predictor was the number of neighbouring boxes within a metre, with the effect negative: occupancy fell as neighbours increased, and boxes with none had the highest. The standard detail, a continuous run of units or a multi-unit brick, is the wrong detail, and the recommendation is to place them a few metres apart near the roof edge. That is the least-cited measured result in the whole strand. Beside it, a British dataset comparing boxes with and without an internal nest cup found forty-eight point four per cent occupied with the cup and fifteen per cent without, roughly a threefold increase: the cup matters more than the brick. And a swift entrance is a deliberate penetration of the air barrier into a cavity within the insulation zone, so the energy requirement and the habitat requirement are contradictory descriptions of one construction detail; three separate searches across two bibliographic corpora returned nothing on that conflict at all, and it is not, so far as retrieval can show, a research subject.

Bee hotels were measured properly once and the result is uncomfortable. MacIvor and Packer deployed six hundred hotels across Toronto over three years, recovered five hundred and seventy-four, and reared every occupant to adult: twenty-seven thousand two hundred and seventy-five bees and wasps. Native bees were twenty-seven point six per cent of emergences. Native wasps were the largest group at thirty-seven point eight. Introduced bees were twenty-four point six per cent. The hotels captured seventy-six point nine per cent of the region's entire known introduced cavity-nesting bee fauna. And native bees were parasitised significantly more than introduced ones. The correction to the received summary makes the finding stronger, not weaker: bee hotels are not dominated by parasites, they are dominated by non-target taxa, with parasitism falling differentially on the natives, and the authors themselves raise the possibility that these structures are population sinks for native bees. It is claimed as partnership and operates as image. The bee hotel is bought by the party that wants to be seen to care, installed at the scale that is visible, and measured by nobody. The absence of measurement is not an oversight in that system. It is what allows the system to work.

Bat boxes give the chapter its clearest measured harm. Rueegger logged sixteen multi-chambered boxes in paired designs near Muswellbrook in New South Wales, plywood against wood-cement, black against white. On the warmest day of one summer window, with ambient at 35.4 degrees Celsius, the front chamber of a black plywood box reached 42.4, about seven degrees above the air, and across that window it exceeded forty degrees, the postulated upper tolerance of the animals, on seventeen per cent of readings, where no other design ever exceeded it. The maximum difference between paired designs was nine degrees. Colour and material are the thermal design, and the author's own guidance is that black boxes should not be used in very warm regions, where they become ecological traps: attractive, occupied and lethal at the wrong moment.

The largest measured design lever in the strand is light. Hao and colleagues trapped nocturnal insects on a Tianjin campus under narrow-spectrum lamps at matched irradiance and caught forty-five thousand four hundred and thirty-one insects across fourteen orders. Against ultraviolet at one, relative attraction was 0.401 at blue, about 0.26 across cyan and green, 0.102 at red-orange and 0.013 at far red: about a thirtyfold range from blue to far red at identical radiant power. Nothing else in this material moves a measured ecological outcome by that margin at no change in delivered energy. And no instrumented study of insect or bird response to a specified building's lighting design was found. Every readable study is a lamp in a field.

The wildlife crossing structure inverts its own best source. A meta-analysis of fifty studies and ninety-nine effect sizes found that mitigation overall cut road-kill by forty per cent, that fencing alone cut it by eighty-six per cent, and that crossing structures alone showed no detectable effect at all, with a point estimate of a twenty-three per cent increase. The fence delivers the safety and the structure delivers connectivity, measured by use and gene flow and not by carcasses, so a structure sold on safety grounds without a fence is sold on the wrong evidence. And the connectivity half is almost entirely closed: every paper reporting species-specific use at the best-monitored wildlife infrastructure on earth is unreadable without a subscription.

Standing is different, and it is real. A critical study of legal personhood for the Rhône surveys the machinery: standing to sue, injury cognisable in court, relief running to the entity, a human representative. The Whanganui has two appointed guardians. The Atrato in Colombia has fourteen, from local communities. The Mar Menor in Spain has a collegiate body of about fifteen. And on the Rhône the same authors find no significant change in the governance system despite growing discourse. Standing does not create a voice. It creates an office, and the office is occupied by people with interests: a brief can acquire a non-human client with legal standing and the project can proceed exactly as it would have. Two things belong alongside that. This chapter keeps partnership as standing and partnership as a design mode strictly apart: the first exists in law and is operative, the second is aspiration, and no building was found anywhere in which the partner's own outcome was measured. And there is a failure to report. This research reached no source authored by Whanganui iwi or hapū, and under this book's double-citation law that is a failure in execution, named here rather than covered over.

The fourth strand is computation, and it turns on one distinction. There is a difference between a measurement of a building and a measurement of a model of a building. Both produce numbers with units and error bars, and only one is evidence about a building. Apply that and several substantial literatures move from supporting to unproven. The control field has standardised its measurement onto a simulated emulator, because comparing controllers across real buildings is effectively impossible and a common emulator is reproducible. A fully open 2026 paper reports energy savings of up to twenty-eight point nine per cent for a learning controller transferred to a high-fidelity emulator. That is careful, reproducible, preregisterable work, and the object measured is a simulation. It is more dangerous than a misused measurement, because it looks like good science and lacks only external validity. A practitioner cannot detect it by assessing methodological quality, and the only test is to ask what physical object the number refers to.

Set the field numbers beside it and the ordering is startling. The largest measured energy reduction located anywhere in this material is fifty-three per cent across an eighty-person office suite over two summer months, achieved by Hu, Zhan and Norford by shortening daily schedules on the legacy control system and allowing manual override in individual offices, with no new hardware, no model, no learning algorithm and no twin. The authors are candid: two months, one season, one suite, and a prior-consumption baseline rather than a contemporaneous control. Against that, a year of cascaded predictive control on a campus chilled-water system returned two point two to four point four per cent, measured. And inside one curated special issue on practical applications of predictive control, the simulated figures in the same collection are three to thirty times the one measured figure. The ordering of measured effect sizes is the inverse of cost: the cheapest intervention carries the largest number and the largest uncertainty, while the most expensive carry the smallest numbers and the most confident presentation.

Sensing has a mechanism finding that is new to this book. A Pittsburgh co-location study of a low-cost multi-pollutant package against regulatory monitors reported mean absolute errors of about two per cent for carbon dioxide and twenty-nine per cent for nitrogen dioxide from the same device. Then a Thessaloniki campaign ran thirty-three devices across two years against European reference instruments. Raw sensor data failed the European uncertainty threshold at every reference site, so calibration is the difference between unusable and usable. Ozone sensors were calibrable for eight to ten months, after which they returned readings far below zero, into concentrations that cannot exist, and particulate sensors drifted over thirteen to twenty-four months. The service life of a calibration is therefore between four months and about a year, so an installation commissioned at handover and left alone is producing meaningless gas data within the first year. This is the mechanism behind an earlier chapter's finding that a monitored building stops being monitored quickly, and it is not indifference: the sensor stops working before the programme does, and an uncalibrated network keeps producing data, populating a dashboard and looking like monitoring. The failure state of a building sensing system is not silence. It is confident wrong numbers.

Four further computational findings each remove a claim. The digital-twin advocacy review reached does not distinguish measured outcomes in operating buildings from simulations, and attributes one of its improvement figures to thermal mass and passive solar design. A review of about thirty-seven articles on material passports identifies no implemented and verified system anywhere, and a benefit that accrues at end of life cannot be verified until something reaches end of life. No study anywhere took an optimisation-derived building, instrumented it after occupation, and compared its measured performance against the optimisation's own prediction. And the best measured test of a smart-control claim reachable anywhere is six low-energy dwellings, three with smart thermostats and three with standard ones, which found scarcely any difference in indoor temperature and peak summer bedroom temperatures of thirty-four degrees Celsius in both arms. Behind all four sits one absence that would be cheap to close: across every control paper mapped, not one reported the energy consumed by the controller itself, and a saving of two per cent whose computational cost is unreported is not a demonstrated net saving.

The people being sensed were asked once, and their answers are the ethically decisive part. Li, Tavakoli and Heydarian interviewed twenty-four occupants of a university smart office building. All knew about the environmental sensors, and not one person was clear about the purposes the data would serve. Twenty-three of twenty-four accepted environmental sensing. All twenty-four objected to audio, the only unanimous result. Most showed little concern about behavioural inference from environmental sensors and stayed unconcerned when prompted, against an activity-prediction accuracy from such data of nearly seventy-five per cent that the authors themselves cite. So consent to environmental monitoring is not informed consent to activity inference, and the actionable problem is purpose limitation rather than notification. Alongside it sits an institutional absence. The community data governance frameworks developed by Indigenous data sovereignty networks are mature, published and in operational use in biodiversity, fire, health and polar data, and no building sensing system, post-occupancy programme, digital twin, material passport or occupant-centric control anywhere was found operating under any of them. Those frameworks were built for data about people and lands, and building sensor data does not present as being about people. That is the occupants' own inference blindness, at institutional scale.

Which brings the book to its last strand, the record itself. The closing research read the gaps, conflicts and corrections of all thirty-five earlier research files in full before it fetched anything, so every absence reported was found by somebody looking for the thing that was missing. Three of its findings change the shape of the argument. The first is that the blind inter-laboratory round robin this book kept wishing for exists, and was done once. At the Fraunhofer twin houses in Holzkirchen, under an international energy agency annex, twenty-one independent modelling teams received full specifications and boundary data, submitted predictions before the measured interior conditions were released, and resubmitted afterwards so that user error could be separated from model error. That is the strongest instrument for validating building models anybody has built. And the paper reports no team-to-team spread statistic and no error distribution across the twenty-one teams. The companion paper that would carry the spread in the derived heat-loss coefficients is gold open access and could not be reached by any route. That spread is the most valuable unfilled number in this chapter, and it is unfilled.

The second is that mandatory measured disclosure, the best candidate in the world for the thing this book keeps asking for, closed negatively rather than remaining unreached. The twenty-five-year overview of the Australian rating programme was obtained: two thousand two hundred and twenty-eight offices certified in one year on metered bills rather than models, covering more than twenty-four million square metres, and more than four thousand two hundred unique offices across the programme's life. And it reports no counterfactual analysis, no control group and no formal causal inference; it was written by the scheme's original methodology developer, with the interest declared; and its supporting data is confidential, to protect the identification of individual buildings. The world's longest-running mandatory measured-disclosure programme has published its conclusion and withheld its dataset. That the individual buildings deserve protection is legitimate. That the distribution which would identify nobody is also withheld is the finding.

The third keeps the whole book honest, because it corrects the book's own central complaint. The interior is measured at scale. It is measured openly, at a scale that would satisfy any epidemiologist, for exactly one question. The global thermal comfort database holds a hundred and seven thousand four hundred and sixty-three records from twenty-three countries and sixteen climate zones, including window, blind and fan state recorded beside the occupant's thermal sensation, nearly half of it from naturally ventilated buildings, under an open licence. Beside it sits an energy dataset of three thousand and fifty-three meters on one thousand six hundred and thirty-six buildings, hourly for two full years, and an occupant satisfaction survey administered in more than seventy buildings. These are magnificent instruments about the wrong thing. They cannot answer a single question this book's subject turns on. But their existence settles something. The collapse of measurement at the person is not a physical limit. It happened for the question an industry needed answered and not for these. The charge is not that nobody can measure the interior. It is that nobody would.

There is no open-science turn to report either. One recent sole-authored paper argues the case for reproducibility in building performance simulation, with a software census across five hundred and seventy papers, and gives no measured reproducibility rate for the field. No building-science journal currently requires code or data deposit. Reproducibility in building science has not been assessed; it has been raised. There is no ethics literature at all on measuring the people inside buildings. And interior plant mortality, the simplest question in the whole series, was confirmed absent by a third independent route: the horticultural literature gets the plant to the door and stops.

Out of all that comes an agenda: twenty-five research commissions, ranked by how much the answer would change practice, each carrying the provenance of the file that found the absence, so the agenda can be audited rather than believed. The measured mortality and replacement rate of interior plants in occupied buildings. A second visit to the canon, a fixed short protocol published before the first visit and applied unchanged to a dozen famous buildings, which would settle whether the canon is a canon of performance or of intention. An adequately measured room, with daylight separated from view and a non-nature control matched for novelty. A round robin on daylight simulation, which means transplanting a proven instrument rather than designing a study, and publishing the spread between teams as the original exercise did not. And a farm complex in Schleswig-Holstein, the one defensible partnership building in the whole series, which stands empty and has not been measured for climate, ammonia, air change, yields or animal health in forty-seven years. And the property the ranking revealed is the most encouraging thing in the chapter. The most consequential commissions cluster among the cheapest: a log query, a tape measure, some tags and a light meter, a borrowed instrument set and a researcher's year. The one genuinely expensive commission is the adequately measured room. The binding constraint is attention, not money.

This series keeps a line that runs through all eight chapters, the line of what was measured and who is allowed to read it. The first four chapters found the measurement never made. The fifth found it made and enclosed. The sixth found it given away freely and still lost. The seventh found it made once, with nobody funding the second visit. This chapter does not sit in one band. It supplies most of the ones that were missing, and names seven. Measured at the scale of the coupon and asserted at the scale of the building, which is the materials band and the most dangerous in the whole book, because the evidence is good and openly published and the inference from a fourteen-day specimen a centimetre across to a wall expected to last decades is never stated and never tested. Made, and made about a model, which is the control band. Made by two methods that disagree, with the discipline standardised on the cheaper one, which is the future weather file. Made only in the future tense, which is passive survivability. Made, readable, one query away, and simply not quoted, which is the species provision, where no paywall can be blamed. Measured on the artefact and never on the population: occupancy of a box, emergence from a hotel, detections at an underpass, captures at a lamp, and not one source measuring whether any designed provision changed the abundance or viability of the species it was designed for. And made, licensed open, and still undelivered: three documents under open licences, each with exactly one delivery route, none of them obtained. The licence was granted and the file did not arrive. The collapse this series has tracked extends twice more here. It runs street, then room, then person, then the machine doing the thinking. And a fourth time, at the outcome of the non-human occupant, which is the thing the design was for.

So what does an architect who accepts all of this do on Monday. Nine things, each traceable to evidence in this book and each doable on a live project. Ask what was measured on the finished building, by whom, over how long, and where the data is published, and if any of those five has no answer, treat the certificate as a statement of intent. Before citing a study to justify a move, check that its position and its mode match the move's, because a cohort of eight million people living near vegetation does not license an interior planter. Ask for the distance before the number and the denominator before the fraction. Read the funding statement before quoting the figure, and print the sample size beside it. Specify the second visit in the appointment, a named twelve-month return with a stated instrument set, and if the client declines, record that it was offered. Justify interior planting on the grounds that actually hold, which is that people like the room, and price it as a subscription until somebody measures the mortality rate. When a control can be overridden, assume it will be, and design for the overridden state. Use the open datasets as the benchmark rather than the certificate, because both of the big ones are free. Carry the provenance of a rule with the rule, and say when a number is a heuristic. And the last thing, which is not a rule at all. The buildings that are most loved are the least measured, because nobody has anything to gain from the answer. An architect has one lever that needs nobody's permission: measure your own building, publish the number whatever it says, and cite the sample size. A published number with a method and a date outlives a dashboard, and it is the only form of evidence in this entire book that has reliably survived.
