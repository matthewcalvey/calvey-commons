This is the end of the book, and it owes the listener an answer to the question the section before this one left open. If all of that is true, what does an architect do on a Monday morning.

First, a reckoning of where the chapters landed. The first three sat almost entirely in the first band: the measurement was never made. The fourth found the profession commissioning a measurement, receiving a bad answer, and building anyway. The fifth found the measurements arriving in bulk and being enclosed. The sixth found them given away and lost. The seventh, the practice chapter, found five further kinds at once, from the legal limit whose derivation cannot be read, to the cooling effect that vanishes when the sensor moves, to the metric standardised without its own validation, to the second visit nobody funds, to the meter that measures everything and resolves nothing. The eighth, looking forward, found seven more, and the most dangerous of them all: measurement made carefully and openly, about a computer model rather than a building, which cannot be told apart from good science by any test of method. The only test is to ask what physical object the number refers to.

And the two collapses held everywhere. The space between buildings is measured to a fine grain. The rooms people occupy are measured for one question, thermal comfort, and almost nothing else. The people themselves are measured least of all, and the other species a building is designed to shelter are not measured at all in outcome.

So, Monday. Nine things, drawn from the chapters' rulebooks and from the final chapter's research agenda, in the order you would meet them on a project.

One. Ask what physical object the number refers to. When a consultant, a product sheet, a rating or a simulation gives you a figure, ask whether it was measured on a building, on a coupon in a laboratory, on a model, or on nothing. The chapters found that the most confident numbers in this field are frequently the ones furthest from a building. This costs nothing and it is the single habit that would have caught most of what this book found.

Two. Read the grade before the claim. A code requirement, a guideline and a research finding are three different things, and a measured number, a simulated one, a calculated one and a rule of thumb are four more. A rating credit is not a code. A rated score is not a measurement. The chapters graded every claim twice for this reason, and the practice chapter found the profession's standards routinely presenting the fourth kind as the first.

Three. Design the threshold, and then design for it being defeated. The practice chapter found that the line between inside and out is disabled three ways: by the architect for the sake of the elevation, by the operator for the sake of risk, and by the occupant for reasons of their own, and the last of these has been measured at rates high enough to erase the intended performance. Chapter five found the same thing forty years earlier in the first monitored passive houses. It is a durable law. A strategy that depends on a hand opening a window needs to be shown working when the hand does not.

Four. Bring nature inside on the evidence that exists, not the evidence you wish existed. The chapters were even-handed about this. A view of something living, through glass, has the best evidence in the field and it is thin. Plants indoors do not clean the air at any density a room can hold, and the profession's flagship framework grades the material and system claims lowest by its own reckoning. Representation of nature is a preservative, not a restorative: it stops the decline a windowless room causes, and it does not produce the gain a window does. Give the basement its picture; do not trade a window for one.

Five. Treat measurement distance, season, occupancy and funding as part of the number. A green wall cools at thirty centimetres and not at a hundred and fifty. A green roof retains nearly everything in a small storm and half in a large one, and the large one is the one the drainage engineer is designing for. A solar house performs differently with a family in it. A daylight study funded by the maker of the glazing that won reported a result its own authors could not separate from the window. None of these is a trick; each is a condition, and the chapters carry them because a number without its conditions is a slogan.

Six. Specify the year seven. The longevity material found that the components of a living roof drift on different clocks in different directions, that street trees die on a schedule set by who was supposed to water them, and that a monitored building stops being a monitored building within a year because the sensors drift into confident wrong numbers. Whatever living thing you put on or in a building, write down who maintains it, at what frequency, with what skills, and what happens when they stop. The chapters found this written down almost nowhere.

Seven. Hold the conflicts open rather than averaging them. Fire science says keep fine fuel five metres from the wall; much of this book says bring the living world close. Two large studies disagree about what happens past thirty metres. Airtightness wants a sealed envelope; a swift wants a hole in it. A corridor moves native plants and also moves invasive ants. The chapters left every one of these standing, because a designer who has been told the average has been told nothing. Decide knowingly, and say which side you took.

Eight. Cite the community as well as the scholar, and cite the sample size as well as the finding. The book's standing law was that Indigenous, vernacular and community knowledge is cited twice, to the people whose it is and to those who documented it, and the lanes found that law hardest to keep precisely where the evidence was strongest, because the record itself so rarely does it. And a finding of twenty-three matched pairs in one hospital has been carrying an entire field for forty years because nobody kept saying twenty-three.

Nine, and last. Measure your own building. Not a rating, not a certificate, not a model. A meter, a logger, a survey, a year. Then publish the number, whatever it says, in a place that will still exist in ten years, with the sample size beside it. Every chapter of this book found the profession building ahead of its evidence and then failing to go back. The second visit is the cheapest study in this book and almost nobody has made it. If enough people did, the measurement line would move, and the next book on this subject would be a different book.

That is the end. The chapters carry the evidence; this section carried only what to do with it.
