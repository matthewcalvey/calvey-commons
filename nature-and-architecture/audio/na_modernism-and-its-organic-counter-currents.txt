This is the fourth chapter of a series about how people have brought nature into buildings. The series runs from the deep past to the present and then looks ahead, and it asks one question all the way through: how has nature been brought inside, outside and at the threshold of buildings, and what does the record establish for an architect deciding what to do now. This chapter runs from the middle of the First World War to 1970, and it is where the question changes character, because for the first time the evidence exists and it is normal to put an instrument on a building and write down what it says. So the interesting question is no longer whether a measurement was taken, but where the instruments were pointed, who paid for them, and what happened when the answer was unwelcome.

One idea runs through the whole series. There is a spectrum of ways a building can relate to nature. At one end nature is an image: an ornament, a symbol, a picture of a garden on a wall. Then nature is a form, a shape borrowed from a plant or a body. Then nature is a model, a structure or a process imitated and understood. Then nature is material: timber, earth, stone, fibre, living matter. Then nature is climate: sun, wind, water and cold, read and answered by the building. Then nature is a system: water, plants, soil and habitat made into working parts of the building. And at the far end nature is a partner, where the building and the living world give something back to each other. The spectrum is not a ladder of progress. In this chapter it runs backwards as often as forwards, and the building that earns the far end is a cowshed on a Baltic lake.

The period's most consequential idea is that nature can be abolished as a variable altogether. That is what the sealed, mechanically conditioned interior proposed, and it is not the climate end of the spectrum but the removal of climate.

Start with the claim. Sun, space and greenery was a hygienic doctrine with a nineteenth-century medical ancestry, carrying no number and no authority. The first correction this chapter owes its reader is that the Athens Charter is not a 1933 collective document: the proceedings of that congress went unpublished for a decade and were issued by Le Corbusier alone, heavily edited, in 1943. In Germany the orientation arguments were worked hard, by Gropius at Karlsruhe from 1928 and by Ernst May's New Frankfurt, which produced around 12,000 dwellings between 1925 and 1930; the best-targeted source on the dispute between north-south and east-west layouts is closed with no repository copy and no abstract, and its substance stays unfilled. These arguments were analytical: they answer how much direct sun reaches a window on a given day, and never what the indoor temperature is or whether anybody is comfortable. No microclimate or indoor study of any of those estates was located. And Bruno Taut, remembered for a glass pavilion and for mountain fantasies, built over 12,000 dwellings for one Berlin housing association between 1924 and 1931, more than Wright, Aalto, Häring and Scharoun combined.

Now the sealed wall, which is this chapter's spine, and where two ideas are routinely run together. Respiration exacte, also called air exact, is a sealed building supplied throughout with mechanically conditioned air at one condition, a constant supply at 18 degrees Celsius, on a technique attributed to the ventilation engineer Gustave Lyon and conceived in 1928 with Pierre Jeanneret; it is explicitly universal, one indoor condition for all users everywhere in the world. The mur neutralisant is a double wall with conditioned air circulated in the cavity, its inner skin working as a radiant panel. One source gives the control target as that inner surface held at 18 degrees, which is a surface temperature and not the supply temperature another source reports, and both readings stand.

Then somebody tested it. In the Saint-Gobain laboratories, in 1931 and 1932, an engineer named in the literature as Le Braz, under Lyon, built a chamber of roughly 4 metres by 2 by 3 high holding a double-glazed panel of about 1.5 metres by 2.5. Hot air was circulated between the panes, the outdoor side was chilled with dry ice for the low temperatures, and up to 150 litres a second passed through the glazed sections, at 15 degrees Celsius for the summer case and 30 for winter, with cavity depth varied. The 1932 report concluded that the system would need very much more steam and more than twice the fan energy of a conventional installation. A second modern reading gives more than four times the steam. The report itself is unopened and the two readings differ, so this chapter prints no single multiplier and marks the steam figure unfilled. Two things follow. The evidence existed before the building did, which inverts the nineteenth century, which built ahead of evidence that did not yet exist. And the negative result was a warning about a whole architecture, not one device: if a conditioned cavity cannot economically neutralise a large glazed wall in Paris, a large unshaded glazed wall is a problem there whatever is done behind it, and that generalisation was available in 1932 and was not drawn.

The Cité de Refuge in Paris, a shelter for the Salvation Army, had its first design meeting in May 1929 and was completed in December 1933. What was built in place of the double skin was a single layer of toughened glass 7 millimetres thick, about a thousand square metres on the south façade, with no shading and no cooling. The neutralising wall was not built in reduced form. It was not built. The plant became a simple steam boiler giving about one air change an hour in winter and two to three and a half in summer, off overnight. And on the file, in advance, sat a warning: the American Blower Company had criticised the proposed flow rate as too low and recommended ten times that rate. A second report from the same company, commissioned for this building, survives in no record of its contents.

The building overheated at once. Readings after the opening record peak carbon dioxide of 2,720 parts per million in a fourth-floor mother-and-baby roomette, and temperatures of 30 to 33 degrees Celsius in the roomettes, the playroom and the children's dormitory. These are spot values, not a campaign, and season, duration, occupancy and instrument are not established. It matters who the people in this record were: homeless women, men and infants, who could not leave and could not open a window. Their complaints are the building's primary environmental record. The repairs are a chronology that does not agree with itself, and all versions stand: operable windows in 1935 as a remedy, on one account; on another, a bomb on 25 August 1944 and repairs including a sun-break added between 1948 and 1952; on a third, a sun-break of 1952 by the engineer Vladimir Bodiansky with Iannis Xenakis, with the Salvation Army insisting the lower third of each façade level be made opaque. What all accounts share is the direction of travel: the building was conceived sealed, mechanical and universal, and repaired by being made openable and shaded, which are the two devices the concept had rejected.

Then, in 2014 and 2015, it was modelled twice, and the models disagree with each other and with the 1932 test. One whole-building simulation found it as built with a roomette winter minimum as low as 11 degrees Celsius and a summer maximum of 32, the childcare spaces each about a third of occupied hours below 20 degrees and a third above 25, and a simulated men's dormitory with carbon dioxide peaking around 10,000 parts per million; running the intended air system at the recommended flow rate still left the roomette peaking at 31 degrees and raised energy use by between 370 and 518 per cent, while adding a sun-break eliminated the overheating and cut energy by 65 per cent. The other study, a coupled model of the wall and the air system together, concluded that operative temperatures in summer and winter would both have fallen within the comfort range. Underneath that dispute is the finding to keep. In 1932 there was a physical test of a physical wall. In 2015 there are two computer models of a wall that no longer exists, in a building bombed, repaired and twice renovated, which cannot be calibrated because nobody monitored the building. The evidential quality of the record has gone down, not up.

The brise-soleil is the reversal, and the reversal is what makes this period's environmental history interesting. It has no single inventor: three accounts stand side by side — a skyscraper project for Algiers in 1938, an evolution of the long horizontal window of 1927 with a 1932 Algerian precedent, and an Arab and Iberian lineage of screens credited to no individual. Niemeyer's Obra do Berço in Rio of 1937 and Antonin Raymond's ashram at Pondicherry of 1936 to 1944 are contemporaneous, so Rio is the first monumental case and not the first case.

The Ministry of Education and Health in Rio is dated 1937 to 1942 by the Museum of Modern Art, with other dates in circulation that this chapter records rather than resolves. Lúcio Costa led the team, and Le Corbusier's involvement was a one-month consultancy in 1936. And then there is the sentence this chapter keeps returning to. Costa's project memoir states, in substance, that because this method of sun protection had not been tested, the type to be used would need careful study. The designer of the first major brise-soleil building wrote down that the device was untested. It was untested then, and as far as two independent searches establish it has not been field-tested on that building since.

The geometry is known: louvres on the north-north-west façade at a latitude given as about 20 degrees south, horizontal rotating blades at three positions on hand levers that office workers turned through the day, the south façade fully glazed and unshaded, offices about 9 metres deep. The one technical study of those louvres computed photic fields at solstices and equinoxes. Then the finding that matters: for most of the summer months those louvres receive no direct solar radiation on that façade, so they are not justified there as solar protection, and their dark colour degrades the daylight balance. Against that sits the Museum of Modern Art's public catalogue, which states without measurement or citation that workers adjusting the louvres prevented overheating and kept comfort without air conditioning.

After Rio the device acquired a method, and the method stayed analytical: the climatic grid of 1951, prepared with the comfort engineer André Missenard, set the four parameters thermal comfort standards still use, and a grid selects a solution from climate data without measuring it once built. The modern defence of this work is itself simulation. The headline absence stands after repeated searching by two independent lanes. There is no published monitoring campaign on the Unité at Marseille, the Chandigarh Capitol, the Millowners' Association Building, the Ahmedabad villas or the Cité de Refuge, and for the Rio building only a conservation paper from 2025. The most copied climatic device of the twentieth century appears never to have been the subject of a published field-monitoring study on any canonical building. Where the reputation is highest, the measurement is absent.

The other wing of the period excluded nature entirely, and it had better instruments. Willis Carrier submitted drawings for the first modern air-conditioning system on 17 July 1902, for a lithography company whose paper expanded with humidity and ruined colour registration, and presented his psychrometric formulae in 1911. The target variable was humidity, not temperature, and comfort was a later application of a process designed for paper and textiles. The side that excluded nature had validated physics by 1911, before the Bauhaus and before the five points; the side that celebrated nature had solar geometry, conviction and one unfavourable laboratory report it disregarded. The conditioned box was a parallel programme with better instruments, not a betrayal of modernism that happened afterwards.

The commercial fluorescent lamp dates from 1938, and the claim that it released plan depth from the reach of daylight could not be documented quantitatively from any opened source. What is verified is the consequence: by 1965 office workers were systematically overestimating the daylight component beyond about twenty feet from a window, and the error did not reduce their preference for windows. Twenty feet is the horizon the lamp abolished as a planning constraint, and the canonical towers did not exploit it: Lever House was planned so every office was within twenty-five feet of a window. These are shallow daylit plans behind sealed glass. What those towers abolished was not daylight. It was control.

The United Nations Secretariat is where the argument happened at the façade. It opened on 21 August 1950, with a board of design that included both Niemeyer and Le Corbusier — two of the Rio ministry's designers on the board of the building that became the emblem of the unshaded glass tower. Le Corbusier argued for sun-breaks and also, in a letter of 4 December 1947, protested the rejection of the neutralising wall for the same building; he pressed both devices and lost on both. And the disagreement led the architects to build a full-scale mock-up of the proposed façade on the roof of a nearby Manhattan building to test the idea. What that mock-up recorded is unfilled: no opened source reports what was measured, by whom, over what period, or what it showed. If readings were taken and the sun-break rejected anyway, this chapter has its closing argument. If none were taken, the defining environmental decision of the post-war office building was settled by assertion on both sides.

Here the received story needs correcting, and the correction is load-bearing. The Secretariat was not a sealed curtain wall. The body that took the wall apart documents single-pane tinted vision glass and operable double-hung windows in the original design; a second account agrees on tinted heat-absorbing glass and on windows that opened and disagrees on whether the glazing was single or double, and both are recorded. Its services are the diagnostic detail: 2,300 tons of cooling, high-velocity units serving the perimeter within twelve feet of the glass and low-velocity ceiling units inboard. When a building splits its air system into a perimeter system and an interior system, the envelope is a thermal problem. The sealed tower is Lever House, of 1950 to 1952, whose panes cannot be opened and whose air conditioning was required precisely because of that.

Seagram is where the occupant loses the controls. Opened on 22 May 1958: windows permanently sealed, air from plants twenty floors away, ceiling panels on a timer. And the blinds have three permitted positions, fully open, half open and fully closed, so that the elevation reads uniformly from outside. The last environmental control at the desk was constrained for the sake of the building's appearance to people who are not in it, and Rio's hand-levered louvres, fifteen years and a hemisphere away, are the same device with the agency taken out. On the spectrum these towers are image, not climate. The curtain wall is the century's most expensive landscape painting.

What did the sealed interior do to people? The answer arrives late, and it is attitudinal rather than instrumented, which is itself the finding. A survey published by the United States National Bureau of Standards in 1975 gathered the studies. In 1965 one found 65 per cent of subjects wanted daylight, and it was sponsored by a flat-glass manufacturer, which this chapter says; the same year another found 89 per cent thought it desirable to look outside even with adequate artificial light. In 1970 a study of 139 women working in windowless offices in five Seattle buildings found 90 per cent dissatisfied with the absence of windows and 35 per cent naming it unprompted as their chief dislike. And the crucial result: a nearby window, brighter lighting or warm colours did not change the dislike, which runs directly against the industry's habitual remedy.

Three things follow. On performance, windowlessness barely registers; on preference and wellbeing it registers overwhelmingly, and an architecture optimised on the first finding and indifferent to the second is what got built. And the dates are the argument, because the surveys run from 1965 to 1975 and the towers are 1950, 1952 and 1958, so the research asking whether people could live in the box was published between seven and twenty-five years after the box was built. Both wings of this chapter built first and asked afterwards, and sick building syndrome, usually narrated as a consequence of 1970s energy conservation, was already documented in sealed and windowless environments in the 1960s.

Against all that ran the organic counter-current, where the claims are largest and the measurements strangest. Wright coined the word as an extension of the principle that form follows function, and warned against reading organic as a synonym for natural, which removes by his own definition any obligation for an organic building to perform in its climate. Haring's version means a plan developed from the demands of site and client; Steiner's claims metamorphosis and does not survive the scholarship. Neither is a climate claim.

Fallingwater is the most instrumented building here, and the instruments were pointed at a structural failure. Cracks appeared in the parapets on 30 October 1936, before completion, and engineers in 1995 found one corner of the master terrace 7 inches down and another 5.5 over a 15-foot cantilever, nearly two degrees of tilt, and beams at 95 per cent of ultimate with no safety factor left. Seventeen months of continuous monitoring with crack meters and tilt meters resolving to a thousandth of an inch showed the structure still moving downward, not settling, and the repair of 2001 and 2002 applied about 390 tons per girder of post-tensioning. There is no continuous logged creep series across the decades: there are dated crack observations, survey differences, a seventeen-month window and a further campaign from 2013. And not one measurement in the file concerns the interior, temperature having been logged only so that thermal cycling could be subtracted from the deflection signal. The most famous building in the world for its relationship to water is metered to a thousandth of an inch for how it bends, and not at all for what it is like to be inside.

Jacobs II in Wisconsin is Wright's one explicit passive-solar house, designed in February 1944 and built between 1946 and 1948: a semicircular plan with a south glass wall 48 feet wide and 14 tall, a north earth berm, a five-foot south eave, a slab with a radiant system. The odd argument is Wright's claim that the sunken garden would deflect wind up and over the roof like an aerofoil, a testable proposition that in eighty years has never seen a wind tunnel, a model or a site anemometer. The operating record is two opposed accounts, both of which stand: the family reported that in deep winter the heating could be turned off at nine in the morning and left off until late afternoon, while a later owner, there from 1982 to 1989, recorded the house as persistently cold on about 3,500 gallons of heating oil a year. Nothing reconciles them, because no monitoring campaign or heat balance of this house has ever been made: its reputation rests on the first occupants' unaided recollection, and the only quantitative figure points the other way. And Wright is not the originator of the American passive-solar house: George Fred Keck's 1933 demonstration and his Glenview house of 1939 or 1940 precede Wright's design by five to eleven years, and no instrumented measurement of any Keck building exists in anything reachable.

Taliesin West, offered as a desert climate building, had canvas roofs that came down for the hot months, and its fellowship spent seven of every twelve months in Wisconsin: the credentials of a building evacuated for its hard season are earned in the easy one. Johnson Wax holds the one piece of genuine model-grade evidence: the state commission refused approval for the slender columns because they could not carry the 12 short tons specified, so a full-size prototype was poured in May 1937 and loaded with sand until it carried 60 short tons without strain. Because it was never loaded to failure it establishes a lower bound on capacity, not a capacity. In the same building the 43 miles of glass tubing leaked, fell from the ceiling and produced glare, and no photometric survey of that room was ever made.

Aalto's Paimio Sanatorium, of 1929 to 1933, holds this chapter's most careful room and its emptiest evidence. The patient room was worked through for a person lying flat for months: the ceiling coloured to avoid glare, the lamps out of the line of sight, a noiseless basin angled so water struck obliquely, a double-glazed window with permanent ventilation, sun balconies so weak patients could be wheeled out in bed. The balcony's defining dimension appears in no opened source and stays unfilled, the same gap the previous chapter recorded for the German and Swiss sanatoria. The regime itself is disproved by the mortality evidence carried forward from that chapter, and Paimio stopped being a sanatorium in the early 1960s because antibiotics arrived — a verdict on the regime, not the room, and not one of Aalto's decisions has ever been evaluated. Paimio's status is not disproved. It is never examined.

Scharoun's Berlin Philharmonie, of 1960 to 1963, inverts everything else here. Its language is landscape; its acoustics were engineered by a named specialist, Lothar Cremer, against a numerical target of about two seconds of mid-frequency reverberation, with free-hanging reflectors, prismatic resonators and absorbent upholstery under the folding seats. The conditions behind that figure are unfilled. But the structure is clear: a metaphor at the front, and the performance separately engineered and verified at the back. Set sixteen organic buildings against their claims and two have no gap: Villa Mairea, whose claim was modest, and Gut Garkau, Hugo Haring's cowshed on a Schleswig-Holstein lake, whose plan was generated by 42 dairy cows and a bull around a central feed table and which worked for forty-seven years, from 1926 to 1973, without one measurement of temperature, humidity, ammonia, air change, animal health or milk yield; two have an inverted gap where performance exceeded the claim, Johnson Wax structurally and the Philharmonie acoustically; and twelve are claimed higher on the spectrum than they operate, with the error running the same way every time, toward system and climate and away from form and image. Nobody ever claimed a building was merely beautiful when it was in fact ecologically embedded.

The solar house is where real numbers first arrive, and where the metric moves under them. Solar research at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology began in 1936 and was endowed in 1938 by a carbon-black fortune, and six experimental houses were built under it, not four. Solar House II divided a laboratory into seven sections with different collector and storage configurations on south-facing walls, the earliest controlled comparative experiment on solar collection in a building; it failed as the discipline has failed ever since, the collector working by day and the assembly giving the gains back at night, and no per-section number survives. Solar House III, converted in 1949, was the first one lived in, by a graduate student and his family, and reported 82 per cent of its heat from the sun at peak against a 90 per cent target; it burned in December 1955. Solar House IV at Lexington, completed in 1959 with 640 square feet of collector and tanks of 1,500 and 275 gallons, reported 57 per cent of the building's heat during winter across three heating seasons, and then its solar system was removed.

Set those two numbers side by side and do not average them. Eighty-two per cent is a peak in an occupied house; 57 per cent is a seasonal figure across three winters, and whether that house was occupied while monitored is not established, which is the most damaging unresolved caveat in the lane. The apparent regression may be entirely an artefact of the metric changing, and that is the finding: the series cannot be read as a progress curve, because the measurement protocol did not hold still.

The Dover Sun House of 1948 was built by three women. Amelia Peabody financed and sited it, Eleanor Raymond designed it, and Mária Telkes engineered its heating from the metallurgy department, because the solar committee had already removed her after a storage failure in 1946. It had an 800-square-foot vertical double-glass collector on the south wall and about 3,500 gallons of Glauber's salt in metal drums, and the press reported a house comfortably warm all winter without a furnace. The salt stratified into liquid and solid layers, the drums corroded and leaked, and by the third winter the family had electric heaters in every room. The headline claim is true as stated and misleading as heard, because it excludes the electricity for the circulation fans, reported as substantial, and a performance claim that excludes parasitic electricity is not a performance claim. And the failure is a cycle-life failure, not a peak-performance failure, which a single-winter protocol cannot see: the publicity and the collapse fall on either side of a test that was never long enough. The dispute that followed was settled by a dean's report in 1953 and a dismissal, with no measurement adjudicating between the positions: the central technical dispute of American solar-house research was resolved administratively. Two absences travel with it. No summer measurement of any building in the lane was located anywhere, and Harry Thomason's Solaris houses from 1959 belong beside these and yielded no opened measurements. The mid-1940s commercial campaign that popularised the phrase solar house was sponsored by a company that sold glass, and its title as usually given is almost certainly wrong, because it names more states than the country had.

The building science of the same decades resolves into one asymmetry, and that is the chapter's central diagram. The Olgyay brothers are remembered for a climate-simulating machine, and the archival account is different: a National Science Foundation grant of nineteen thousand dollars in 1955 for a two-year study of the thermal behaviour of buildings by models; the grant expired by 1957; the device was never completed; it was described in an appendix to a book in 1963, six years after the money ended. What defeated it was thermal scaling, because geometric, dynamic and thermal similarity are all required and thermal similarity proved unattainable. And when the Olgyays applied for a follow-on grant to establish the similarity criteria, that grant was denied. So no error figure exists and none can now be recovered. It should not be called a working instrument.

Now set that against daylight. A daylight factor is the illuminance due to daylight at a point on the indoor working plane divided by the simultaneous outdoor illuminance under an unobstructed overcast hemisphere. It is a ratio, depending on geometry, reflectance and transmittance, all of which survive when a room is built at one to fifty, so a model under a sky of the right luminance distribution yields the real number. Heat arrives by conduction, convection and radiation at once; the three obey different similarity laws; thermal capacity scales with volume and surface exchange with area; no single reduction preserves all three. That is why the daylight side of this chapter describes a discipline that produced a code-grade number in twenty years and the solar side a discipline that produced an equation, six demolished or stripped houses and an uncalibrated machine, and why thermal building science had to wait for the digital computer and lighting science did not.

Outdoors, the same period produced the strongest measured case in the chapter and its sharpest reversal. The designed landscape of these decades — Jensen's prairie parks, Church's Californian garden, Eckbo's shade planting for migrant workers' camps, Kiley's bosques, Barragán's walls and water, Burle Marx at Copacabana — carries between all of them not one instrumented microclimate measurement. At The Sea Ranch, where the legend is that wind studies produced the siting, what the sources verify is that the houses were grouped between hedgerows the sheep ranchers had already planted as windbreaks, with no anemometer, wind rose or numerical result in any opened source.

The federal made ground is different, because it was measured. The Tennessee Valley Authority begins with a rare quantified baseline — around 30 per cent of the affected population with malaria, average rural income of 639 dollars a year, 10 per cent of forest lost to fire each year — and it also displaced more than 125,000 people, roughly 15,000 families; a chapter that prints the reforestation figures and not that one is not telling the truth about made ground. In the Dust Bowl, drought ran from 1930 for about a decade across 100 million acres, with more than 75 per cent of the topsoil gone from many regions, and by 1938 blowing soil was reported reduced by 65 per cent — at programme level, by the implementing agency, in the years the drought partly broke. The Prairie States Forestry Project planted 220 million trees in 30,233 shelterbelts between 1935 and 1942, and no survival census appears anywhere, which for a programme whose output is living organisms is the most consequential missing number here. The Civilian Conservation Corps planted over 3 billion, with peak enrolment of 505,782 men in August 1935, of whom 70 per cent arrived malnourished and roughly 15,000 were Native Americans in a separate division whose own scholarship the research could not reach, so the double citation this book requires cannot be supplied here.

The aerodynamics of a windbreak are well measured and consistent: barrier height sets every distance, the deepest reduction sits between two and five barrier heights leeward, the agronomically useful zone extends to ten or twelve, and measurable reduction can be detected out to twenty or thirty-five, so quoting thirty heights as a design protection distance misuses a detection limit. Dense barriers of 60 to 80 per cent give the greatest reduction over the shortest distance; evaporation falls by as much as 20 to 30 per cent leeward, roughly with the square root of wind speed; and weighted crop yield increases are reported at 12 per cent for corn, 13 for soybeans, 25 for barley and 23 for winter wheat.

Then comes the finding that inverts the received account. A study published in 2021 took 234 counties across the Great Plains, 117 heavily treated and 117 control neighbours, with county panel data from 1910 to 1992, using the politically drawn hundred-mile planting zone as an instrument. For every ten-percentage-point increase in shelterbelt coverage, crop revenue fell by 8 to 12 per cent in the 1950s and 1960s, and by over 20 per cent in the western, drier counties, while animal-product revenue rose by 7 to 13 points and cattle numbers by 6 to 10 per cent. The stated mechanism is that the tree rows physically obstructed the sprinkler irrigation systems adopted in the 1950s, and the shelterbelt counties show significantly reduced irrigation adoption. Both literatures are true and they point in opposite directions: the windbreak demonstrably reduces wind, cuts evaporation and raises plot yield, and the counties with most of it earned less from crops, because a row of trees obstructs a centre-pivot sprinkler and irrigation turned out to be worth more than shelter. A designed landscape correct at the microclimatic scale, defeated at the system scale by a technology that did not exist when it was planted. A designer reading both will ask what machine has to move through this landscape in thirty years.

Indoors, the plant became architecture and the claim was tested and lost. The office landscape, developed from 1958 by a consultancy outside Hamburg, put large potted plants on the plan as visual barriers and space definers, and the verified source says visual only, with no opened source attributing any acoustic function to them, so the acoustic claim that circulates should not be printed as fact. The one contemporaneous evaluation surveyed 120 employees before a conversion and nine months after, and found judgements of aesthetic value up significantly and judgements of functional efficiency down significantly: claimed at system, evidenced at image, tested inside the period and lost. The Ford Foundation atrium of 1967 has one genuinely systemic element that is almost never mentioned: rainwater collected from the roof into a basement tank, and a cistern collecting interior condensation, both feeding the sprinklers under the garden. Its only published environmental number is a relative humidity ranging from 10 per cent in winter to 50 per cent in summer, with no investigator, instrument, period or height stated, in a volume a hundred and sixty feet tall where stratification is certain. Into that went saucer magnolias, which need winter chill and outdoor humidity, and eucalyptus brought from California; both failed, for opposite reasons, and it took fifty years and a substitution to subtropical species to match the garden to its own climate. Dan Kiley anticipated a struggle in which only the fittest would survive, in a building whose interior climate had not been characterised; he measured nothing first, and no thermal intent for that garden appears in any near-contemporary account: both sources describe it as social. The same silence covers the period's two enclosures for plants. The Climatron in St Louis, whose geodesic design is by Thomas Howard of Synergetics, opened in 1960 claiming to be the world's first completely air-conditioned greenhouse, and it has no published climate data and no located instrument. Eleven years earlier the world's first phytotron was built at the California Institute of Technology, and there the discovery was made that plants respond differently to day and to night temperature: one was a public spectacle that won an architecture prize and left no reachable data, and the other was a windowless research building nobody photographs, which produced the knowledge the interior gardens of the 1960s were built without.

That leaves the tropics, where the measurement is real, abundant and attached to the wrong objects. Tropical architecture in this period is not a climate zone but a category of governance, because its institutions, funding, students and commissions were organised around the political map of empire rather than any map of climate. A Conference on Tropical Architecture was held in March 1953 and is the founding event; out of it came a course at the Architectural Association from 1954, headed in its initial years by Maxwell Fry, with Otto Koenigsberger from 1957. The curriculum was developed with an officer of the British building research station whose remit was colonial building, and a hygienist approach imported from the London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine played a significant part in it: the design rules and the disease rules came down the same pipe. The department ran no instrumented programme that any opened source describes; it sat downstream of measurement and upstream of building, and closed neither loop.

The measurement that does exist is in medical journals. The same building research station had an officer taking thermal comfort observations in Singapore in 1949 and 1950, published in 1959 in a journal of industrial medicine, where he built a replacement comfort index because the established one did not track sensation. In Australia in 1963 a study at a mission settlement in north Queensland found that the threshold at which more than half the men reported comfort was 27.5 degrees Celsius by day and 25.5 at night. Those are the only period comfort figures in this chapter, and two things travel with them. A threshold near 27.5 degrees is substantially warmer than the set points air-conditioned tropical buildings were subsequently given, so the measured evidence did not require the cooling that followed it. And the subjects at that mission were the settlement's white residents, by the paper's own categorisation, which is the empirical substance behind the argument that this science measured European bodies. The lesson is also bibliographic: this period's tropical building science sits in the hygiene and industrial-medicine journals. Two institutional corrections go with it: the building research station at Garston and the building research body at Haifa share a name and nothing else, and the Australian body in the literature is the Commonwealth Experimental Building Station.

Between the comfort study and the building there is a gap, and the manuals filled it with judgement and called it science. Fry and Drew's three books, of 1947, 1956 and 1964, could not be opened, so what rules they stated stays unfilled; they were written by practising architects, published commercially and not peer-reviewed, and the only measured literature available to them concerned human tolerance and not envelope performance. Koenigsberger's own career supplies the best-documented technical failure in the lane: a prefabricated housing factory at Jungpura in Delhi from 1949, whose autoclaved foamed concrete panels cracked, producing a parliamentary controversy and his resignation in August 1951. A specific, physical failure with a named material and failure mode is better evidence than any success story here, and it happened to the man who would write the discipline's manual.

That manual, published in 1973 or 1974 and reviewed in 1975, appeared after the department that produced it had closed. Its procedure, the Mahoney tables, converts monthly meteorological data into recommendations on layout, orientation, openings, walls, roofs and shading. The thresholds at which a climate statistic becomes a recommendation have no derivation that could be established from any opened source and stay unfilled, and there is no visible peer-reviewed publication record behind the instrument at all. Compared against a modern procedure for one Egyptian site it agreed about 30 per cent of the time on natural ventilation and about 10 per cent on passive solar heating, with roughly 70 per cent of its output not matching — which does not show the tables wrong, but shows two unvalidated procedures disagreeing with no measurement refereeing between them. It is nonetheless still in active use in 2026, from Ghana to Shanghai to Hamadan to India and south-west Nigeria. A design instrument devised inside a colonial-era teaching department, never refereed and never validated against instrumented building performance, is still the yardstick by which buildings are judged climate-responsive.

And the buildings themselves were never measured. Charles Correa's Tube House in Ahmedabad, from about 1961, is a narrow deep plan under a sloping roof with a vent at the high end intended to drive ventilation without machinery, and its architect published performance claims for that section; the figures he claimed are unfilled here rather than reproduced from memory. Three separately phrased searches returned no study of its climate performance, by him or by anybody since. An explicit, falsifiable, published claim about air movement in a real building was allowed to stand for six decades without anybody putting an anemometer in it. The same null returns for Fry and Drew's West African schools, for any Bawa or de Silva building, for Hassan Fathy's market at New Baris with its wind-catchers, and for the Rio ministry. Fathy's village at New Gourna, dated variously from 1942 or 1946 to 1948 or 1952, has been surveyed for conservation and not for performance, and that survey names groundwater among the problems, because a mud-brick village on a floodplain beside irrigated land attacks its own walls from below.

The period's signature comfort index was doubted from within by measurement twice: by that Singapore work, and again in 1984 by a Sri Lankan study which found, across close to a hundred occasions in two subjects over a year, that comfort temperature shifted with a season that barely shifts, and that the composite index revealed no more than plain dry-bulb temperature did. Two independent studies, a quarter-century and an ocean apart, reached the same verdict, and the design manuals went on using it. How to read the whole apparatus is contested and this chapter does not settle it: as an instrument of colonial power, as a network of circulating people and publications, as a period of West African production now inherited, as postcolonial models, or as environmental nationalism. That the machinery was colonial is not interpretation, given a curriculum co-written by a colonial-liaison officer and the Portuguese state sending architects on the course for territories it had no intention of relinquishing.

This series keeps a line running through all eight chapters, the line of what was measured and who can read it. The earlier chapters found the measurement never made. This chapter owns the second band on that line and names it: commissioned, answered unfavourably, and built anyway. A sealed double-skin wall was tested at full scale in a manufacturer's laboratory in 1931 and 1932; the report said it needed very much more steam and more than twice the fan energy of ordinary heating; the building was completed in December 1933 with the double skin deleted and the seal kept, and it overheated at once. Across the rest of the chapter the first band still governs: the brise-soleil, the organic canon, the tropical monuments, the designed landscapes and the interior gardens have no field measurement between them. And where the numbers do arrive, in the solar houses and the daylight laboratories, they arrive with the metric changing under the data, the occupants not independent of the experiment, the auxiliary system left out of the accounting, and the daylight factor alone reaching code grade. Measurement density collapses at the façade and again at the person, and this is where that collapse first becomes visible against a background of measurement that exists.

What carries forward is this. The evidence base for modernism's climatic devices has degraded rather than improved: a physical test in 1932, and two contradictory models in 2015 of a wall that no longer exists. Daylight became a code-grade measured science and heat did not, and the reason is physics, so thermal building science had to wait for the digital computer and the next chapter receives it there. Twelve of sixteen organic buildings are claimed higher on the spectrum than they operate, always in the same direction. The tropical discipline measured people, climates and materials, wrote rules, and never closed the loop by measuring the buildings. The science that arrived was thermodynamic and not ecological, which is why the environmental architecture of the 1970s is so mechanical and so uninterested in living systems: the instruments it inherited could only see heat and light. It inherited something else as well, which the next chapter will use — thirty-five years of federal soil surveys, erosion surveys, hydrological records and forest inventories, the datasets landscape architecture would shortly discover it needed and did not have to make. And the best candidate in this entire book for a commissioned monitoring campaign is a cowshed, empty since 1976, publicly protected, holding the one partner claim the record still needs to test.
