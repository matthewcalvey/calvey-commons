This is the second chapter of a series about how people have brought nature into buildings. The first chapter ran from the painted caves to about the year five hundred and found a record rich in ideas and almost empty of measurement. This chapter takes up the twelve and a half centuries that follow, from roughly five hundred to seventeen fifty, and it covers the buildings that most people mean when they say that traditional architecture knew how to work with climate: the wind tower, the courtyard, the cave house, the skywell, the screen, the stepwell, the paradise garden, the earthen mosque, the cliff dwelling, the snow house and the turf farm. Six lanes of research went looking, in every one of those traditions, for the instrumented study behind the reputation. What they brought back is the subject of this chapter, and it is not what the reputation predicts.

Before anything else, the idea that runs through the whole series. There is a spectrum of ways a building can relate to nature. At one end nature is an image: a carved leaf, a painted garden, a woven pattern. Then nature is a form, a shape borrowed from a plant or a body. Then nature is a model, a structure or a process or a cosmos imitated. Then nature is material: earth, stone, timber, thatch, coral, snow. Then nature is climate, read and answered by the fabric. Then nature is a system, with water and soil and plants made into working parts of the building. And at the far end nature is a partner, where the building and the living world give something back to each other. The spectrum is not a ladder. In this chapter, as in the last, the most integrated end is frequently the oldest, and the most interesting cases are the ones where a tradition is celebrated at one point on the spectrum and measurably operates at another.

Here is the honest limit, stated first. This research was done without web search, and the six lanes exhausted the session's search allowance part way through their run, so several subjects were lost not because the sources do not exist but because the lanes could not reach them: the English deer park, the Thule sod and whalebone winter house, the Taino house of the Caribbean, the Maya house with its thatch, and Sami dwellings. Those are retrieval failures and the chapter names them as such. The Sami case is different. The house law under which this research was done requires that Indigenous and community knowledge be cited both to the community that holds it and to whoever documented it. The lane could not reach a single Sami-authored source before its budget ran out, and rather than paraphrase Sami building knowledge from an encyclopaedia and a design magazine it recorded what it had found and stopped. That is the correct outcome, not a hole in the chapter.

Begin in Iran, with the building this chapter most needed to test. The wind tower, the badgir, rises above the roof of a courtyard house in Yazd or Kerman, open at the top and open at the bottom into the rooms below. The popular account is that it scoops the wind and drives it down as a cooling draught. In the winter of two thousand and fourteen and two thousand and fifteen a team led by Zohreh Hedayat instrumented one of them, the tower of the Mortaz House in Yazd, a rectangular tower just over six metres by three and a half holding six internal shafts, with twenty-six calibrated sensors logging every minute for three months. What they found is that the tower delivered air between half a degree and four and a half degrees Celsius cooler than outside during the warmest hours of the day, and between half a degree and three degrees warmer than outside at night. The largest daytime difference came in mid-afternoon. The mechanism the authors credit is not the wind but the mass of the tower walls, charging with heat through the day and discharging it after dark. The best instrumented historic wind tower in the literature behaves less like a scoop than like a thermal battery standing in a chimney, and the night-time warming is that battery's signature. Two cautions travel with the number. It is a winter campaign, so four and a half degrees is not a summer figure; and the much larger figures that circulate for wind towers, twelve degrees, fifteen, even twenty, belong to modern augmented devices in models and laboratories, or to a single second-hand report of a tower coupled to an underground water channel whose original measurement nobody could reach.

The other Persian devices are in the same condition. Shahin Attia's wind-tunnel work on the Egyptian roof scoop, at one twentieth scale with smoke visualisation, is a real measurement, and its lesson is that the scoop is half a circuit: a single inlet raised flow by two hundred per cent, two facing the wind by two hundred and forty, and the best case of all, one facing into the wind and one away from it, reached five and a half air changes an hour. The device depends on its outlet as much as its inlet. The underground cistern has been modelled but never, in anything reached, had a thermometer put in its water. The ice house, the yakhchal, has never been instrumented at all. An engineer named Hamid Pochee modelled it: a shallow shaded pond of about four hundred square metres beside a massive conical store over a pit five metres deep, walls two metres thick at the base, making about fifty cubic metres of ice a winter and losing about a fifth of it by September. The dominant freezing mechanism in that model is not evaporation, the usual popular explanation, but radiative loss from the pond to a very cold clear desert night sky. A four-hundred-square-metre radiator aimed at the sky, and nobody has ever measured one.

The courtyard is the period's most widespread device and the evidence about it is genuinely divided. In Granada, in September and October of nineteen ninety-seven, Beatriz Jimenez Alcala put data loggers and spot instruments through the Alhambra and found the halls of the Court of Comares five to six degrees below the outside air and the Hall of the Two Sisters eight to eleven degrees below, with the patio itself about three degrees below at the peak and a lag of at least four hours. Afternoon humidity in the patio ran well above the outside air and converged only in the evening, which is the evaporative fingerprint of the pools and the jets. Her conclusion is that none of the three mechanisms she identifies, ventilation, evaporation and the thermal capacity of the fabric, would produce that result alone. Then set beside it the work of Amr Abdallah and colleagues in Upper Egypt, published in two thousand and twenty-five, in which two adobe courtyard houses near Luxor and Aswan were measured and modelled. The interiors ran between two point seven and six point seven degrees below the outdoor air, which is a real and useful result. But in the base case the courtyard itself ran about three tenths of a degree hotter than the outdoor reference. The cooling belonged to the mass and to the shaded interior; the hole in the plan did not, by itself, cool anything. Both results stand. The difference is probably the pool and the depth of the court, but that is an inference, not a measured comparison, and this chapter does not average them.

Behind the courtyard sits a sharper problem, about who decides what comfortable means. In Ghadames in Libya, in the summers of nineteen ninety-seven and nineteen ninety-eight, a team surveyed two hundred and thirty-seven residents across fifty-one buildings and instrumented eleven of them, in a climate whose outdoor air sometimes stood above forty-seven degrees by day and thirty at night. The international predicted-comfort index said the people in the traditional buildings were uncomfortable. The people said they were satisfied, and reported a higher standard of comfort in the old buildings than in the new air-conditioned ones. Put that next to a riad in the Fez medina, assessed by calculation rather than measurement, which the same family of standards scored at eighty-two per cent dissatisfied in summer and fifty-one in winter. Whether the failure belongs to the building or to the standard is the chapter's adaptive-comfort problem in miniature, and it is not resolved.

The screen is the other Islamic device everyone knows, and its measured story is a surprise. At the Baeshen House in old Jeddah, through August of two thousand and eighteen, two comparable rooms were instrumented, one with its turned-timber screen open and one with it closed. The open room ran up to two point four degrees cooler by day. More striking, indoor air speed in the open room ranged from nothing to six point nine metres a second against nothing to one point one in the closed room, a roughly sixfold multiplication of air movement. Measured, the screen is first an air device and only second a light device. And the light claim, which is the one architects repeat most often, has no verified field measurement behind it anywhere in this chapter. For the Indian pierced stone screen, the jali, there is no measurement of a historic example at all; there is only a computer optimisation of a screen-derived facade for present-day Delhi, which found a seventy to thirty solid-to-void ratio at one hundred millimetres depth giving useful daylight across most of the floor with direct beam almost eliminated. That is the performance the jali is credited with, achieved in a model of a new building.

Where light has been measured in this period, it has been measured in the bath. Kyriaki Tsikaloudaki and colleagues took a lux meter through the Bey Hamam in Thessaloniki, built in fourteen forty-four, and mapped the light from twenty-four circular openings in the central dome and a hundred and fifty in the peripheral ones. Magda Sibley then logged thirteen working hammams in the Marrakech medina through the summer of two thousand and sixteen. Total roof opening rarely above two per cent of floor area; apertures eighteen to twenty centimetres across in rows of eight; maximum horizontal illuminance never above sixty lux; daylight factors below two per cent; weekly averages around twenty-five lux. And the finding that no one would have predicted: the hot room was the brightest until early afternoon and then fell away sharply as steam accumulated to near saturation. The light was extinguished by the building's own atmosphere. Sibley argues that these very low levels are an authentic experiential quality to be conserved rather than a deficiency to be corrected, and no scholarly source was found arguing the other way, so the conflict here is between a building and a standard rather than between two scholars.

Turn east. In China the picture divides very cleanly, and the division is the chapter's asymmetry in its purest form. The classical gardens of Suzhou are among the most written-about designed landscapes on earth, nine of them inscribed as world heritage, celebrated precisely for making a landscape inside a city. No microclimate monitoring of any Suzhou garden was located in this research. Not one. The Chinese measured literature is all about dwellings, and it is excellent. On the Loess Plateau, through the whole of January in two thousand and twenty-three, a team led by Jiaying Shi instrumented three comparable five-room dwellings in one village in northern Shaanxi: a cave cut into a loess cliff, a detached earth-covered vault, and a modern brick house as a control. The January bedroom means were minus one point nine degrees Celsius in the cliff cave, minus three point two in the detached type, minus nine point three in the modern brick house, and minus twelve point eight outdoors. Against that outdoor figure the cave held about eleven degrees of unheated lift, the detached type about ten, and the modern house about three and a half. Excavation, not construction, delivered roughly three times the lift of the new building in the same village in the same month. That is the strongest cold-climate number in the chapter.

The great ring houses of Fujian, the tulou, tell a subtler story. Machiko Ueda logged one of them, Cheng Qi Lou, sixty-one metres across and four storeys high, through nine days at the end of June two thousand and nine. The outdoor daily swing was six and a half degrees. Inside, the deepest and most mass-enclosed room damped that to one point seven degrees, about seventy-four per cent, while the room opening directly onto the central court damped it only to five point five, about fifteen per cent. The court is not a cooling device so much as the building's only connection to the weather, and the instrument is the gradient from court to core. A second tulou, in Guangxi rather than Fujian, measured about two degrees of summer advantage but at a humidity penalty of about thirteen and a half percentage points. The two buildings disagree about whether the type buys a summer temperature advantage, and no single number should be generalised from either.

The skywell house of southern China makes the same point again and more bluntly. Guohui Yao and colleagues measured eight dwellings across three villages over seven continuous summer days. At Xidi the skywell air ran about three degrees below the outdoor mean; at Zhifeng about four; at Yuyuan the advantage was effectively nothing. Same element, same campaign, three results. And the coolest case was the wettest, with humidity consistently above eighty-five per cent, so the two things cannot be optimised together in that climate. The honest summary of the Chinese void is that it is a connection, not a cure.

One more Chinese case, because of what is claimed for it. A study published in two thousand and twenty-six measured wind speeds in a Beijing courtyard compound on two mornings, one hour each, and used them to validate a model that found a south-east entrance putting just over seventy-five per cent of the compound inside a comfortable wind band. That has been read as evidence that the Chinese siting tradition is applied aerodynamics. Two mornings of wind, no temperature, no radiation, one building. And the anthropologist Ole Bruun, who spent the nineteen nineties watching the tradition practised in Jiangsu and Sichuan, reports that when practitioners are asked directly whether they in fact maintain balanced environments by their own principles, the answers are uncomfortable.

Japan gives the chapter its most complete idea about maintenance and its emptiest evidence base about performance. At Ise the shrine is rebuilt entire on an adjacent site every twenty years; the rebuilding of nineteen ninety-three was the sixty-first, covering sixty-five buildings and employing about a hundred and sixty-eight carpenters. Each cycle consumes on the order of thirteen thousand cypress trees yielding about a hundred thousand worked components, and the trees for the largest columns are three to five hundred years old. In nineteen twenty-three the shrine began replanting its own forest, essentially from scratch, because the original resource was exhausted; that programme needs about two centuries to produce column timber, is now about halfway, and will finish about a hundred years from now. A building whose maintenance schedule is a forest rotation. The story usually told about it, that the cycle has run unbroken for more than two millennia, does not survive contact with the sources: sixty-one cycles of twenty years is about twelve hundred years, not twenty-two hundred, and Cassandra Adams documents a severe interruption from fourteen sixty-three to fifteen eighty-five, a silence of a hundred and twenty-two years when the country was too disordered to keep it up. Of the shrine itself there is no instrumented study of any kind, and there will not be one, because Ise is not open to that sort of investigation. That is the shrine's condition, not a defect in the record, and the chapter keeps the distinction.

The rest of Japan is the same absence without that excuse. Katsura, the most photographed threshold in the world, has no measured study of its daylight, ventilation or thermal behaviour, and neither has the dry garden at Ryoan-ji. The tea room has no illuminance survey and no thermal or acoustic measurement, which is striking given how much is claimed about tea-room light. There is no measurement of the transmittance of a paper screen and none of ventilation through an open veranda. What there is, is a townhouse in Kyoto, measured by Chiemi Iba and Shuichi Hokoi, where an unfloored earth passage runs the depth of a deep narrow plan and opens to the roof. In winter they measured air moving up that passage at between two tenths and half a metre a second across a ten-degree vertical spread: a real, steady, fan-less vertical air path in a house that cannot ventilate across because it has neighbours on both sides. And there is one study, by Anna Sdei, of traditional Japanese dwellings that reports no numbers at all but makes an argument the chapter needs: these houses have large openings and no insulation, and comfort in them is produced by what people wear and do as much as by the envelope.

South Asia supplies the chapter's best interior and its largest silence. The stepwell is a building entered by descending: a stepped corridor from the ground down to a well shaft, lined with storeys of galleried pavilions, so that the deeper you go the darker, damper and cooler it becomes and the smaller the patch of sky. At Rani-ki-Vav in Gujarat there are seven levels, a tank nine and a half metres square at a depth of twenty-three metres, a shaft ten metres across and thirty deep, and more than fifteen hundred sculptures. Samirsinh Parmar and Debi Prasad Mishra took thermometer readings in seventeen stepwells through April, May and June and found interiors four to six degrees below the outside air at most sites, five to ten at one, and twelve to seventeen where stack effect and evaporation act together. One site returned forty-three degrees outside against twenty inside. The method is thin, one thermometer held for five minutes at half past twelve, weekly, with no continuous logging and no humidity, so the four-to-six band is the defensible figure and the twenty-three-degree reading should not travel as a design expectation. Still, almost everything a stepwell offers is interior: light as a shrinking rectangle of sky, replaced by reflection off water and stone; a measured gradient down the descent; water as destination, sound and smell.

And then the Mughal garden. The quartered walled garden with its axial water channels, the four quarters standing for the four rivers of paradise, is the chapter's purest case of nature as a diagram at landscape scale, and one of the most celebrated ideas in the history of designed landscape. Despite targeted searching there is no microclimate monitoring of any Mughal garden, no instrumented study of the cooling effect of a water chute or a channel, and no computational model of a garden pavilion, for Humayun's Tomb, for the Taj, for Shalamar at Lahore or for the Kashmir gardens. The most visited Mughal garden on earth has no planting record even in its world heritage description, and the argument about whether its present lawn is a British substitution for a dense Mughal fruit garden rests on two positions this research could verify neither of. The Mughal garden is celebrated as a climate device and has never been published as a measured one.

Against that, the chapter's strongest case of nature as partner, and it is a living practice. In the Meghalaya hills the Khasi and Jaintia peoples grow bridges. A cutting of a rubber fig is planted on one bank; its aerial roots are guided across a temporary bamboo framework over the stream; implanted in the far bank they shorten, thicken and graft to one another where they press together, so that the structure strengthens itself over time. Ferdinand Ludwig, Wilfrid Middleton and their colleagues inventoried seventy-six such bridges and measured seventy-three of them: spans from two metres to fifty-two point seven, with about eighty per cent under twenty; altitudes from fifty-seven metres to twelve hundred and eleven; and bridges near villages better maintained than isolated ones. They had confident age data for only fifteen of the seventy-six, and in one bridge they resolved fifty-seven roots into age groups of about seven, eighteen and sixty-six years. The popular claim of five hundred years and more stands against that, and the ambiguity is intrinsic: a living structure holds roots of seven years and of sixty-six at once. This is the inverse of every other case in the chapter: the builder supplies geometry and maintenance and the organism supplies the structure, on its own timescale, for as long as somebody keeps tending it.

Southeast Asia gives the chapter its most uncomfortable measured result. At Kampung Cheriau in Negeri Sembilan there stands a timber Malay house more than a hundred and fifty years old, raised on piles, with permeable walls, a deep roof and a graded sequence of rooms from an outer porch to an inner core. Nik Hassin and Alamah Misni measured it over three August days in two thousand and nineteen at twelve points inside and twelve outside. At the afternoon peak the interior read thirty-four point nine degrees against thirty-seven point two outdoors, a depression of about two and a quarter degrees. After about four in the afternoon the relation inverted and the house ran roughly a degree warmer than outdoors, which is the signature of a lightweight envelope that tracks and lags rather than damps. Indoor air speed never exceeded four tenths of a metre a second, an order of magnitude below the authors' own stated optimum, so the raised floor and the permeable wall did not on those days deliver the air movement the type is credited with. The interior peak sat almost seven degrees above the comfort ceiling the authors applied. Their own plainly stated conclusion is that the house exhibits climatic design strategies and was not comfortable most of the time. One detail redeems the reading: the outer porch and the veranda were the hottest zones at the peak and the inner kitchen the coolest. The thresholds take the load and the inner rooms are protected, which is precisely what a threshold is for.

Oceania was named as a coverage gap in the first chapter and carried forward to this one. It has been thinned and not filled. Across the Samoan open pavilion, the Maori sleeping house and storehouse, the Fijian house at Navala, the Palauan meeting house and the Tongan house, there is no thermal, ventilation, daylight or acoustic measurement of any kind. The open thatched pavilion is the Pacific's dominant answer to a hot humid climate and it is, on this evidence, entirely uninstrumented, while the Malay house next door has several published field campaigns: a finding about where the discipline has chosen to look. Two corrections belong here. The carved meeting house that everyone pictures when Maori architecture is mentioned emerged in the middle of the nineteenth century, about a century after this chapter closes; the in-period buildings are the low, thatched, earth-banked sleeping house and the raised storehouse. And the Fijian house is not simply cyclone-proof: when Cyclone Winston crossed Navala in February two thousand and sixteen the great majority of the traditional houses there were destroyed, and the differentiator among the survivors was a deeply founded central post. The figures as reported do not even reconcile with one another, so the chapter flags them rather than passing them on.

Africa was the other coverage gap carried from the first chapter, and here the situation reverses: this region holds more instrumented dwelling evidence than any other in the chapter. A team led by Maria Aguilar-Sanchez logged four housing types in Burkina Faso across the dry season and one wet-season comparison. The circular thatched Peul house damped the outdoor maximum by about one degree with almost no lag. The circular adobe Mossi house with a conical thatch damped it by one to two degrees with about an hour of lag. A rectangular popular house under a fibre-cement roof was actually one to three degrees hotter than outdoors at the peak. And the compact shared-courtyard house damped the swing by more than five degrees with more than five hours of lag. The ranking is unambiguous and it is not the romantic one: the round thatched hut is not a thermal marvel, and the compactness and the court do the work. Barbara Widera instrumented six vernacular types across six West African countries in both seasons and found traditional dwellings holding twenty-six to thirty degrees in the hot dry season with sixty to seventy per cent user satisfaction against about twenty per cent in modern houses. And she measured something almost nobody else in this chapter measured: carbon dioxide of eighteen hundred to nineteen hundred parts per million inside tight-envelope structures, roughly four to five times the outdoor level. That is the interior finding that matters most in this chapter and it is a negative one. The tightness that buys thermal steadiness is paid for in the air people breathe, and any account of vernacular thermal performance that leaves the air quality out is incomplete.

Africa also supplies the chapter's best-measured material and its best case of maintenance built into a fabric. At Djenne the great earthen mosque carries projecting palm beams permanently in its walls, the scaffolding for an annual community replastering carried out at a festival. The Musgum earth dome of Cameroon carries moulded ribs down its outside that are at once footholds for maintenance and drainage channels. At Taos Pueblo the adobe is renewed periodically by community members with traditional materials, and the community keeps electrical lines and piped water out of its walled village by its own decision. In every one of those cases the envelope is a verb rather than a noun, and in every one there is no instrumented study whatever. What has been measured is the render. Halidou Bamogo and colleagues tested a Burkinabe clay reinforced with cattle dung and found reduced thermal conductivity, better abrasion and water resistance, and the formation of an insoluble compound chemically linking the particles. A second laboratory study tested cow dung at ten and twenty per cent by volume in three earths and found shrinkage down, strength up by as much as forty per cent, adhesion doubled, and, most vividly, that reference mortars immersed in water disintegrated within two to five minutes while the mortars with twenty per cent dung held together through a full thirty. The difference between a render that fails in five minutes and one that survives thirty is the difference between a wall remade after every storm and one that is not, and the agent is cattle dung applied by the household.

Two African disputes are carried whole because they are about knowledge itself. At Great Zimbabwe the question of who built the city was answered politically for a century before it was answered archaeologically: an explorer commissioned by Cecil Rhodes who disregarded the evidence of Shona origin, then David Randall-MacIver, who established African origin in nineteen five or nineteen six, and Gertrude Caton-Thompson, who led the first all-women expedition to the ruins and met substantial resistance when she confirmed it. At Bandiagara the elaborate Dogon cosmology that underlies almost all popular architectural writing about the village as a human body was systematically tested by Walter van Beek, who worked in Tireli from nineteen seventy-nine with eight return visits over a decade and could not replicate it: no recognisable creation myth, no knowledge of the star names outside the original informant circle, and, in his words as paraphrased here, body symbolism that does not structure house plans or village layouts. His explanation is not fraud but a collaboration under pressure between a confident outsider and informants oriented toward overt harmony. The published defence of the original work was truncated in the fetch and this chapter cannot report it, which is stated rather than hidden. The cliff, the granaries and the men's shelter are real and observable; the village as a body is a contested ethnographic claim with one side unread, and it is not used here as fact.

Across the Atlantic the chapter finds its clearest correction and its clearest vindication. Mesa Verde is the most photographed passive-solar argument in the world and it has never been instrumented. There is no figure for winter gain, no alcove surface temperature, no interior-against-ambient difference, no diurnal swing inside any Mesa Verde room. What there is, is a direct counter-example from the park's own description: Balcony House faces north-east, which makes it cold in winter, and it was chosen for easy access to two springs. Water access outranked solar orientation at one of the four best-known dwellings, and any account that presents Mesa Verde as a uniform solar doctrine is contradicted by the park itself. The one thing the region has been measured for superbly is timber. Over two hundred and forty thousand trees were harvested for construction at Chaco Canyon across about three centuries, from mountains fifty miles and more away in every direction, by people without draft animals or the wheel; three independent methods, wood anatomy, strontium isotopes and tree-ring provenance, converge on the Chuska Mountains as the dominant source, and a single excavated log from Pueblo Bonito has been traced there individually. Chaco is the clearest pre-industrial case in the Americas of a building culture that could not make its architecture from what was local and moved the forest to the site.

Machu Picchu is the chapter's demonstration that most of a building can be invisible. Kenneth Wright and his colleagues excavated test pits and tested soils and found, under the visible city, half a metre of sandy loam over about a metre of rock and stone chips over shaped fill, with a hundred and twenty-nine wall drainage outlets sized, by back-calculation, to about two hundred square metres of tributary area each, on a site that receives about fourteen hundred and seventy millimetres of rain in the wet season against under two hundred in the dry. Their headline estimate is that approximately sixty per cent of the construction effort at Machu Picchu was underground. At Tipon, Charles Ortloff measured the flow and channel geometry of the principal fountain and showed that a deliberate contraction drives the flow toward the critical state, so that the water arrives at the drop in the condition that produces four clean, equal, stable falls: hydraulics tuned to a criterion for appearance and sound.

Two Andean corrections follow. Moray, the great concentric terraced depression, is universally described as an agricultural laboratory with a temperature gradient of twelve to fifteen degrees between its upper and lower levels. The peer-reviewed study that reports that gradient deployed no sensors on the site at all. Its methods were digital modelling and environmental simulation, and its only measured climate data came from a weather station about thirty kilometres away at Urubamba. The famous number is a model, not a measurement, and no study deploying sensors across Moray's levels was located. Then the raised fields of the Titicaca basin, where the standard account says that canal water absorbs solar heat by day and releases it at night to protect the crops from frost. Two field seasons of instrumentation by Daniel Sanchez de Lozada, Philippe Baveye and Susan Riha, with air sensors, soil probes and moisture reflectometers at two sites, found something else. The protection is real: minimum air temperature over a platform centre about a degree higher than over flat ground on clear calm nights, up to two point eight degrees on the coldest, and in the severe frost of February nineteen ninety-five sub-freezing conditions lasting more than four hours in the control plots against about an hour and a half at platform centres, with significantly less leaf damage. But there was no consistent heat transfer from canal water into the platforms. The soil gradients showed net heat flow toward the canal, not away from it. The raised-field soil was not appreciably warmer than the control on frost nights. The authors attribute the protection to above-ground processes, to natural convection and cold-air drainage, with humidity measurements confirming air circulating from canal toward platform. The mechanism as usually told is wrong, and the protection is better stated as hours of exposure than as degrees.

And then the chapter's vindication. In February nineteen ninety-three, at ambient temperatures between minus twenty-eight and minus forty-one degrees Celsius, Peter Kershaw, Peter Scott and Harold Welch instrumented two snow houses built in traditional Inuit technique, each with seven thermocouples, humidity and wind sensors, logging every ten seconds. The large one used seventy-two blocks at an average of twenty-three and a half kilograms; the snow densified from three hundred and seventy-seven to four hundred and thirty kilograms per cubic metre as it was lived in. Heated by two seal-oil lamps and occupied by one to five people, the interior stood up to forty-five degrees above the outside air. Empty and unheated, the same structure stood about five degrees above it. The snow wall was worth an insulation value equivalent to about a hundred millimetres of conventional insulation; the caribou-skin lining cut heat loss by at least fifty watts per square metre across a measured gradient of nearly five degrees; heat rising from the ground supplied about fourteen per cent of the total. Set beside it an unheated, unoccupied igloo measured in Harbin in December two thousand and nineteen, which gained about four degrees. The difference between four degrees and forty-five is not the snow. It is the lamp, the bodies, the skin lining and the sealed door. Performance belongs to the inhabited system, not to the shell, and this chapter hands that sentence forward to every chapter that follows.

Europe in this period offers the most famous nature-in-architecture claim of all, and it turns out to be a claim made about Gothic rather than by it. The idea that the nave was conceived as a forest, the piers trunks and the ribs branches, is documented with certainty from Goethe in seventeen seventy-two onward, extended by Chateaubriand and echoed by Coleridge. Whether it has any medieval antecedent is open: the one candidate, a passage attributed to Raphael, could not be verified, and the canonical statements of the alternative medieval rationale, light and geometric order, were all behind doors this research could not open. What the period does deliver is nature counted. In the chapter house at Southwell, built between twelve eighty-eight and about thirteen hundred, consultant botanists identified ten species in the carved foliage, with published counts of thirty occurrences of maple, twenty-six of oak, nineteen each of hawthorn, buttercup and cinquefoil, eighteen of vine, twelve of ivy, eight of hop and two each of rose and bryony: a hundred and fifty-five identified occurrences of local hedgerow and woodland-edge plants, not an exotic or heraldic repertoire. It is the one place in the chapter where nature brought inside is quantifiable, and it is achieved entirely as image, with no thermal, hydraulic or biological function whatever. The one hard number set about Gothic light comes from a laboratory: twenty-nine excavated window-glass fragments from a Flemish castle, measured by absorption spectroscopy, giving about twenty-one per cent transmission for fifteenth-century glass at a simulated three millimetres and about thirty-four per cent for sixteenth-century glass at two. That permits a statement about plain glass and nothing at all about a coloured, painted, leaded window, and no measured daylight study of Chartres or the Sainte-Chapelle was located.

The European system case is water. The oldest surviving architectural drawing in the West, the Plan of St Gall, drawn about eight hundred and twenty on five sewn sheets of sheepskin, is as much a drawing of a community's food, medicine and burial ground as of its church. The Cistercians then made the river a part of the building: at Maulbronn about twenty lakes and ponds linked by channels, with two streams diverted through the monastery to drain the wetlands, feed the fish, power a mill and flush the latrines, all at once, and sections still performing their original function. And at Tivoli the whole of the Villa d'Este runs on the fall of the ground, with no pump anywhere in it, including a water organ that plays today every two hours. Against that gravity tradition stands the chapter's counter-example. Versailles, whose world heritage inscription says nothing whatever about where its water came from, was served by the Machine de Marly: fourteen wheels of nearly twelve metres, more than two hundred and fifty pumps, a lift of a hundred and sixty-two metres from the Seine, delivering on average about three thousand two hundred cubic metres a day. Running the fountains in the ordinary manner required at least four times that. The machine met roughly a quarter of the demand it was built for, the fountains were played in rotation as the King walked, and the canal intended to fix the problem was abandoned unfinished. One great early-modern landscape whose ambition outran its water by a factor of four or more, where the nature on display had to be rationed.

The cold north closes the regional tour and closes it with an absence. The Icelandic turf house is the best-instrumented cold vernacular in Europe and there is still no published thermal conductivity value and no measured insulation value for Icelandic turf anywhere this research could reach. What has been measured is geometry, surface temperature, moisture and fire. A year of monitoring at two farms found turf about twenty-five to thirty centimetres thick at one and about ten to twelve at the other, a two-and-a-half-fold difference inside a single tradition; on a north-facing roof in winter the difference between the outer and inner turf sensors was only one to three degrees while the outer surface fluctuated; and one set of loggers failed and read a constant hundred per cent humidity, which the authors reported as a finding rather than hiding. An earlier study at Glaumbaer found the living and sleeping room cool in winter and, oddly, colder still at the bottom of the summer range, with warmth maintained partly by the close proximity of the occupants. The one place where earth has been measured against stone is Scotland, where seventy in-situ heat-flux measurements across fifteen mostly pre-nineteen-nineteen properties found six-hundred-millimetre solid stone walls at about one point one watts per square metre per degree, better than the published defaults assumed, and earth or mud walls at the same thickness at between four tenths and eight tenths. At the same thickness, earth outperformed stone by roughly half. Neither turf nor a Hebridean black house was in that sample, so the transfer is an analogy and the chapter says so. The Russian masonry stove, the purest thermal-mass device in the chapter, about two tonnes of masonry charged by a half-hour fire and giving heat back for eight to twelve hours at a surface warm enough to sleep against, has never had an instrumented study in situ in anything reachable; every number available comes from nineteen-eighties North American guidance for a modern derivative. And the Finnish smoke sauna, the one building type in the whole chapter whose entire purpose is a controlled extreme interior climate, could not be reached at all, because three attempts at its heritage inscription returned the page for an unrelated element in Venezuela.

Gather the interior evidence, because the interior is the axis this series under-serves and this chapter is where the pattern becomes obvious. The measured interior light of twelve and a half centuries amounts to two bathhouses and a box of excavated glass fragments. The measured interior air amounts to a wind tower, a scale model of a roof scoop, a screened room in Jeddah, a staircase in Kyoto, a Malay house, two mornings in a Beijing courtyard and a snow house in which no interior air movement could be detected at all, though soot patterns showed air diffusing under pressure through the snow blocks themselves. The measured interior air quality amounts to one study, and its answer was eighteen hundred to nineteen hundred parts per million. The measured interior sound amounts to nothing whatever: not the water organ, not the water chute, not the ice-glazed dome, not a single vault. And the measured interior person amounts to two hundred and thirty-seven residents in Ghadames, a hundred and forty-nine Balinese farmers, some satisfaction percentages in West Africa, a few questionnaires in Ethiopia, and between one and five people in a snow house. Every other interior in this chapter was measured empty, or was not measured at all.

So to the band. The measurement line that runs through this series sorts claims by their relationship to evidence, and this chapter's band is the first one: never made. The ideas are old, sophisticated, regionally specific and frequently brilliant, and for the overwhelming majority of them nobody ever took the number. The band is visible only because of its exceptions, and the exceptions are of exactly two kinds. There are the contradictions, where somebody finally measured and the reputation did not survive: the wind tower that warms at night, the courtyard that ran hotter, the thatched hut that damps one degree, the Malay house its own investigators called uncomfortable, the alcoves nobody instrumented and the one that faces north-east, the terraced laboratory whose famous gradient is a computer model, and the raised field whose mechanism runs the other way. And there is the one great vindication, where somebody measured and the tradition proved larger than the claim: the snow house at forty-five degrees above the outside air, inhabited, and five, empty. Both kinds carry the same instruction. A performance claim without a measurement is a tradition, not evidence; and a measurement without an occupant may be measuring the wrong thing.

What carries forward. That performance belongs to the inhabited system and not to the shell, so later chapters should ask of every instrument whether the room was occupied. That the void is not the device: the benefit lives in the mass, the shade, the depth and the water, not in the hole. That the mass is the mechanism, and the same tightness which buys steadiness costs breathed air. That maintenance is not a cost appended to a design but the design itself, with its access moulded permanently into the fabric, and that later chapters should ask whether anyone ever came back and measured a second time. That the water chains built on gravity ran, and the one built on pumps met a quarter of its demand. And that a long list of the most celebrated buildings in the history of architecture has no number attached to it at all, which is the inheritance this chapter hands on, together with the subjects its own exhausted budget could not reach, named as retrieval failures so that somebody can go and get them.
