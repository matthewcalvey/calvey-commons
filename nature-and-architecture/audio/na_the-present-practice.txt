This is the seventh chapter of a series about how people have brought nature into buildings. The series runs from the deep past to the present and then looks ahead, and it asks one question all the way through: how has nature been brought inside, outside and at the threshold of buildings, and what does that record actually establish for an architect deciding what to do now. The previous chapter audited a field and its claims; this one audits a practice and its quantities, and almost everything in it is a number somebody will ask you to put on a drawing.

One idea runs through the whole series. There is a spectrum of ways a building can relate to nature. At one end nature is an image, a picture or a framed view; then a form, a shape borrowed; then a model, a process imitated; then a material, living matter as the substance of the building; then a climate, the building shaped by sun, wind and water; then a system, the building as a working part of an ecological cycle; and at the far end a partner, the living world as a collaborator with standing of its own. The spectrum is not a ladder of progress. Partnership is claimed in this period by law and by planning instruments, and measured in almost none of those claims.

This chapter is not organised by time or by region, because the evidence does not sort itself that way. It is organised by position: outside, then the threshold, then inside, then the apparatus by which a practice produces a number and checks it, and finally what happens to all of it over twenty-five years. This research ran without web search, so every source was reached by direct retrieval or not at all, and where a figure could not be read this chapter says so and does not fill it from memory. Image collections were out of reach, so every illustration is a drawing still to be made.

Start with the most widely repeated quantity in the design of a street: about two cubic feet of soil for every square foot of crown. Nina Bassuk and Susan Lindsey published it in 1991, and their paper calls it an explicitly rough general estimate offered for professional convenience. It is not a measurement made on a tree. It is a water budget: daily water loss from crown projection, leaf area and pan evaporation, divided by the water the soil can hold and multiplied by the interval between rains. And their own third table runs the same tree through eight cities and produces 0.70 for Ithaca, 0.80 for Mobile, 0.91 for Miami, 1.12 for Minneapolis, 1.27 for Indianapolis, 1.53 for Seattle, 2.23 for Denver and 17.20 for Phoenix. A twenty-four-fold spread sits inside the source of the rule, and the number the profession quotes is not one of the eight.

Nine years later Arthur DeGaetano ran a different budget through fifty years of Boston weather. For a sealed container with roots wholly confined, at a ten-year return period, the requirement runs from 0.6 cubic metres of soil for a two-metre crown to 14.1 for an eight-metre crown, a ratio of 0.19 to 0.34 cubic metres per square metre. Irrigation collapses it: a six-metre crown needs 8.0 cubic metres with no water, 3.8 with water every second week and 2.4 with water every week. And out of 183 potential days in a Boston growing season it needs watering on 93 days if you deliver 2.6 cubic metres at a time, while Atlanta needs 111 to 123. An undersized pit does not produce a smaller tree. It produces an irrigation obligation covering half to two-thirds of every growing season, handed to somebody who was not in the room. And one thing must be said plainly, because practice has inverted it: this literature documents a growth constraint. No source reached attributes street-tree mortality primarily to soil volume.

The growth constraint does have a measured fix. A pavement sub-base must be compacted so it does not settle; a root needs pore space; the two are opposed unless you separate the load path from the soil. Jason Grabosky, Nina Bassuk and Barbara von Es grew oak seedlings in stone and soil mixes compacted to pavement standards and in a clay loam compacted to its own peak density. In the clay loam at 1.67 megagrams per cubic metre, exactly what a sidewalk installation is built to, root growth stopped entirely. In the stone and soil mixes at 1.85 to 2.07, denser material by any reading, roots were not restricted, and a companion result put the increase at a minimum of 320 per cent over the compacted control. The denser material is the habitable one, because the load runs stone to stone and the soil in the voids is never compressed.

Then a Brooklyn street, planted in 1997 and 1998 and measured in 2003 and again in 2007, with a continuous trench of that blend under granite pavers on one side and a conventional grass tree lawn on the other. At year six and at year ten no difference in diameter, height or canopy width reached statistical significance: structural soil under paving grew trees indistinguishable from an open verge, not better ones. Four deaths of thirty-five under the pavement, about 11 per cent. In the grass lawn, nine deaths of twenty-six, three further replacement deaths and two more missing, about 41 per cent of the originally planted stock. The authors attribute the difference to maintenance and infrastructure repair activity, not to the rooting medium. The open grass strip, which every designer treats as the generous option, is the exposed one: mowed, dug for utilities, parked on and salted.

Now the survival failure, which is the more important half and has no engineering fix. In the autumn of 1985 volunteers planted 480 trees along five and a half miles of Martin Luther King Boulevard, from southern Berkeley into inner-city Oakland, and David Nowak, Joe McBride and Russell Beatty censused them every January for three years. Two-year mortality was 34 per cent, average annual mortality 19, and only 62 per cent were alive and un-vandalised. Oakland lost 37 per cent against Berkeley's 23. And the variable that correlated most strongly with whether a tree lived was census-tract unemployment, at a coefficient of 0.78. The sentence to carry is buried in the method: watering and fertilisation, if any, were left to whoever lived next to each pit, and city staff watered only the commercial and industrial stretches, and only in summer. The maintenance obligation was allocated to people who had never agreed to it, and it failed hardest where they had least capacity to absorb it.

Thirty-six years later, in another city by another method, the same answer came back. Lara Roman and colleagues tracked 2,884 trees planted through Philadelphia's Tree Tenders programme. Cumulative survival was 92.5 per cent after the first summer, 87.2 after the second, 80.9 after the third and 77.1 after the fourth. Three predictors were significant: more impervious surround reduced survival, higher neighbourhood social vulnerability reduced survival, and trees planted in autumn were one and a half times as likely to reach a second summer as trees planted in spring. Maintenance could not be tested at all, because the level of group activity was not a usable proxy for it. The figures across cities sort into two families by age rather than disagreeing: establishment mortality high and variable, 34 per cent over two years on that boulevard and 22.9 over four in Philadelphia; post-establishment mortality low and consistent between cities at 2.8 to 3.8 per cent a year. The one lever everybody recommends, stewardship, is the subject of at least five studies and not one could be opened, so the size of its effect cannot be stated.

Below ground the instrument cannot reach the problem. Construction compaction was measured on real sites at depths of 0.3 to 0.8 metres. The surface nuclear gauge a contractor will bring is valid only to 0.15 metres and only in soils below 5 per cent organic matter, and the penetrometer that stands in for it is unreliable on compacted, dry or stony ground. The damage sits two to five times deeper than the routine instrument can see, so a compaction specification on a drawing is in ordinary practice unverifiable at the depth that matters. Across a review of infiltration studies in eleven countries, permeable paving loses capacity from the day it is laid: rates fall significantly within two years and, unmaintained, have generally dropped below 1,000 millimetres an hour by year four. Milling two and a half centimetres off a twenty-one-year-old porous asphalt nearly restored it to like-new, while moistening and then sweeping had very limited success and sometimes increased clogging, and the review contains no discussion of cold climates at all.

Wind is the strangest item outside, because it is the one constraint in this chapter with direct legal force over the form of a building and its evidence cannot be read by the profession it regulates. In London a wind assessment is required for tall buildings; in the Netherlands a national standard governs wind nuisance and danger outright. The thresholds most commonly applied are Lawson's, recovered sideways from an open paper that tabulates them rather than from any standard. At a 5 per cent exceedance probability, sitting becomes uncomfortable above 2.5 metres a second, standing and strolling are acceptable to about 5, and walking to 8. The danger criterion, which carries the heaviest legal consequence, could not be read and is left unfilled. The Dutch standard is a purchased document and both its authoritative journal accounts are closed with no open copy anywhere. A binding constraint on the massing of buildings sits behind a purchase.

Shade is where the outside evidence is at its best and its most misquoted. Around a single twelve-metre lime in Den Haag on a sunny July day in 2023, with sixteen skin sensors on participants walking between sun and shade every twenty minutes, the peak thermal index in sun reached 36.3 degrees Celsius and the physiological equivalent temperature 39.9. Shade cut the thermal index by more than 10 degrees and the physiological temperature by more than 15, while air temperature between sun and shade differed by a degree or two. And the asymmetry that matters most: skin warmed in sun at up to 1.14 degrees a minute and cooled in shade at 0.79. Because you heat up faster than you cool down, the shaded fraction of a walking route must exceed the sunlit fraction, which is the most transferable comfort rule in the chapter and rests on one tree, one day and a conference abstract. Four magnitudes circulate for what shade delivers and they are four quantities rather than four estimates of one: more than 15 degrees of physiological temperature measured at a point, 1 to 2 degrees of the same quantity modelled as a spatial average, 11.5 degrees of mean radiant temperature in an optimisation, and 2.02 degrees of air temperature in canyon simulations. Extending the lower canopy has three and a half times the effect of extending the upper, so crown-lifting for clearance removes the part that does the work.

Two instruments of policy close the outside position. In England, biodiversity net gain became mandatory in 2024: a minimum 10 per cent gain in biodiversity units, secured for thirty years, computed from baseline habitat mapping by size, condition and type, with developments of no more than nine units outside the requirement, and delivery ordered on site, then off site, then statutory credits. Those quantities are legal arithmetic performed on a form before the habitat exists, and the critique is quantitative. Early-adopter assessments showed a 34 per cent reduction in the area of non-urban habitats, compensated by smaller areas of higher-quality habitat, with 95 per cent of units originating from sites the developer would manage. Twenty-seven per cent of all units fall within governance gaps at high risk of non-compliance, and 21 per cent of applications contained conspicuous calculation errors. And the comparable French instrument placed 64 per cent of more than a thousand offset areas where biophysical integrity was already above the national median: offsets follow economic constraint, not ecological need.

Beside that stands the one instrument in this chapter where Indigenous knowledge has legal force over how a site's water is handled. In Aotearoa New Zealand freshwater is governed under a hierarchy that places the health and wellbeing of water first, expressed as Te Mana o te Wai, and it binds regional councils. This is written in the present tense as living practice and cited twice. To the communities whose knowledge it is: the Whanganui River, recognised in 2017 as the first river in Aotearoa to hold legal personhood, with Tupua Te Kawa values guiding decisions across the catchment, documented by Nancy Tuaine of Whanganui; and Murihiku, documented by Jane Kitson. To the scholarship: Linda Te Aho on rivers as the bloodways of Papatūānuku; April Bennett on mana in five dimensions and on the argument that Māori must be the ones to define Te Mana o te Wai; Dan Hikuroa, Kelli Te Maiharoa and Lesley Rameka. And to the critique, also Māori-authored: Lara Taylor on the Crown's stated rationale. No published hydrological or ecological outcome attributable to the settlement was reachable eight years on, and a general search for Indigenous knowledge of urban land and water returned thirteen chapters of a single book and nothing urban. That is the finding: this knowledge appears in the indexed record almost only where a state has given it statutory form, and where a community holds knowledge that is restricted, its absence from a database is correct and the route to it is relationship rather than retrieval.

Now the threshold, and the first thing to say is how little of it has been measured. One team ran nine bibliographic sweeps and classified every record as instrumented on a real building, laboratory, or simulation. Double-skin façades came back at five measured against fourteen simulations. Windcatchers, two field tests against eighteen computational or wind-tunnel studies. Courtyards in hot-arid climates, one field-measured case against nineteen simulations. And three sweeps returned zero records carrying the quantity a designer needs: how much daylight a balcony removes, what a commercial windcatcher delivers in an occupied classroom, and how often occupants defeat automated shading, that last across forty records. The count understates the measured record by an unknown amount, because the publisher most likely to hold those measurements serves no abstracts to the index. The honest form of the finding is not that the measurements do not exist, but that the measured record on threshold devices is not retrievable by any route an architect would use.

Then the question of whether the threshold ever moves. In tropical offices, longitudinal logging captured 9,517 discrete behavioural events, and half the participants never adjusted their window shades across the whole period, leaving them closed. The unoperated default of a manual shade is the state it was last put in, and in practice that state is shut, so a daylight calculation assuming shades raised whenever daylight is adequate is wrong for half the occupants. That is one of three ways the threshold gets disabled, and only one of the three is architectural. The architect restricts the occupant for appearance, as at Seagram, where the blinds were limited to three positions so the elevation read uniformly. The people running the building restrict the building for risk: at the Manchester Civil Justice Centre, a fourteen-storey court designed with natural ventilation predicted to run about 40 per cent of the time, the management system locks natural ventilation out in rain and low wind, and night cooling by natural ventilation was never once carried out. The occupants preferred the mechanical supply because they wanted to feel moving air: the passive system failed to deliver a sensation, not a temperature, and a room at the right temperature with imperceptible air reads as stuffy. Three mechanisms, one outcome, and three entirely different remedies: a drawing, a handover, and a default.

A double-skin cavity is not a buffer at a single temperature; it is a column. At a research institute in Daejeon, on a December day with outdoor air averaging minus 0.36 degrees Celsius, the cavity showed 24.15 degrees of vertical stratification between its lower and upper sections, so where you tap it selects between two different machines. Exposed thermal mass is in the same condition, the physics sound and the total reachable measured support a single conference abstract. And the double-skin record overall is not negative; it is absent and internally inconsistent. No measured annual energy outcome for any real double-skin building was located, no overheating threshold and no maintenance record, and the one measured paper reached disagrees with itself between its abstract and its text by a factor this chapter declines to arbitrate.

Green walls are the clearest case in this book of evidence that is real, reachable and not about what it is cited for. At Geisenheim in Germany, four experimental textile living walls, one to each orientation, with an unvegetated control wall, were measured at 30, 90 and 150 centimetres from the surface. At 30 centimetres only one of three planting variants cooled. At 90 centimetres only one read lower than the control. At 150 centimetres there was no significant difference between any variant and the bare wall, and air on the north and east walls ran about 2 kelvin hotter than ambient, while across the campaign the vegetation itself averaged 0.6 kelvin warmer than the air. The authors could not reproduce the cooling reported in earlier studies and proposed shading rather than evapotranspiration as the mechanism. Measurement distance was never standardised, so an open literature reports quantities that cannot be compared, and the counter-family of two to four degrees of summer cooling and a 30.2 per cent energy reduction from a filtration prototype stands beside it unaveraged, distances unstated.

What a living wall demonstrably is, on the evidence, is a replacement schedule with a vertical irrigation problem. An exterior modular wall in Adelaide, 144 pots in twelve rows, three substrates, two irrigation regimes, eighteen months across two summers: survival at eighteen months was 102 of 144, a mortality of 29.2 per cent. Drainage from the bottom rows ran 2.2 to 3.6 times that from the top while the top sections outgrew the bottom two to four times by dry weight, so a single-zone wall over one storey over-waters its base while its top grows away from it. Species differences were highly significant; substrate and irrigation differences in survival were not. That trial is the only independent living-wall mortality measurement this entire research run could reach from any climate. A separate team searching specifically for wall mortality and replacement rates found nothing at all, including in the industry's own journal, which carries twelve-year survival data for roofs and no survival study for walls, so any failure rate quoted to you is manufacturer-supplied or unfilled.

The balcony has a ledger with both columns filled, because Korea absorbed balconies into apartments at scale. On the cost side, one refurbishment near Flensburg found an open balcony one and a half metres deep cut daylight in the room behind by 30 to 35 per cent, and glazing it took the cut to 60. Across twenty open records nobody has quantified that reduction since the year 2000, so a single study from that year, reached through a review published twenty years later, is the whole daylight term. On the return side, rooms that lost their balconies ran a degree colder in winter in one Korean study and 0.8 degrees colder in another, with heating load up 39 per cent and cooling up 22, 61.6 per cent of owners whose balconies were removed reporting mould and condensation afterwards. One more threshold requirement is driven entirely by the outside face. Monitored collision counts recorded 233 bird collisions across 51 species, 204 at untreated façades against 29 at treated, with treatments meeting published guidelines reducing collisions by 98.4 per cent and non-compliant treatments by 76.3. The pattern spacing dimension is, because every measured source holding it is closed, and this chapter states no spacing rule in any form.

Inside is where the measurements were made, published openly, and the metric standardised without them. The shift to climate-based modelling was complete in research about two decades before the European daylight standard adopted it, and the standard then kept a daylight-factor route anyway. That standard's legal status must be stated carefully, because this is a correction: nothing reachable verified any jurisdiction in which it is legally binding. It has national editions, it is a purchased document, and it is graded here as guidance rather than as a requirement. What its minimum level costs in built form has been simulated for Ljubljana: a room 7.5 metres deep, the maximum the local legislation permits, required a mean vertical daylight factor at the façade of 42.48 per cent, and the maximum floor-area ratio compatible with ground-floor compliance was 0.93 at low site coverage against 4.34 for a room 4.0 metres deep. Minimum daylight provision and urban density are in arithmetic conflict, and the variable that decides it is room depth, not window size.

Then the reproducibility of the compliance number itself. One identical model, a single-storey building of 511 square metres with a fifth of its wall in glass, was run through four daylight tools with every input held constant. Spatial daylight autonomy came back at 46 per cent, 78, 66, 49 and 86 depending on the tool: a forty-point spread on one model. The dominant cause is whether the software models the occupant reaching for the blind, since tools omitting that rule returned perimeter values above 90 per cent. The threshold that decides whether a room counts as daylit sits inside a spread roughly four times its own width, and no inter-laboratory round-robin exists anywhere to characterise it. In one lecture hall at Warsaw University of Technology, instrumented at 319 points with external irradiance logged, two engines were compared against measurement under a clear September sky and a fully overcast January one. Under overcast, one engine ran 60 per cent bright in the row nearest the windows, 102 per cent bright in the middle and 204 per cent bright at the back. Under clear sky the other ran 53 per cent dark in the middle of the room. Error grows with distance from the window in every case and flips sign with sky condition, so the deep part of the room, where compliance is decided, is where prediction is least trustworthy.

The view has been turned into a quantity and only part of it can be read. The criterion rests on horizontal sight angle, outside distance to the nearest obstacle, and the number of view layers, meaning sky, landscape and ground. The sight angles are recoverable, at around 14 degrees for the minimum level, 28 for medium and 54 for high. The outside distances and the layer counts could not be verified through six separate routes and are left unfilled. Matusiak and nine co-authors state that the criteria address only the geometric configurations that secure access to a view, not its content or quality, and that no window-view assessment method is in application anywhere, while the strongest single piece of evidence behind any of it concerns layers rather than angles, in an association across 220 classrooms where the number of view layers predicted reading achievement. Glare then shows that a metric can be a property of an adapted population rather than a constant of the eye. The European bands put daylight glare probability at or below 0.35 as imperceptible, rising to intolerable above 0.45. In eight open-plan offices in Bangkok, with 1,488 samples and vertical eye illuminance logged every minute, every single value fell below 0.35, inside the European imperceptible band, and occupants reported discomfort anyway, at a mean of 0.25, with locally derived boundaries at 0.22, 0.24 and 0.26. And the practical rule from the simulation work is that the seat and the facing direction change the answer more than the fabric does.

Circadian light gives the chapter its single best interior measurement, and it is a comprehensive failure. Eighteen authors recommend a daytime minimum of 250 melanopic lux at the eye, vertically at about 1.2 metres, with a maximum of 10 in the evening and 1 in the sleep environment, and they are unusually candid about what that rests on: laboratory studies of melatonin suppression, individual sensitivity varying more than tenfold for unknown reasons, healthy adults aged 18 to 55 only, field studies never designed to isolate the relevant photoreceptor, and an explicit statement that visual performance and safety take precedence where they conflict. Against that, a team measured all fifteen nursing homes with dedicated long-term dementia units in Bergen, at sixty degrees north, a complete population, 315 spectrometer readings. Facing inward, the median melanopic illuminance at the eye was 45 lux after dark, 57 on a winter day and 112 on a summer day; facing the window it was 38, 98 and 186. The relevant credit threshold is 217. Homes meeting it out of fifteen: two in summer facing inward, six in summer facing the window, and, with the electric light turned to maximum after dark, zero against every threshold tested. Statistically there was no difference between a winter day and astronomical night. And only 15 per cent of the variance lay between buildings, which means that which chair you sit in matters more than which building you are in. The rooms fail; the failure is one of position rather than of specification; and at sixty degrees north the winter interior has no day.

Air indoors produces the chapter's most quotable pair of numbers. Across a hundred randomly selected offices, measured three independent ways, outdoor air delivery averaged 55 litres a second per person as found, and about 11 once adjusted to minimum outdoor-air conditions and actual occupancy. About 17 per cent of measurements failed the requirement as found and about half failed under the adjusted condition: the same measurement, two answers, with compliance a function of occupancy at the moment of reading. On carbon dioxide and cognition, chamber and field are two orders apart. In a chamber, cognitive scores were 61 per cent higher on a green-building day and 101 per cent higher on an enhanced day. In the field, 302 office workers in 42 buildings across six countries, with sensors at the workstation reporting every ten minutes, gave an increase in response time of 0.85 per cent per interquartile increase of 315 parts per million. The field authors are explicit that carbon dioxide combines its own effect with that of ventilation rate and works as a tracer rather than necessarily as an agent below three thousand parts per million. The design implication is the opposite of the usual one: chasing a setpoint chases the symptom, and the health case is ventilation rate and filtration.

Then the interior's small strange literature about living and natural things. The most useful number about indoor planting was published in 1992 and appears to have been unread since: six interior foliage species grown for six months at two light levels, which convert to roughly 810 and 1,610 lux, grew better at the higher level for every species except three. A separate twelve-month study found no light source consistently superior once total photosynthetically active radiation was equalised, so it is dose and not spectrum. And acclimatisation re-tunes a plant's metabolism over about fifteen weeks, which is why the first three or four months after handover are the mortality window. Set 1,610 lux beside the Bergen measurements away from a summer window and much ordinary floor area sits below the level at which half those species merely survived: interior planting is a light-budget decision made on the plan. No study of measured interior plant mortality or replacement rates in occupied buildings was retrievable at all, while a survey of 300 rental users found them still expecting air purification, a claim dead in the literature for three decades and alive in the market.

Wood is non-monotonic, and the result is twenty years old and still surprising. Fifteen male students sat for ninety seconds each in three small rooms at zero, 45 and 90 per cent wood coverage, at 40 lux. The 45 per cent room gave falls in blood pressure and the highest ratings for comfort and restfulness. The 90 per cent room gave the largest and earliest falls in both systolic and diastolic pressure and relatively low comfort and restfulness. More wood lowered blood pressure more and was liked less. Sound produces the most complete inversion in the chapter. In a real open-plan office of 930 square metres with seventy-seven employees, six three-week masking conditions ran over eighteen weeks: a plain fan-like noise, then four water and river sounds, then a return to the plain noise. Acoustic satisfaction fell steadily across the nature sounds, the nature sounds masked speech worse than the plain noise did, every self-rated task worsened, the river sounds were rated less natural than the fan, and job satisfaction fell and had not recovered when the plain masker was restored. The authors' explanation is the lesson: water with no visible water element is heard as lavatories and running plumbing. Nature delivered to the interior through one sense at a time, without the others, does not merely fail; it inverts. Three small electric mats under 400 watts in total, chosen and controlled by the occupant, carried 41 subjects from a mean thermal sensation of minus 0.92 to plus 0.7 in a room held at 16 degrees Celsius.

Behind all three positions sits an apparatus that should tell an architect whether any of it worked, and it cannot. Four kinds of number circulate and are spoken of as one. A prediction is a model of a building that does not exist, and it cannot be wrong at the moment it is made. A rating is a normalised comparison against a peer population and can improve while the underlying quantity worsens. A calculated certificate is a second prediction and falsifies nothing. Only a measurement can falsify all three, and it alone is neither procured, paid for nor contractually required. Nothing in the apparatus requires that a prediction ever be compared with a measurement of the same building. The cost is visible in the one clean instrumented case this research read in full. A federal test house at Gaithersburg, Maryland, was sub-metered by end use for a demonstration year against a model published before that year began, and it had no occupants at all. Consumption was under-predicted by 15.9 per cent, generation over-predicted by about 5, and the net surplus, the quantity the whole building was designed to deliver, was predicted at 3,066 kilowatt hours and delivered at 596, wrong by 80.6 per cent. The arithmetic is general: a net target is the difference between two large separately estimated quantities, so the error in the net is the sum of the component errors divided by a small number, and a building designed for a large surplus is verifiable by a model of ordinary accuracy while one designed to balance exactly is not. The end uses the model got most wrong were heating and cooling precisely where the architect's decisions live. Occupant behaviour explains none of it, because there were no occupants.

The embodied carbon number is a choice of database rather than a property of a building. One fixed steel-framed supermarket of 2,500 square metres was assessed three ways, from a general government dataset through a large inventory to detailed material-specific matching. The coarsest gave the highest figure and the finest the lowest: a 35.2 per cent reduction, then a further 5.5, some 40.7 per cent in all. Structural steel alone differed by 200 tonnes, plasterboard varied by 225 per cent between sources and one acrylic by 1,183, and the authoring software's own default weight for internal steel stud walls was wrong by 88.4 per cent. Ratings do better, and one scheme deserves its reputation on structure: the Australian system rates buildings on actual, audited, metered performance on a one-to-six star scale, a rating lapses after twelve months and must be reassessed, and since 2011 it must be disclosed in advertising when a large building is sold, leased or sublet. A rating that expires annually cannot be won once. But the disclosure threshold conflicts between sources, at one thousand square metres against two thousand, and since neither the legislation nor the scheme's own guidance could be reached, no threshold is asserted here. And the claim that mandatory disclosure produced market-wide measured improvement failed along five separate routes: the scheme's own site served sentence frames with the numbers missing, and the one review readable in full cites price studies and not one study showing that disclosure lowered measured energy use. New York's benchmarking and emissions laws could not be reached at all, so no law number, threshold or outcome is stated.

The most telling single result in the apparatus is about enclosure. The British programme that measured completed buildings and reported honestly on them in the late 1990s has been unreadable to this book for two chapters. A fifth route finally opened one paper, the conclusions paper, which says that improvement effort in the industry has gone to production rather than to performance in use. The same route, run in the same minute, for the energy-performance paper from the same journal, the same issue and overlapping authors, returned an abstract explicitly elided by the publisher. The morals are readable and the measurements are not. What measurement does produce, when somebody pays for it, is things nobody was looking for. A post-occupancy evaluation of 350 offices in a federal headquarters, run around a lighting retrofit, met its lighting targets, and also found relative humidity at 14 per cent, which the project was not about; found the proportion of luminaires actually switched on rising from 72 to 98 per cent after lamp density fell,; And measurement does not happen for structural reasons: appointments end at practical completion, measurement happens afterwards on data the architect has no right to and cannot invoice for, and a measured shortfall converts a professional opinion into an actionable discrepancy at the expense of whoever commissioned it.

The last subject is time, and the drift does not all run one way. Of eight measured longitudinal findings, three show degradation, two improvement, two a reversal from benefit to harm, and one abandonment producing more life. Nine extensive roofs in the Stuttgart region, installed in the early 1980s and left when the maintenance contracts lapsed, were surveyed at twenty to twenty-seven years old. mean substrate depth had fallen to about 40 to 53 millimetres on the oldest against 86 and above on the younger, acidity had drifted, and organic content exceeded the guideline limits on four of the nine. And the original depths, compositions and species lists are lost, a finding about record-keeping rather than about plants. A roof specified at eighty or a hundred millimetres is not that roof at year twenty-five; it drifts out of its own standard by doing nothing, and no inspection regime in general practice would detect it. Against that, a school roof in Rome where irrigation and maintenance ceased entirely in the spring of 2020 held 62 spontaneously colonising species a year later, with four of six planted species holding or gaining. One year without maintenance diversified a roof; two decades simplified one. Plant choice on a roof is decided by growth form, not by species: on a roof at Denver Botanic Gardens, 118 taxa followed for twelve years in a semi-arid climate survived at 87 per cent for woody shrubs, 77 for succulents, 43 for grasses, 36 for creeping forbs and 22 for upright forbs. A fourfold spread, and every single ice plant taxon failed, in exactly the cold semi-arid climate for which those plants are marketed.

The carbon claim for a green roof has a measured sign change and the mechanism is maintenance. An 8,600 square metre extensive roof at Berlin-Brandenburg Airport has carried an eddy-covariance tower continuously since 2015. Annual net exchange ran to a nine-year mean of about minus 92 grams of carbon per square metre, and then in 2022 and 2023 the roof shifted to net release, driven by rising respiration, while substrate organic carbon rose from 2.5 per cent to 7.9 in a single year, attributed by the authors to fertiliser and to plant material not removed rather than to capture from the atmosphere. A grounds team leaving the arisings was doing grounds work; nobody had told them it was a carbon decision and a dead-load decision. Meanwhile the maximum water-holding capacity of a brick-based substrate rose by 9.8 per cent over a year in laboratory microcosms: one study measures the water store per unit volume growing while another measures the volume it sits in shrinking, and the product, the absolute storage on a real roof, has been measured by nobody.

People drift too, and the measured basis for the commonest passive strategy in temperate architecture is six houses in Poland. Six single-family houses near Wrocław were logged for temperature, humidity and carbon dioxide with magnetic reed switches on the windows. The naturally ventilated house that depended entirely on window-opening had its windows open on 2 per cent of logged cycles, at a mean carbon dioxide concentration of 2,384 parts per million, while the mechanically ventilated houses, which did not need the behaviour, opened theirs on 33 to 45 per cent of daytime cycles. Air quality is imperceptible at the concentrations that matter, so the occupant is running a different control loop from the one the designer assumed. Across 750 English households in the hottest summer in the national record, main bedrooms overheated in 18.6 per cent of homes and living rooms in only 8.0, and a cooler room to move to at night existed in 76.4 per cent of affected homes but in only 29.6 per cent of flats. The stock fails in the room where people sleep and often succeeds twenty feet away, and a single-aspect flat has no internal adaptive capacity at all. The tools understate it: a calibrated comparison found the standard energy model alone under-predicting overheating hours by 39 per cent in a Mediterranean case and 35.6 in a temperate one, a bias that is real, directional and still too small to explain the certified dwelling of the previous chapter that ran above 25 degrees for more than half a year. What is left of that gap lies in how the building is run.

The one continuous maintenance regime this chapter can point to belongs to communities rather than to a profession. The knowledge is the knowledge of Aboriginal communities and land owners across northern Australia's savannas, and of Indigenous and local communities across the savannas of East and Southern Africa, and it is written here in the present tense as living practice. The documenting scholarship is a scoping review that screened 730 papers and included eighteen: the fire season is more than a month longer than in the 1980s, colonial exclusion and fire suppression pushed Indigenous burning knowledge to the margins, and prior elicitation of traditional practice is missing from the literature. Continuity of practice, not design intent, sustained the system. Interruption damages the transmission of the knowledge as well as the outcome,, which is the lost Stuttgart species lists at a far more serious scale. Part of that missing elicitation is a research failure and part is knowledge properly held by its custodians and not the literature's to carry, and this research cannot separate the two from outside and does not claim to.

Which brings this chapter to its band on the measurement line. The earlier chapters found the measurement never made, then made and enclosed, then made, given away freely and still lost. This chapter's five teams each found a band of their own. Outside, the band is made, enclosed, and simultaneously made mandatory: wind criteria bind the form of buildings by law while both authoritative papers and the standard sit behind a purchase, which is worse in kind than simple enclosure, because compliance is compulsory. At the threshold, the band is made, reachable, and not supporting the claim it is cited for: green wall cooling survives at thirty centimetres and vanishes at a hundred and fifty because measurement distance was never standardised, and it is the most dangerous band for a practitioner, because it looks like evidence. Inside, the band is made, published openly, and the metric standardised without it: validation, reproducibility and occupant-response studies all exist, are all reachable, and all sit downstream of the thresholds they contradict. Over time the band is made once, and the second measurement never commissioned: four restudies in an entire practice subject, all of vegetation, none of a building, every one a by-product of something else. Nobody funds the second visit. And in the apparatus the band is made universally at the coarsest possible resolution and almost nowhere finer: the utility meter is the most widely deployed instrument in the built environment, one number per building per month with no spatial resolution at all. Cutting across all five, measurement density collapses at the façade and then collapses again at the person: sixteen skin sensors against thousands of trees outdoors, 302 office workers as the largest interior human sample, six houses for the window. And the enclosure is selective, because two papers from one issue, queried in one minute, returned the morals and withheld the measurements.

What carries forward is this. The growth failure of the urban tree is measured and its fix quantified; the survival failure is measured and its fix is not, because the predictors are social. The most repeated rules in practice are heuristics with analytical pedigrees and no field validation, and this chapter grades each as what it is. The threshold is disabled three ways and only one is architectural, from which follows the most useful single rule here: a device that will never be adjusted must be right in its resting position, because the failure mode of a hand-operated component is non-use. The interior's compliance quantities are analyst-dependent, population-dependent and sky-dependent in ways their own documents do not acknowledge. A net balance cannot be verified by a model of ordinary accuracy, a carbon number is a database choice to within about 40 per cent, and a flow visualisation without its validation is an illustration. Living systems on buildings drift on different clocks in different directions, and nobody has measured all of them on one roof long enough to say which governs. Where a maintenance regime belongs to a community, the durability of the instrument is the durability of the relationship. And the chapter's own method is a finding: maintenance has no jargon, so its literature is invisible to subject search, and open access is a licensing status, not a retrieval status. The final chapter looks forward, and it inherits both collapses and all five bands.
