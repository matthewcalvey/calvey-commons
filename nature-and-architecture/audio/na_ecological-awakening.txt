This is the fifth chapter of the Calvey and Claude Nature and Architecture Book, and it covers the three decades from about 1970 to the end of the century. The series asks one question all the way through: how has nature been brought inside, outside and at the threshold of buildings, and what does the record establish for an architect deciding what to do now. The earlier chapters kept finding the same thing, that the ideas were old and the measurements had never been made. This chapter is where that changes. In these thirty years the measurements were made, in enormous quantity, by governments, universities and the profession itself, and the surprising thing is what happened to them afterwards.

The spectrum the series uses runs from nature as an image or a view, through nature as a form borrowed, a model imitated, living or once-living material, climate read and answered by the building, a working system of water and soil and habitat, to nature as a partner with its own agency. It is not a ladder of progress, and this chapter is the clearest proof of that. It is the period in which buildings were most loudly claimed at the far end of the spectrum, as systems and partnerships, and whose most celebrated buildings turn out, on the record, to be operating at the near end, as images.

Here is the honest limit before anything else. The research ran without a search engine, reaching its sources through bibliographic indexes and open repositories, and it found the measured record of the period visible almost everywhere and readable in only a few places. The American federal report series holding the largest body of building measurement from the 1970s and 1980s is indexed to the last title and blocked to the last page; the British programme that studied buildings by name and published their failures is locatable to the page number and unopened by four separate routes; the two documents by which Europe made ecology a legal obligation are invisible to scholarly search altogether. Where this narrative gives a number, it is a number somebody read; where it says a number could not be reached, it stays unreached, and nothing here has been filled in from memory.

Begin in the United States, with the sun, because that is where the money went first. The oil embargo of 1973 did not invent the passive solar house. Harold Hay was publishing on air conditioning by movable insulation in 1969, and the French solar laboratory at Odeillo, where Félix Trombe and Jacques Michel built houses heated by a masonry wall behind glass, was in print by 1973 with work from the late 1960s behind it. What the embargo created was the budget to instrument these things. The American Department of Energy ran its solar experiments in three classes: a controlled test facility with hundreds of sensor channels and almost always nobody living in it; a cheap instrument package on an occupied house; and asking the occupants what they thought. Nearly every apparent contradiction in the passive solar literature dissolves once you notice which class is being quoted.

The best-documented passive solar building of the period is a laboratory shaped like a house, at the National Bureau of Standards in Gaithersburg, Maryland, at latitude thirty-nine degrees north. Four identical cells, each of thirty square metres, stood side by side on open ground. One held the instruments. One had an unvented thermal storage wall of mortar-filled concrete block painted black behind double glazing. One was the control, with an ordinary double-glazed casement window. And one was direct gain, the archetypal image of passive solar: a wide glass sliding door across the south wall, a clerestory above it, concrete on the floor and an added block wall to store the heat. Between them the cells carried four hundred and fifty-seven sensors, logging every ten minutes through the winter of 1984.

Bal Mahajan reported the result. Over three hundred and twelve hours in late January and early February, the storage wall cell used eighty-nine kilowatt hours of auxiliary heat. The control cell, with its plain window, used one hundred and thirty. The direct gain cell used two hundred and twenty-five, nearly double the plain window. Over a longer second period the pattern held: four hundred and fifty-four against two hundred and sixty. The direct gain cell was not failing to collect sun; it was collecting it through an envelope whose measured heat loss coefficient was thirty-eight watts per kelvin against eighteen for the other two cells. The aperture that gathered the gain was the hole that lost the heat, and in that cell, in that month, the hole won. And here is the twist. The design handbook derived at Los Alamos and used by architects across the country predicted the metered energy of every cell to within a few per cent, in the worst case seven. The prediction was right. The building was worse than a window. Predictive accuracy and design merit are independent properties.

The instruments also contradicted the comfort standard. Stanley Liu put three black globe thermometers down the centreline of the direct gain cell, one and a half metres from the glass, then nearly five metres, then seven. On clear days in October and again in January the globe nearest the glass ran between one and seven and a half degrees Celsius above the upper limit of the comfort standard for more than six hours, and at night it fell below the lower limit, because the big cold pane pulled the heat off anything near it. Seven metres back, the same room was fine. So overheating in a direct gain room is not a temperature. It is a place, a zone within about a metre and a half of the glass, and a single room air reading, which is what almost all the cheap monitoring recorded, cannot see it. Worse, the equation the standard used to compute radiant temperature left out sunlight reflected from surfaces and from snow, and on a January day with snow outside it under-read the room by eight degrees, and by three degrees in October: for a decade a designer checking a direct gain room by the book would have calculated it several degrees cooler than the instruments found. The facility ran with no blinds at all, on purpose, to produce clean data: the blinds a real occupant would have shut were the blinds whose absence made the data clean. The chamber studies of the same years found slow drifts of half a degree an hour as acceptable to laboratory subjects as constant conditions, and the passive solar literature leaned on that: but drift in time is not the gradient in space the instruments found.

Then the occupied houses. The Class B programme summarised the winter of 1981 to 1982 as the largest consistently monitored sample of occupied passive solar houses to date, and its abstract, which is all that can be read because the full report sits behind a blocked federal server, gives seven findings. The solar systems carried thirty-nine per cent of total heating load, or fifty-five per cent once internal gains were subtracted, and that difference of denominator is why solar fractions from different papers cannot be compared without care. None of the three system types, direct gain, sunspace or storage wall, performed detectably better than the others at the ninety-five per cent level. Modest solar on a very tight house generally used less heat than ambitious solar on a leaky one. Occupant habits were critical wherever something had to be operated by hand. And the houses were comfortable, at an average of about twenty degrees, measured, almost certainly, as room air, the one quantity that cannot see what Liu measured near the glass. The one survey of purchasers that could be read at second hand found people had bought these houses for location, price and design, not for the solar: they were house buyers who got a solar house.

What the industry did over the next twenty years is, on the reachable evidence, roughly what the numbers said. It adopted the envelope, the insulation and the air sealing, added mechanical ventilation with heat recovery, and dropped the device-level solar architecture: thermal storage walls and roof ponds did not become normal, and sunspaces survived as an amenity. The profession took the conservation finding and left the solar finding, and the movement that generated the evidence disappeared while its evidence was absorbed under the name of energy codes. That reading rests on an abstract and on the titles of the standards that followed, and the reviews that would confirm or overturn it are all closed.

The air sealing has its own story, and it is an instrument story. Before the mid-1970s air leakage was inferred, not measured. Richard Grot's 1979 tracer-gas method found between about one and two air changes an hour by floor in occupied low-income houses, against a third of an air change in a Maryland test house. Then came the fan in the doorway. Its lineage, from the reference list of a 1984 calibration report, is Swedish: a pressure method from Sweden in 1978, Princeton's hunt for hidden heat losses in 1979, a Princeton report titled The Blower Door in 1981, an American standard practice the same year, and Sweden already requiring new homes to meet a tightness level by pressurisation before the United States had a standard at all.

Sweden regulated before America standardised. And the instrument was biased. Andrew Persily, a postdoctoral fellow at the Bureau of Standards, calibrated two commercial blower doors against tracer gas and found that the straight-line calibration the manufacturers supplied under-read the airflow, by about a quarter at the low pressures used to estimate natural leakage and by a tenth at the standard test pressure. The report says plainly that such errors would cause tight or small houses to be reported as more airtight than they were: the instrument that made airtightness real carried an error running in exactly the direction the field wanted. Read that beside the health review the same Bureau published two years earlier. In six of its own tight thermal-mass test buildings, radon ran at eight to eighteen picocuries per litre in spring, roughly doubling in autumn, and the highest reading was in the tightest building, at nine hundredths of an air change an hour, against a recommended maximum of three. Houses in Maine with solar storage beds on granite ran up to two hundred. A survey of three hundred and thirty-four mobile homes found formaldehyde from three hundredths to nearly two parts per million, and emission doubled as humidity rose from thirty to sixty per cent, which is exactly what a warm planted sunspace does. The mass that made the passive system work was itself a source. By 1986 the Bureau was estimating a million affected dwellings and twenty thousand deaths a year, and admitting that no inexpensive field monitor for widespread use yet existed. The instrument that made the problem visible arrived a decade before the instrument that could measure its consequence.

Now the famous buildings. Harold Hay's Skytherm house at Atascadero, with its roof of water bags and sliding insulation, has a published thermal evaluation from 1976, and it is closed, with even its abstract withheld, so this chapter gives no solar fraction, monitoring period or interior temperature for the most elegant device of the decade. Whether the Odeillo houses were ever instrumented is unverified in the English-language indexed literature, and Steve Baer's drumwall has no instrumented study anywhere that could be reached. Village Homes at Davis, the most celebrated ecological neighbourhood in the United States, has no instrumented microclimate study indexed at all, while the federal programme was logging millions of measurements in houses nobody has heard of, in reports behind the same blocked server. The measurement and the fame attached to different buildings. And nobody, in the whole federal record, put four hundred and fifty-seven sensors in a house with a family in it, which is why this chapter's comfort evidence and its energy evidence cannot be joined.

Leave the house for the land, because the same decades made ecology a way of planning. Ian McHarg published Design with Nature in 1969, and what travelled was not the argument but the procedure: a stack of transparent maps, geology, soils, water, slope, vegetation, habitat, each shaded darker where the land was less suitable, laid over one another so the composite showed where to build. The slope is measured; the decision that a slope class should be two shades darker is not, so the composite has the look of data and the standing of a weighted opinion. McHarg did not invent the overlay and never said he had; the New Deal agencies built the layers, and the Harvard laboratory for computer graphics turned the transparencies into a grid of cells with numbers in them, which is why a 1969 method is now a default setting in every planning office. Fifty years on, no study could be found testing whether a composite suitability map produces better outcomes than the methods it displaced.

The one McHarg project with a published outcome is The Woodlands, north of Houston, planned from 1972 on the flat, wet Gulf coastal plain. Density and streets followed soil infiltration, water was shed to swales and ponds rather than pipes, streets were built without kerbs and the canopy was kept. The received account is that it came through the great Houston floods of 1979 and 1994 almost unscathed. The peer-reviewed record contains no flood comparison at all. What it contains is a study by Bo Yang and colleagues correlating daily rainfall with daily streamflow across three watersheds for 2006 to 2010. In storms above thirty-five millimetres The Woodlands watershed responded at a correlation of 0.716 against 0.814 and 0.766 for two conventionally developed neighbours, while carrying nearly thirty-two per cent impervious cover against their sixteen and twelve — a stronger result than the folklore, because the ecological case carries more hard surface and still responds less. Its land surface was also one to two degrees cooler on two satellite dates. And then the same paper reports the finding nobody quotes. Residents of the ecologically designed villages rated them less safe than the later, conventionally landscaped villages, in parks, by day and by night, in every survey wave from 1999 to 2010, by a tenth to a third of a point on a five-point scale. The mechanism is plain: dense canopy over an unmown understorey removes sightlines, and residents read the loss of sightlines as concealment. The hydrological virtue and the inhabitational cost are the same physical fact, and nobody has averaged them, and neither does this book.

Landscape ecology gave planners a vocabulary of patches, corridors and matrix, and the corridor became design orthodoxy before anyone had tested it. In 1987 Daniel Simberloff and James Cox pointed out that there were almost no controlled data. In 1998 Paul Beier and Reed Noss reviewed thirty-two studies claiming to show corridors work, judged fewer than half persuasive, and argued for corridors anyway: they agreed about the evidence and disagreed about what to do without it, which is a designer's problem, not a scientist's. Then somebody built the experiment. On a former weapons reservation in South Carolina, a team cut replicated landscapes of open patches, some joined by a corridor and some not, with the unconnected controls shaped to carry the same edge and area. Corridors moved animals, pollen and seed, and connected patches held more native plant species with the difference widening over time, in a result whose first author is Ellen Damschen. And in the same landscapes corridors raised the abundance of an invasive fire ant and lowered the diversity of native ants. A meta-analysis of seventy-eight experiments gives corridors about fifty per cent more movement than unconnected patches, weakest for birds, and finds natural corridors working better than constructed ones. A corridor is a conduit, and conduits are neutral about their cargo. The reserve-design diagram on every studio wall, one large patch better than several small, turns out to be the position with less evidence behind it: a review of forty years of studies finds several small patches holding more species.

The cleanest policy failure in any design field belongs here. Under the American Clean Water Act a permit to fill a wetland could be conditioned on making a wetland somewhere else, at a ratio, typically one and a half or two acres for one, checked against a vegetation standard over five years. From 1989 the stated goal was no net loss. In 2001 the National Research Council audited two decades of it. The national ledger looked healthy: about forty-two thousand acres a year required against twenty-four thousand filled, a nominal net gain. The field studies underneath said otherwise. Across some twenty studies in six states, a median of seventy per cent of permits had mitigation attempted at all, a median of fifty-three per cent complied with their own conditions, the median area outcome was a third short, and the median functional equivalency with the wetland lost was eighteen per cent. In one Ohio study ten projects showed one hundred per cent compliance and zero per cent functional equivalency at once. The most cited cause of failure was insufficient hydrology, and in California hydrology was monitored at twenty-two per cent of sites while vegetation was monitored at seventy-two. Of eight real permits reproduced in the report, all eight set vegetation criteria, one set a hydrologic criterion, and none set a biogeochemical one. The regulation measured the leaves and the failure was in the water; the one drafting lesson is that in Virginia permits with specific conditions were complied with eighty-six per cent of the time against forty-four without. Eleven years later a meta-analysis of six hundred and twenty-one restored wetlands found them still twenty-six per cent short on biological structure and twenty-three per cent short on function a century after restoration. The monitoring period was shorter than the phenomenon by a factor of between four and twenty.

The small device that came out of this tradition and conquered the world is bioretention, the vegetated soil basin in a car park that puts rain back into the ground, developed in a Maryland county in the early 1990s, though its peer-reviewed record begins only around 2001. It removes metals almost completely and phosphorus at seventy to eighty-five per cent, and nitrate at under twenty; later studies found every soil mixture tested exporting nutrients. Concentration is not load, and the dissolved fraction, the part that feeds algae downstream, is the part these devices handle least reliably. Three things here are findings, not complaints. Its unit of analysis is the parcel, and a parcel has no inside, so the interior axis of this book is empty here by construction. Its maintenance labour, on which every measured performance depends, is almost never reported. And Indigenous and community land-management knowledge, of fire in prairie, of wetlands, of what a reference community even is, is absent from its entire evidential record, which is produced by federal agencies, universities and consultancies; the research reached no community-authored source, and that absence is a defect in the field.

Cross to Europe, where the same decades turned ecology into a product and a number. The product is the extensive green roof, made in West Germany between the early 1970s and the mid-1990s: a specified stack of membrane, root barrier, drainage layer, filter fleece and about ten centimetres of manufactured mineral substrate under a mat of sedum, and roughly eighty-five per cent of German green roofs are that. The guideline that governs it, from the German landscape research society known by its initials, is a trade document sold commercially and cited in contracts rather than papers; it could not be opened, its date could not be verified, and it is invisible to the scholarly record. The number is Berlin's biotope area factor, developed in the late 1980s for West Berlin and made binding through landscape plans in 1994: every surface on a site is weighted between zero and one and the area-weighted mean must meet a minimum. A green roof scores 0.7, whether it is ten centimetres of sedum or forty centimetres of garden. That is not a measurement. It is an administrative judgement that a square metre of roof is worth seven tenths of a square metre of ground, and it worked precisely because it did not wait for the evidence. Europe regulated first and measured afterwards, and when the measurement arrived it arrived in English, from North America, about a different climate.

The one long German record redirects the whole thermal argument. Manfred Köhler and Daniel Kaiser monitored a sedum roof and an adjacent gravel roof at Neubrandenburg, north of Berlin, continuously from 1999 to 2018 with meteorological-grade instruments logging every ten seconds. Over twenty years the surface of the green roof warmed by seven tenths of a kelvin. So did the surface of the gravel roof. Identical. What diverged was the inside of the substrate: the gravel roof's interior warmed by one and four tenths of a kelvin and the green roof's by nothing at all, a tenth cooler if anything. A green roof did not insulate the city from the surface. It insulated the building from the roof. The celebrated claim of a cool green surface radiating less heat to the city is a claim about a summer afternoon, and this record does not test that timescale. The winter heating saving, from one year's measurement, was four per cent. And the authors record that their lysimeters and soil moisture sensors never worked properly on dry coarse media: the water measurement failed where the temperature succeeded, on the same roof, which is part of why the field is nearly half thermal studies and less than a fifth drainage.

The drainage story comes from Illinois. Twenty-four small roof platforms at ten, fifteen and twenty centimetres, planted and unplanted, had every storm's runoff weighed for three years. At fifteen centimetres a roof held ninety-four per cent of a small rain and forty-six per cent of a rain above about an inch, still a small storm by drainage standards; the published literature does not reach the storms a drainage engineer designs for. Two results in the same data invert the design intuition. Fifteen centimetres held more than twenty in the big storms, because the deeper roof was still wet from the last one. And at ten centimetres the bare roof held more than the planted one, because bare media dried faster while foliage shaded the substrate. The variable that governs a green roof is not how much water it holds but how fast it gives it back, and sedum, chosen because it survives an unwatered roof, is the plant least able to empty one. And three school roofs in Haifa, built with the standard biodiverse-roof recipe of varied substrate patches, produced two hundred and fifty-nine arthropod species over three years, with the substrate heterogeneity making no significant difference to anything and the roof's position in the city making all of it: forty per cent of species were found on only one of the three roofs, a kilometre or two apart. The green roof is three stacked systems with conflicting demands, habitat above, hydrology in the middle, envelope below, each with its own journal and its own funders, and nobody owns the trade-off; Berlin's single 0.7 institutionalises exactly that flattening.

The Dutch had been making designed wild landscapes since before the war, in the native-planted heemparks of Amstelveen and in Louis Le Roy's process gardens; no date could be verified for any of them and no measurement of any heempark exists. The great Dutch experiment is the Oostvaardersplassen, a polder reclaimed in 1968 into which Heck cattle, Konik horses and red deer were introduced in the 1980s and early 1990s and then left, to test Frans Vera's hypothesis that the pre-agricultural European lowland was a half-open wood-pasture held open by grazing. Three decades of vegetation records say two things at once. Grazing sustained nearly sixteen plant species per plot against fewer than three in the ungrazed control, a factor of five and a half. And the prediction failed: no trees in any grazed plot, plots growing more alike over time, and twenty indicator species in the first decade of records reduced to none by 2022, though the authors are careful to say the flat, seed-bankless polder is a poor test of the general idea. The herbivores rose to a plateau of two and a half to three and a half thousand and were regulated by winter starvation, in a fenced reserve without predators or refuge, until a policy change in 2018 brought them down to about thirteen hundred by culling. Forage data published from the 1980s had shown the winter marginality from the start.

The British contribution is an observation: that the richest urban habitat is land nobody designed. Oliver Gilbert's book of 1989 treated railway banks, cemeteries and demolished industrial ground as habitats on the same footing as woodland, and his word for that last category was urban commons, not brownfield, a planning designation meaning available for development. The famous measured claim, that such ground can hold more beetle diversity than managed green space, has its citation in a 2003 paper that could not be opened, so this chapter gives no species count for its own most interesting British sentence. Hundertwasser's Vienna apartment block and its tree tenants, the most photographed vegetated buildings of the century, have no measured environmental record at all, and Emilio Ambasz's terraced green hall in Fukuoka returned nothing whatever. The earth-sheltered movement, by contrast, was measured on occupied buildings with federal money within a decade of its emergence, in reports that sit in the same blocked series and that architecture never read. And the Nordic turf roof, the living tradition from which the engineered roof subtracted the weight, returned nothing in the scholarly record, not a thermal measurement, not a history, which cannot be explained by remoteness; the research could name the Norwegian, Icelandic and Faroese communities whose knowledge it is and could not find the scholars who have written it down, and that is a failure of sourcing to be corrected, not evidence that the writing does not exist.

Now the ideas, which gave the profession the words it still uses. They are four different kinds of claim, and most of the trouble since comes from citing them in one voice. Arne Næss's deep ecology of 1973 is a claim about value, that non-human life has worth independent of its use; it cannot be measured and is not weakened by that. It gave architecture a licence, that a site's other inhabitants have standing, and a category error, citing a moral commitment as if a study had shown it. Bioregionalism, first stated by Peter Berg alone in 1976 before the version carrying Raymond Dasmann's name, is a politics of watersheds with no empirical content to test. Gaia is a claim about how the planet works. Its weak form, that life shapes the chemistry of the air and sea, was never in dispute. Its strong form, that life regulates the planet like a control system, drew an objection from evolutionary biologists that a planet is not a unit of selection and cannot have been selected for self-regulation, and an objection from James Kirchner that the testable versions were trivial and the interesting ones unfalsifiable. Lovelock's answer was Daisyworld, a model of an imaginary planet with two kinds of daisy, which its own title calls a parable, and which shows that the objection is not a proof of impossibility and nothing about the Earth. Four positions stand unresolved; only the weak form entered Earth system science.

Permaculture is a claim about how to design, and it took forty-six years to test. Its literature, examined by Rafter Ferguson and Sarah Lovell across two hundred and thirty publications, was fourteen per cent peer-reviewed, and several of its central claims misapply ecology. A second review, indexed everywhere as systematic and on reading nothing of the kind, found published support for all twelve principles, but for the constituent practices one at a time; for zones and sectors, the spatial apparatus an architect would use, it found no evidence at all, and stated plainly that nobody had monitored farms that follow the principles against farms that do not. Then in 2024 somebody did. Nine permaculture farms in Germany and Luxembourg, each paired with the conventional field next door, held twenty-seven per cent more soil carbon, two hundred per cent more earthworms and four and a half times as many plant species, and the authors say at once that these are also small, old, organic, tree-covered farms and that the confound cannot be separated. The fields did far better than the literature gave reason to expect, and the design system as such is still untested.

Two buildings tried to be ecosystems. The New Alchemy Institute's Arks of 1976, on Cape Cod and Prince Edward Island, were glazed bioshelters in which the fish tanks were the building's heat store, the fish's habitat and the plants' fertiliser at once, one of very few cases in this book genuinely at system and partner inside the envelope. Their performance was written up at the time, in eleven pages in a 1979 solar engineering volume by people who were there, and that chapter could not be opened; the Institute's own journal is in no index; every temperature, yield and solar fraction for the Arks stays unfilled, as does every removal figure for the living machine that grew out of them. Biosphere 2, in Arizona, is usually told as a farce. It should be told as the chapter's only honest experiment. Eight people were sealed for two years from 1991 in a glass building of three acres holding some thirty-eight hundred species in seven biomes. Oxygen fell from twenty-one per cent to fourteen in the first sixteen months, enough to make the occupants ill. The explanation the study is credited with, which could not be verified from the closed source, is that the imported soils respired far faster than designed and the carbon dioxide they produced was absorbed by the building's own uncarbonated concrete, so the budgets balanced only when the structure was counted as a chemical participant in its own atmosphere. That finding appeared in the geophysical union's member magazine rather than a primary journal. Every other project here kept its reputation by not being measured. Biosphere 2 ran the loggers, failed in public, and produced the transferable science.

And so to biophilia, and to the one study on which the modern field rests. Edward Wilson's book of 1984 proposed an evolved human tendency to affiliate with life, and he called it a hypothesis, which is what it still is. The affective evidence, that people feel better after exposure to nature, is now supported by a meta-analysis of forty-nine studies and over three thousand participants; the evolutionary explanation is disputed on three technical grounds and contradicted by a study of children who showed no preference for wild or biodiverse ground even when they had it. The feeling is real and measured; the ancestry is not established, and the word carries the ancestry smuggled inside it. The study everybody cites is Roger Ulrich's paper in Science in 1984. Ulrich, then in a geography department, examined the records of gallbladder patients in one suburban Pennsylvania hospital, now identified as Paoli, for the years 1972 to 1981, and matched twenty-three patients whose windows gave onto a natural scene with twenty-three whose windows faced a brick wall. The first group stayed less long, drew fewer negative remarks in the nurses' notes, and took fewer strong painkillers. That is forty-six people, one hospital, one operation. The abstract gives the direction of the three effects and none of their sizes, so the familiar numbers stay unfilled in this chapter, as does the detail that the natural scene was trees, and the variables the patients were matched on. The paper has been cited more than five thousand times, and two independent searches found no study that ever reproduced its design. In 2026 a re-examination argued that the two views differed in depth and in visible movement as well as in naturalness, and that is more damaging than any complaint about sample size, because a confounded study cannot say which variable did the work: the alternative instruction, make the view deep and put something in it that moves, can be met with or without a tree. The same year a cohort of four hundred and forty-eight children recovering from spinal surgery compared beds by the window with beds by the door and found no difference on any outcome, which is not a replication, because it varied nearness rather than content. Ulrich's own study of 2019 found that intensive-care families' stress fell significantly during breaks whether they took them in the hospital garden or indoors, and that the garden won on sadness alone. The break did most of the work.

Here is the chapter's structural figure, and chapter six will build on it. Ulrich's evidence is about a framed, static, distant scene seen from a bed through sealed glass. On this book's spectrum that is nature as image: the patients did not touch, smell, hear or inhabit the trees. The field has used it to justify planting, water, natural materials, ventilation strategies and living walls — nature as material, as climate, as system, and in the rhetoric as partner — none of which Ulrich tested. And the two theories the field cites in one breath are not one theory. Ulrich's stress reduction is fast, affective and autonomic, and favours a view visible from wherever the stress is; Rachel and Stephen Kaplan's attention restoration, in a book their reviewers date to 1990, is slow and cognitive, and favours a place to go and stay. A brief that cites both and specifies only a window has silently chosen Ulrich; one that specifies only a garden has chosen the Kaplans. Most briefs cite both and notice neither choice.

The last movement of the chapter is the machinery the profession built to check itself, and it gives the period its two cleanest results and its emptiest file. The rating systems began with BREEAM in Britain in 1990 and spread to Canada, Hong Kong and, as LEED, to the United States. What BREEAM measured, how it was weighted and whether it rated design intent or performance in use could not be established from any opened source; the founding documents of the most-copied scheme are unreachable while the commentary about it is abundant. A rating is a statement about a document, and a credit for predicted energy saving is a claim that a model was run: the plaque on the wall sits on the spectrum at image.

In Britain a programme called PROBE studied buildings by name in the second half of the 1990s and published their failures. Its synthesis is a five-part series in one issue of Building Research and Information in 2001, followed by nine commentaries and a reply from the team titled as walking a tightrope; every one of those papers is located to the page number and none could be opened by four separate routes, because the publisher serves neither full text nor abstracts to automated retrieval. This chapter therefore reports no PROBE finding: not the number of buildings, not the energy results, not the lessons. The paper everybody quotes on the killer variables of productivity has over four hundred citations and an abstract the publisher withholds; it circulates as a phrase. The period's most celebrated naturally ventilated building, the Queen's Building at De Montfort University in Leicester, has three publications by its architects and its client and no reachable instrumented study, and the claim that circulates about its energy use is not repeated here. The same practice's later building in London reached its intended behaviour, on its designers' own account, only because the design team stayed on after handover and re-tuned the controls, work the standard appointment does not pay for.

The first clean result is about comfort. Fanger's comfort model came from climate chambers. Richard de Dear and Gail Brager assembled about twenty-one thousand field observations from about a hundred and sixty office buildings on four continents, from Bangkok and Singapore to Athens, Michigan, Wales, six Australian cities and five Pakistani ones, each one a questionnaire answer with clothing, activity, indoor conditions and outdoor weather attached. In air-conditioned buildings the chamber model worked, the field regression explaining fifty-three per cent of the variance; in naturally ventilated buildings it under-predicted what people found comfortable, and the field regression explained seventy. The decisive move was a test: could the disagreement be absorbed by adjusting the model's own inputs within plausible bounds. It could not; reconciling the two would have required assuming people's metabolic rates were a fifth lower than any plausible value. A disagreement that survives that test is a falsification, not a calibration problem, and a model built indoors under control was overturned by observation of the inhabited world, in the hot climates where most people live. The result went into the American comfort standard as an optional adaptive method, applicable only where occupants control easily reached, operable windows, with no mechanical cooling of any kind, in bands five and seven degrees wide, between ten and thirty-three degrees outdoors. The allowance belongs to occupant control, not to natural ventilation as a servicing strategy, and the thirty-three degree ceiling is the provision most likely to bind as the climate warms. Fanger himself, with Jørn Toftum, published a counter-position fifteen pages earlier in the very same issue, proposing an extension of his model rather than its replacement; that paper is closed, and this chapter knows it by its title alone and says so.

The second clean result is about the ratings themselves, and it arrived in 2009, two decades after BREEAM, which means the schemes ran through the whole of this chapter's period without anyone having checked them against measurement at scale. Guy Newsham and colleagues at the National Research Council of Canada took a year of measured energy from a hundred LEED-certified American buildings, drawn from a hundred and twenty-one that had volunteered data out of five hundred and fifty-two eligible, and matched them to conventional stock. On average the certified buildings used eighteen to thirty-nine per cent less energy than their matches. Between a quarter and a third of them used more. At design stage the rating worked perfectly: the energy saving a building promised rose reliably and significantly with its certification level. In measurement the level carried no information at all. Silver did not beat Certified; Gold and Platinum did not beat Silver; the number of energy credits explained eleven per cent of the variance in measured energy; and the credits for commissioning and for measurement and verification, the ones written to guarantee performance, had no measurable effect. The average ratio of measured to designed energy was 0.92, almost exactly on target, while individual buildings ran from under a quarter of their prediction to more than two and three quarter times it. The average was right and almost every building was wrong: a model unbiased across a population can be useless for the building on your desk. John Scofield published a rejoinder four months later in the same journal under a near-identical title ending not really, and that paper is closed, so this chapter has read one side of the exchange. A later study of federal buildings with a stronger causal design found no effect of certification at all, and a review of forty-four papers found ten saying the ratings save energy and eight saying they do not.

The gap between predicted and measured energy was documented in 1994, in a Princeton paper whose title states a two-to-one discrepancy in a low-energy office, and named as the performance gap only around 2012. Two decades separate the finding from the word, and without the word the finding did not travel. Where the modelling profession locates the discrepancy is, consistently, occupant behaviour, which is also the part of the building it does not control. And what became of post-occupancy evaluation can be read off the publication record like a heartbeat. In 2001, the year the definitive demonstration that it works was published, the same journal ran commentaries asking where post-occupancy evaluation was, who it was for, and whether the industry suffered from collective amnesia. In 2003 one of the PROBE authors asked whether the profession was learning more from its buildings or just forgetting less. In 2005 he and his colleague wrote a three-part series on making feedback routine, which is not something you write about a routine practice, and from which the Soft Landings framework came. In 2010 they restated the principles. Twenty years, the same two people, the same case.

So where does this chapter fall on the book's line of measurement. Chapters one to four found that the number was never taken. This chapter's band is the next one: made and enclosed. The measurements exist, in bulk. Four hundred and fifty-seven sensors in four cells, twenty-one thousand comfort observations, six hundred and twenty-one restored wetlands, twenty years of ten-second logging on one roof. They sit behind a blocked federal server, behind commercial paywalls that withhold even abstracts, inside a purchased trade standard, inside a municipal file, in unindexed trade magazines and self-published institute journals and a learned society's members' newsletter. The best reads the research could make came, without exception, from institutions with a public-access mandate. The enclosure is selective, and what it encloses is the measurements. The profession's most candid self-examination is its least obtainable.

What carries forward is a short list. Envelope before device, because the largest sample of occupied houses found tightness deciding more than the choice of solar system, which was not discriminable at all. Overheating as a place, a metre and a half from the glass, that a room-average temperature cannot see. An instrument whose calibration ran in the direction of the field's hope, and a tightest building that held the most radon. A rule that complied on paper at fifty-three per cent and worked at eighteen while the ledger showed a gain. A roof that is not a cool surface but a buffer delivered downward, governed by how fast it dries, and that is three systems nobody owns together. A number Berlin invented rather than measured, because that is what enforceability costs. A field model that falsified a chamber model, and an allowance that belongs to the person who can open the window. A rating whose levels predicted the promise and not the performance. A building whose fabric breathed. And a founding study of forty-six patients, unreplicated, about a picture, asked to carry every other mode of nature in architecture. Chapter six takes up that last one, in the present day, where the measurements were made, given away freely, and lost anyway.
